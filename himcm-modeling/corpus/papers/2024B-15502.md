Summary
Against the backdrop of energy shortages and growing environmental concerns, the energy
consumption and carbon emissions of HPC have become issues that attract widespread attention.
In response, we conducted a study on the energy consumption and carbon footprint of HPC.
To assess the energy consumption of HPC, we designed an HPC energy consumption model
that indirectly estimates energy use based on societal computing demands, overcoming the chal-
lenge of obtaining HPC energy consumption data. Our model first allocates the demand to two
categories of systems: Massive Parallel Processing and cluster systems. Then, it proportionally
distributes the computing tasks executed by these systems to individual high-performance comput-
ers and determines the global annual energy consumption of HPC based on their energy efficiency
metrics. Through our computational analyses, we discern that the annual energy consumption of
HPC under full load is approximately bounded within the range of [1.97 × 10 8 , 2.87 × 10 8 ] MWh,
with the average annual consumption falling between [0.72 × 10 8 , 1.43 × 10 8 ] MWh.
To calculate the carbon emissions of HPC, we first developed an HPC carbon emissions cal-
culation model that incorporates the energy mix, drawing on traditional methods for calculating
carbon emissions from energy sources. Secondly, we utilized grey prediction models, resistance
differential equations, and ARIMA models to forecast future trends in HPC energy consumption,
HPC utilization rates, and seven major energy sources. By combining these forecasting models
with the carbon emissions calculation model, we can determine the annual carbon emissions of
HPC for the coming years. Our computational findings indicate that the projected carbon emis-
sions for the year 2030 are poised to lie within the interval of [8.18 × 10 8 , 10.54 × 10 8 ] tons.
Expanding upon these models, we constructed an HPC carbon emission calculation model
with the proportion of clean energy and nuclear energy as variables. By iterating through the
increase of clean energy and nuclear energy proportions from 0 to 100%, we found that increasing
the proportion of clean energy can effectively reduce carbon emissions. For instance, when the
nuclear energy proportion is 20% and the new energy proportion is 60%, carbon emissions can be
reduced by 77%.
Additionally, to study the impact of the increasing energy consumption of HPC on community
energy rights, we took a distribution network that includes an HPC center as an example and used
KCL and KVL to describe the relationship between the upper limit of community energy access
and HPC energy consumption. By combining this with the aforementioned model’s predictions for
HPC energy consumption, we determined the upper limits of community energy access from 2024
to 2030. The results indicate that by 2028, the upper limit of community energy access is expected
to drop below 80% of the 2024 level.
Finally, based on our research findings, we propose two recommendations: 1) Offer electricity
price discounts to attract HPC companies to locate their servers in the ocean; 2) Provide power
generation subsidies to encourage new energy enterprises to develop ocean-based renewable en-
ergy sources. By leveraging the abundant offshore new energy, we can offset the significant energy
consumption of high-performance computing, which is beneficial for energy saving and carbon
emission reduction.
Keywords: HPC; Energy Consumption; Carbon Emissions; Carbon Emissions Model; Forecasting
Model; Island New Energy

1 Introduction
1.1  Background
High Performance Computing (HPC) has become ubiquitous in today’s society, with its formidable
computing power driving revolutionary progress in multiple fields. For instance, in the fieldof ar-
tificial intelligence, HPC has made it possible to train large language and painting models; in the
field of cloud computing, HPC has spurred the rapid development of big data analysis and online
services[1 ]. Despite the advancements in society driven by HPC, it has also brought about numer-
ous environmental issues, such as excessive energy consumption, generation of a large amount of
electronic waste, constant noise production, and overexploitation of rare earth resources.

Amidst energy shortages and environmental degradation, the performance of HPC in terms of
energy consumption and carbon emissions has garnered unprecedented attention. HPC facilities,
especially large-scale data centers and supercomputers, are becoming new giants in energy con-
sumption. To meet the growing computational demands, they not only exacerbate the pressure on
energy supply but also have a significant impact on the environment. To ensure that HPC technol-
ogy can serve the needs of future society in a more sustainable manner, it is essential to delve into
its impact on energy consumption and carbon emissions and to develop innovative solutions.

3.3.3  Energy Forecast Model

We employ the ARIMA model to forecast the electricity generation of various energy sources
in the coming years. This is because 1) electricity generation often exhibits significant seasonality
and trends, such as wind and solar power being influenced by seasonal and weather conditions; 2)
electricity generation data are often non-stationary, meaning their statistical properties (like mean
and variance) change over time. The ARIMA model combines three methods: Autoregression
(AR), Integration (I), and Moving Average (MA), making it particularly suitable for processing and
forecasting non-stationary time series data. Below is a detailed explanation of the mathematical
principles of the ARIMA model [14]:

• Autoregression (AR): The autoregressive part focuses on the linear relationship between
the current value and past values in a time series. If the current value Yt of a time series can
be expressed as a linear combination of its past p values plus a random error term ϵt , then
the time series has autoregressive properties.

Yt = ϕ 1 Yt−1 + ϕ2 Yt−2 + . . . + ϕpYt−p + ϵt  (16)
where ϕ1 , ϕ2 , . . . , ϕp are the autoregressive coefficients.

• Integration (I): The integration part is the process of transforming a non-stationary time
series into a stationary one. The number of differences d indicateshow many times differ-

1. Unstable Power Supply: Meteorological factors such as wind speed and solar radiation

supply. For example, when there is no wind or illumination at night, new energy equipments
cannot generate power, which is fatal for HPC and may lead to server shutdowns.

2. Voltage Instability: Traditional thermal power units are considered voltage sources and
can provide stable support for grid voltage, while new energy often adopts grid-following

and its lifespan is short. When the proportion of new energy is high, a large amount of energy
storage is required, which not only creates significant costs but also faces maintenance and
recycling issues for the storage devices in the next decade.

6  Recommendations of Policy and Technology
6.1  Recommendations to Reduce the Environmental Impact of HPC
We have noticed that some top technology companies are already using island new energy to
power their undersea HPC centers, and at the same time using seawater as a cooling medium, set-
ting an excellent example for improving PUE and reducing the carbon emission intensity of HPC
[17, 18]. Policies and technological developments can be leveraged to guide the layout of HPC to-
wards islands/deep-sea development, and thus we propose the following three recommendations.

1. Attracting Enterprise Relocation Policies: For enterprises willing to relocate their HPC
centers to islands or deep-sea areas where new energy sources are more abundant, offer a
discount subsidy on electricity prices based on electricity consumption.

2. Industrial Chain Agglomeration Policies: For enterprises willing to build new energy
power generation facilities for the HPC industry in areas with abundant new energy sources
such as islands or deep-sea areas, purchase electricity at a higher price to help enterprises
quickly recoup investments.

3. Developing Seawater Cooling Technology: Provide HPC centers with better and more
cost-effective cooling systems, enhancing the energy efficiency of HPC centers.

The first two policy recommendations aim to use electricity price subsidies to attract the HPC
industry to relocate to island/deep-sea areas rich in new energy resources, making the energy mix
of the HPC industry more environmentally friendly. At the same time, they encourage new energy
companies to accelerate the construction of local new energy networks, promote energy restruc-
turing, and reduce carbon emissions from electricity use. The third suggestion, tailored to the
unique environment of islands/deep-sea areas, involves the development of distinctive seawater

7  Strengths and Weaknesses
Strengths

1. Innovative Estimation Method. Our model indirectly estimates the energy consumption
of HPC by the required computing power of society, innovatively linking computational
demand with energy consumption, solving the problem of difficult access to direct energy
consumption data of HPC.

2. Comprehensiveness. Our model takes into account a variety of energy types and calculates

## Page 23

Team # 15 502  Page 23 of 25

carbon emissions based on the characteristics of each type of energy, enabling the model to
more accurately assess the impact of different energy mixes on HPC carbon emissions.

3. Adaptability. Our model has predictive capabilities, able to forecast future changes in HPC
energy consumption and energy mix, which helps to more precisely calculate the carbon
footprint of HPC.

Weaknesses

1. Dependence on Assumptions. Our model makes many assumptions of uniform distribution,
such as the number of each type of computer in the TOP500 list being consistent, and the
computing power undertaken by each type of computer being evenly distributed according
to rated computing power. These assumptions deviate from reality.

2. Data Limitations. Our model is limited by the quality and scope of available data. For
instance, specific energy consumption data for HPC and lifecycle carbon emission data for
new energy devices, if incomplete or outdated, can affect the model’s predictive capabilities
and the reliability of the results.

3. Lack of Technical Details. When assessing the impact of HPC energy consumption on
community energy rights, we simplified the AC power grid into a DC grid because we only
learned about DC models during high school, which certainly deviates from the actual AC
power grid.

8  Conclusion
This study presents a comprehensive assessment model for the energy consumption and carbon
emissions of High Performance Computing (HPC), aiming to provide a quantitative analysis of the
environmental impact of HPC and to explore the potential effects of increasing the share of new
energy on reducing carbon emissions. Here are the main conclusions from our research:

1. HPC Energy Consumption Assessment: We found that the annual global energy consump-
tion of HPC is closely related to the Average Utilization Rate (AUR), Power Usage Effective-
ness (PUE), and the total scale of societal computing demands. By analyzing scenarios with
different PUE values and scales of computing demands, we concluded that the annual en-
ergy consumption of HPC under full load ranges roughly between [1.97 × 10 8 , 2.87 × 10 8 ]
MWh, while the average annual consumption falls between [0.69 × 10 8 , 1.44 × 10 8 ] MWh.

2. HPC Carbon Emissions Model: Considering the energy mix, we predict that by 2030, the
carbon emissions of HPC will exceed the current level by more than ten times, ranging
from 8.18 × 10 8 to 10.54 × 10 8 tons of carbon dioxide equivalent. This forecast highlights
the potential environmental impact of HPC, especially in the context of a global electricity
structure that has not yet fully transitioned to green energy.

3. Impact of New Energy Proportion on Carbon Emissions: Our model indicates that as the
proportion of new energy and nuclear power generation increases, the carbon emissions of

## Page 24

Team # 15 502  Page 24 of 25

HPC significantly decrease. When the power generation structure fully shifts to new energy
and nuclear power, carbon emissions will drop to 4 % - 5 % of the current level, essentially
achieving green computing for HPC.

4. Impact of HPC on Community Energy Rights: We constructed an electrical power system
model to analyze the impact of HPC facilities on the upper limit of energy access for local
communities. The results show that without upgrading the power supply capacity of the re-
gional distribution network, as the load of HPC centers increases, the electricity available to
community residents exhibits an accelerating downward trend, dropping below 40% of 2024
levels by 2030, severely affecting the production and life of residents and local businesses.

5. Policy and Technology Recommendations: Our three recommendations, Attracting Enter-
prise Relocation Policies, Industrial Chain Agglomeration Policies, and the development of
Seawater Cooling Technology aim to use electricity price subsidies to attract the HPC in-
dustry to relocate to island/deep-sea areas rich in new energy resources, while encouraging
new energy companies to accelerate the construction of local new energy networks, promote
energy restructuring, and reduce carbon emissions from electricity use.

In conclusion, based on our research findings, we have written a letter to the United Nations
Consultative Committee urging them to improve the section on the environmental impact of high-
performance computing in their development goals for 2030.

Dear Members of the United Nations Advisory Board,

We are writing to you in light of the recent report titled Governing AI for Humanity, which
was issued in September 2024. We commend the Advisory Board for its comprehensive approach
to AI governance; however, we note with concern that the report does not significantly address the
environmental impacts of High Performance Computing (HPC), a critical component of modern
AI systems.

Our research reveals that HPC, while indispensable in advancing fields like AI, climate science,
and data analytics, also poses substantial environmental challenges. For instance, the annual global
energy consumption of HPC, when operating at full load, ranges between 1.97×10 8 and 2.87×10 8
MWh. This energy demand not only strains global power resources but also contributes heavily to
carbon emissions, with projections suggesting that by 2030, HPC carbon emissions could exceed
current levels by over tenfold, reaching up to 10.54 × 10 8 tons of carbon dioxide equivalents
annually.

Moreover, our findings indicate that a gradual transition to cleaner energy sources within the
HPC sector could substantially mitigate its environmental impact. If HPC centers shift to renew-
able energy sources like wind, solar, and nuclear power, emissions could be reduced by up to
95%. However, such a transition requires targeted policies to encourage HPC centers to relocate
to areas rich in renewable resources and adopt energy-efficient cooling technologies like seawater
cooling. Specific measures, such as providing electricity subsidies to HPC centers that integrate
local renewable energy sources and expanding support for green technology development, could
be pivotal in reducing the carbon footprint of HPC.

Beyond environmental concerns, the unchecked growth of HPC infrastructure may also strain
community energy resources. In regions where HPC facilities are sited, our models predict a po-
tential reduction in local community energy access to as low as 40% of the current levels by 2030,
threatening the energy security and quality of life of local populations. These findings emphasize
the urgency of including HPC in sustainable development goals and call for policies that support
equitable access to energy resources.

To conclude, we respectfully ask the Advisory Board to recognize HPCs environmental impact
within its 2030 goals. By addressing HPC in upcoming developmental agendas, the United Nations
can lead the world toward responsible and sustainable technological advancement.

Thank you for considering our recommendations. We look forward to seeing a more inclusive
approach to HPC and AI governance in future reports.
