# The summary sheet

The summary sheet is the first page a judge reads and often the page that decides whether the rest is read closely. It has to stand alone: a reader who sees only this page should know the problem, your approach, your main results and your recommendation.

## What it must do

1. **Set the context in one or two sentences.** Why the problem matters, in concrete terms.
2. **State your approach for each requirement**: the key idea or definition, the model, and the data.
3. **Give the main results**, with numbers where the problem asks for numbers.
4. **Give the recommendation or conclusion**, and the most important caveat.
5. Mention validation or sensitivity in a phrase, and the non-technical deliverable if there is one.

## What the corpus shows

Every Outstanding paper had a one-page summary, but their styles differed. Two broad types both won:

- **Quantified summaries** that name the answers. For example, 2020B-10876 gave the first-year project IDs, a first-year budget of $1,034,015.58, a 31-year horizon and a recommendation to raise 1.3 times current giving (p. 1). 2022B-12465 attached a number to nearly every claim (p. 1). 2021B-11576 packed each problem's paragraph with results (p. 1), and 2024B-15502 gave intervals in scientific notation (p. 1). 2021A-11462 gave the result (4 Powerwall+, $34,000), a market check and an honest caveat in three paragraphs (p. 1).
- **Method-narrative summaries** that explain the chain of ideas clearly but give few final numbers. Most 2020A papers and several 2023A papers are like this. These worked mainly where the answer is a model or a tool (a job recommender, an impact index) rather than a number.

**Recommendation:** where the requirements ask for values, forecasts, schedules or a choice, put the actual answers in the summary. A reader should not have to search the paper for them. Where the deliverable is a tool, state concretely what it does, what it was tested on, and one or two representative outputs.

## Structures that worked

- **Context, then one paragraph per requirement, then conclusion and keywords.** The most common pattern. 2018A-8700 opened each paragraph with "In problem one", "In problem 2" and so on (p. 1); 2024B-15022 used the form *The Nth requirement was …; to address this, we …* (p. 1); bold "For Task I / Task II" lead-ins (2024B-16226, p. 1).
- **Sequenced steps**: First / Second / Third / Last (2020B-10876, p. 1).
- **Section pointers** inside the summary, "(Section 3.1)", so a judge can jump to the detail (2020B-10839, p. 1).
- **A tiny table or figure**: a single-row Top-10 table (2018A-8744, p. 1); a miniature of the schedule (2020B-10839, p. 1). Use this only if it fits without crowding the text.
- **A title** at the top of the summary page (2020B-10876, 2023A-13719 "Every Single One Matters", 2024B-16226 "Let's Build a Green HPC Together!").
- **A keywords line** at the end, listing the main methods and topics.

## Writing it

- **Write it last**, from the finished paper. Copy the numbers from your table of headline results so they match the body exactly. The 2017A-7359 card notes a drone count that differs between the summary and the body.
- **Lead each paragraph with the result or the key idea**, then the method. "We find that … using …" reads better than "First we used …, then we used …".
- **Name the key definition** ("We define the best battery as the system that meets the worst-case night at the lowest cost") and **name your model** if it helps the reader keep track (2021A-11931's "Battery Ranking Model", 2018A-8992's GRR and PRR).
- **Name methods precisely but briefly.** A string of method names without results is a common weakness: the reader learns what you did but not what you found.
- **One sentence on robustness**: what the sensitivity analysis showed.
- **Fill the page, but do not cram it.** Short paragraphs, white space, no tiny fonts.

## Phrasing patterns

Adapt the pattern, not the words:

- Context with a hook: "We are here to "spread" the news about dandelions and their effects on our environment." (2023A-14632, p. 1)
- The reframing in one sentence: "Any battery can be tiled to be sufficient, but some batteries will do this more efficiently than others." (2021A-11462, p. 1)
- The key idea: "Cost effectiveness balances and quantifies the somewhat contradictory objectives of FRPCE." (2020B-10876, p. 1)
- Overcoming a data gap: "we designed an HPC energy consumption model that indirectly estimates energy use based on societal computing demands, overcoming the challenge of obtaining HPC energy consumption data." (2024B-15502, p. 1)
- Design approach: "Our team constructed our model from the bottom-up, first considering the basic ecological and financial impacts of individual buses." (2023B-13694, p. 1)

## Quick test

Give the summary to someone who has not seen the paper. Ask them: What was the question? What did the team conclude? Why should I believe it? If they cannot answer all three, revise.
