# Memos, letters, articles and app pages

Most HiMCM problems ask for one non-technical piece: a memo to a board, a letter to officials, a news article or release, or the design of an app or web page. It is usually short (often one page) and is read by judges as a test of whether you can explain your work to the people who would use it.

## The range of formats in the corpus

The statement names the reader and the format, and it changes every year. Across 2014–2024 the corpus includes:

| Format | Reader | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Letter | Director of Transportation, mayor, company president, IOC, UN advisory body | 2014A, 2015A, 2016A, 2016B, 2024A, 2024B |
| Two-page letter or memo | Mayor; a client's agent ("Ms. Mogul") | 2016A, 2017A, 2017B |
| Memo to a board | Foundation board | 2020B |
| Report to a mayor (1–2 pages) | City leadership | 2015B |
| One-page news synopsis, news release or newspaper article | General public | 2014B, 2018A, 2018B, 2019A, 2019B, 2021B, 2022B |
| Blog, infographic or public web page | General public, farmers | 2022A |
| Driver-education material | Drivers | 2015A-6080, 2015A-6117 |
| App or web page design | Students, riders | 2018A, 2020A |

The principles below apply to all of them; the format sets the layout.

## What all good pieces share

- **Written for the named reader.** A homeowner, a city council, a foundation board, high school students. Use their vocabulary and answer their question ("What should we do?", "Which should I choose?", "Should I worry?").
- **No technical vocabulary.** No equations, no model names unless explained in plain words. "The model compares how much each project would achieve per dollar" rather than "we maximize Σ B·S/P".
- **Concrete results.** One to three memorable numbers, in units the reader understands, and a clear recommendation. The non-technical piece must agree exactly with the numbers in the paper.
- **Looks like the real thing.** A memo has a To/From/Date/Subject header; a letter has a salutation and a signature; an article has a headline, often a standfirst, short paragraphs and a captioned image; an app page shows screens.
- **Fits the page limit** set by the statement (usually one page).
- **An honest caveat** where it matters (2020B-10839's memo ended with an explicit caveat about failed projects, p. 18).

## Memo or letter to a decision-maker

Typical structure:

1. **Header**: To, From, Date, Subject (memo) or salutation (letter).
2. **Opening**: the purpose in one or two sentences. Letters to officials often open by acknowledging their work, then name the gap: "We commend the Advisory Board for its comprehensive approach to AI governance; however, we note with concern that the report does not significantly address the environmental impacts of High Performance Computing (HPC), a critical component of modern AI systems." (2024B-15502, p. 25)
3. **What we did**, in plain words, in two to four sentences.
4. **What we found**: the key numbers. 2024B-15502 led with three quantified findings and an equity argument; 2023B-13694 gave a yearly replacement table and two ten-year totals ("just under $500 million" and "just slightly over 300 million kilograms of CO2 equivalent emissions", p. 24).
5. **What we recommend**: specific actions, possibly numbered or bolded (2024B-16226 ended with five bolded policy asks, pp. 19–20). 2020B-10876 listed a "First solution" and a "Second solution" with concrete numbers and a strong recommendation (p. 2).
6. **What the reader can adjust**, if the model is a tool (2020B-10839 explained which rates and benchmarks the Board could change, p. 18).
7. **Close**: a forward-looking sentence and the team's signature (control number, not names, under current rules).

Lead with the answer the reader asked for. 2024A-15041's letter states its prediction in the first paragraph: "Our model predicts that the first SDE to be considered for inclusion in Brisbane 2032 is Skateboarding, followed by Sport Climbing, and then Breaking." (2024A-15041, p. 2). 2017B-8121's letter to the client's agent checks the design against the brief with numbers: "Our design meets all requirements laid out in the request." (2017B-8121, p. 2), followed by total slope length and the trail mix by difficulty.

If the deliverable includes a table the reader must use, explain how to read it. 2020B-10997's board memo does this for its schedule: "Under the specific annual funding column, the number corresponding to a project indicates the start year of the project." (2020B-10997, p. 23), followed by a worked example and the meaning of "NC" (not chosen).

A schedule the reader will act on can go inside the letter itself. 2016A-6453's two-page letter to the mayor is followed directly by a race-day start schedule by group (pp. 2–3).

Other good devices: a small table or chart (2020B-10839 included the schedule chart), a general rule the reader can apply ("transition as early as the budget allows", 2023B-13694), headed sections for different concerns (2017A-7359's letter to the mayor had four headed parts).

## News article or press release

Typical structure:

1. **Headline** that states the news or asks the reader's question: "Would The Bottled Water Ban Backfire?" (2019B-9548, p. 4); "Saving Lake Mead: Recycling Wastewater Does Wonders" (2021B-11898, p. 3); "Act! Drastic temperature rise in response to massive carbon dioxide emissions" (2022B-12465, p. 22).
2. **Opening hook** from the reader's life or this week's news: everyday batteries ("When you panic over a dead T.V. remote…", 2021A-11462, p. 20); this summer's heat and the shortage declaration (2021B-11898); a satellite-photo comparison (2021B-11552); a news event such as COP26 (2021A-11900, p. 2).
3. **The finding in plain terms**, with one memorable number: a lake that "could lose up to 200 feet in water elevation" by 2050 (2021B-12384, p. 2); a level falling about 12 ft per year (2021B-11576). Turn numbers into comparisons: "increased by 30% compared to 60 years ago", "twice as high at the end of this century" (2022B-12465, p. 22).
4. **How the team reached it**, in a few sentences and without jargon.
5. **What it means for the reader**: actions, from personal to policy (2022B-12465 graded actions from talking to friends up to international cooperation).
6. **Visuals**: a photo or one simple chart with a caption.

More opening hooks from the corpus: a direct question, "However, have you ever considered the impacts and the costs behind free charging in public places?" (2019A-9824, p. 2); the reader in a familiar situation, with a closing line that returns to it, "Next time you’re thirsty, think about that." (2019B-10407, p. 3).

**An analogy can carry the model's structure** if it follows the real structure. 2022B-12678 explained three nested models as a hot dog: "We sum up the creation of our models using the analogy of a perfectly grilled hotdog." (2022B-12678, p. 2). The linear model is the bun, the economic model adds the sausage and the mechanistic model adds the toppings, which matches how each model builds on the previous one.

Press-release conventions also worked: a dateline, attributed quotes from team spokespeople and a "for more information" line (2018A-8744, p. 2); a product launch with an invented company and a quote (2018B-8506, p. 3; 2018A-8874, p. 1).

**Tone can vary.** Plain and informative (most papers), upbeat and conversational for teenagers ("What are you waiting for? Come and find yourself a job today!", 2020A-10549, p. 2), or openly lyrical (2023A-13643's dandelion article opens with "It is always a dreamlike scene to see dandelion seeds flying on a summer day", p. 24). Whatever the tone, connect it to your results. An article with no findings from the model reads as a stance piece rather than a summary of the work.

## Infographic, blog or public web page

When the statement asks for an infographic or a blog (2022A), the same rules apply with more visual weight: one main message, a few numbers with units the reader knows, a simple chart or diagram of the process, and practical advice. 2022A-12821 designed an infographic to introduce a public website holding its findings (p. 1); 2022A-13014 summarized its population, sensitivity and hive results in a non-technical infographic. Check that every number on the graphic matches the paper.

## App or web page design

When the statement asks how you would present the model to users (2018A, 2020A):

- **Show the screens**: input, results, and one or two supporting features, with callouts explaining each (2020A-10549 annotated three phone screens; 2020A-10701 described each screen by function).
- **Map inputs to the model.** The questionnaire wording should be the actual user-facing text, and each question should correspond to a model input (2020A-10550's 0–100 questionnaire maps to the cluster weights; 2020A-10656's website buttons use the model's own scales, p. 15).
- **Explain why this medium.** "We also need to do calculations in the background, so physical options like newspapers do not suffice" (2020A-10656, p. 13).
- **Keep user effort low.** "Conscious that our algorithm is user-facing, it is infeasible to ask the user 28 questions just to get a personalized rollercoaster ranking." (2018A-8992, p. 22)
- **Consider real-world details**: privacy (2020A-10701 let students withhold school and gender "but this will result in a less targeted list", p. 22), updates when data change, and interactive sensitivity such as weight sliders (2020A-11135, p. 24).
- **Show the output with reasons**: a ranked list with scores or a short explanation of why each item is recommended.

## Common weaknesses

- Repeating the summary sheet in different words instead of writing for the named reader.
- Technical terms the reader would not know.
- Numbers that differ from the paper's.
- No recommendation, or a recommendation the model does not support.
- A layout that does not look like the genre requested.
