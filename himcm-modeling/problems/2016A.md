# 2016A: Swim, Bike, and Run

## The task in brief
The organizer needs divisions and wave starts that reduce overtaking while keeping road closure under 5.5 hours, then asks whether changing race distances helps. The hard part is coupling heterogeneous segment times, transitions, wave interactions and two objectives.

## What makes this problem hard
All teams use the prior-race dataset to estimate pace distributions, group athletes into waves and simulate or calculate overlap. They report an organizer-facing timetable and discuss distance changes as a sensitivity question.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Route and result |
|---|---|
| [2016A-6453](../papers/2016A-6453.md) | cluster analysis; genetic wave order; linear-programming refinement |
| [2016A-6770](../papers/2016A-6770.md) | 24 divisions; quartiles; log/linear/exponential segment curves |
| [2016A-7054](../papers/2016A-7054.md) | three-lane NetLogo model; 55-person wave-packets; composite metric |
| [2016A-7055](../papers/2016A-7055.md) | 18 divisions; normal speed sampling; 15 schedule comparisons |

## Common ground

- Prior-race segment and transition data drive divisioning and wave timing.
- Every paper treats congestion and the 5.5-hour closure as coupled operational constraints.
- Waves are chosen by pace or speed compatibility, then checked with tables or simulation.
- Race-day schedules and a mayoral letter make the model implementable.

## Different routes that all worked

Clustering, quartile grouping, wave-packets and 18-category schedules all work because they preserve pace information before simulation. Some teams use a hard road limit; others include it in a composite score, which changes how results should be compared.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Define congestion in terms of passing, local density or speed overlap.
- Use observed segment and transition times, not only total time.
- Optimize wave order and interval, then simulate individual variation.
- Check the road-closure hard constraint separately from the congestion score.

A robust triathlon workflow is data summaries → candidate divisions → wave optimization → individual-level simulation. Keep the 5.5-hour limit as a constraint, not a soft weight unless the organizer explicitly agrees. If changing distances, compare against the selected schedule and report finishers, congestion and closure together.

## Comparative synthesis

The papers differ in how much they compress the athlete data before scheduling. Team 6453 clusters prior-race results into six groups, uses a genetic algorithm to order and time the groups, and then applies linear programming to account for race distances; its letter reports total competition time within 4.5 hours (6453, pp. 1–3, 4–29). Team 6770 estimates average swim, bike, run and transition times for 24 divisions, uses quartiles to form waves, and refines a distance-time picture with logarithmic swimming, linear cycling and exponential running curves. Its proposed 2.5-minute wave intervals produce a 5.3-hour road-closure plan (6770, pp. 1–3, 4–35). Team 7054 models the course in NetLogo with fast, main and slow lanes, then tests 55-person wave-packets containing 25% fast, 50% medium and 25% slow athletes. It reports 72% lower congestion with lanes, 29% lower congestion when bike and run road systems are separated, and 27% lower congestion when packets are ordered by speed, although its schedule discussion must be read alongside the road-closure requirement (7054, pp. 1–3, 20–40). Team 7055 evaluates 15 schedules with normal speed sampling, 18 gender/age/status divisions and a composite score for congestion, closure and finishers; its recommended schedule closes roads from about 8:00 to 1:30 (7055, pp. 1–3, 14–35).

The comparison suggests a three-stage workflow. First summarize segment and transition distributions, not just total finish time. Next choose divisions and wave order so a slow tail in one wave does not repeatedly block the next. Finally simulate individual athletes and enforce the 5.5-hour closure limit as a constraint or a clearly justified weighted objective. Distance changes should be tested on both a good and a poor schedule, because the same shorter course can reduce overlap in one order and increase it in another. A useful report gives the timetable, expected closure interval, finishers by cutoff, and the definition of congestion used in every percentage.


## Detailed comparison

The main design trade-off is compression versus variation. 6453’s six clusters make optimization tractable and produce a short schedule, but cluster spread needs checking. 6770 keeps 24 divisions and fits segment-specific speed curves, which preserves more detail but increases scheduling complexity. 7054’s wave-packets deliberately mix fast, medium and slow athletes to manage lane interference, while 7055 uses 18 divisions and samples individual speeds from normal distributions. These are all defensible if the paper explains why the chosen grouping matches the organizer’s goal.

A complete comparison should report at least four outputs: congestion definition and value, road-closure duration, number of finishers or late athletes, and the wave timetable. If a paper reports a percentage reduction, state its baseline and whether it comes from one simulation run or repeated trials. The hard 5.5-hour road constraint deserves a separate pass after optimization; a composite score can otherwise hide an infeasible schedule. Distance sensitivity should be linked to wave order because changing a segment changes when waves overlap. Operational details matter too: transition-area capacity, awards, volunteer staffing and road-opening time should agree with the timetable. The strongest new solution can combine 6453’s staged optimization, 6770’s quantile view, 7054’s explicit course lanes and 7055’s probabilistic entrants, then show which assumption changes the selected schedule (6453, pp. 4–29; 6770, pp. 4–35; 7054, pp. 20–40; 7055, pp. 14–35).

A schedule is only complete when its times are internally consistent. Check that the last wave’s predicted finish, transition-area closure and road reopening agree, and label any schedule that trades the 5.5-hour limit for lower congestion. This is the clearest way to compare the four papers’ different objective choices.

The same table should identify wave interval, expected finish tail and road reopening. This lets an organizer compare a compact six-cluster schedule with a more detailed 18- or 24-division schedule without losing the operational constraint.

When presenting a new schedule, keep the model’s grouping rule visible. A reviewer should be able to tell whether a wave contains athletes with similar means, similar quantiles or a deliberately mixed speed composition, because those choices change the interpretation of congestion.
