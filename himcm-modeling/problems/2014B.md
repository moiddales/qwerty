# 2014B: The Next Plague?

## The task in brief
The problem combines sparse early case data, disease classification, containment decisions, questions for a field team and a revised model after new evidence. A useful solution must show how uncertain transmission and mortality affect both the epidemic curve and the allocation of scarce staff, isolation, transport and vaccines.

## What makes this problem hard
Every paper starts with an SIR-family model, then adds a feature needed by the prompt: latent infection, a contagious sub-state, age-specific mortality, vaccination, Monte Carlo uncertainty, resource scoring or a response tree. They treat the new contact-transmission and age-risk evidence as a model update rather than an appendix.

## Common ground

- Every team starts from an SIR-family compartment model and states what is known versus assumed.
- Latency, mortality and contact structure are represented explicitly in at least one revision.
- Simulations or scenario tables show that intervention recommendations depend on uncertain parameters.
- The nontechnical deliverable translates model thresholds into public-health action.

## Different routes that all worked

The routes differ in compartment detail and policy layer: Monte Carlo ranges, R0/SEIR, age-stratified five-state equations, or a vaccine decision tree. The common reusable structure is forecast → intervention scenarios → data questions → update.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Separate biological progression from policy action; make compartments and time step explicit.
- Use ranges or repeated runs when contact, latent-period and mortality parameters are unknown.
- Ask for data that changes decisions: contacts, age risk, incubation, deaths and geography.
- Turn outputs into thresholds and recommendations, then state which assumptions drive them.

A strong epidemic paper is modular. Start with a baseline forecast, add the evidence-supported mechanism, then show intervention scenarios and resource consequences. Keep R0, attack rate, peak cases and deaths distinct. If a resource score or aid ranking is used, publish its weights and test alternatives. Simulation spread is evidence about model uncertainty, not a substitute for calibration.

## Comparative synthesis

The team-by-team comparison reveals a progression from biological compartments to operational policy. Team 4637 uses a standard SIR foundation and adapts a rumor-spreading model to estimate the probability that an infected person contacts and infects a susceptible person. Its Java program varies unknown parameters, runs until infection reaches zero, and uses repeated trajectories to classify severity and containment (4637, pp. 9–21). Team 4880 begins with an R0 estimate of about 1.79, classifies the disease by comparing that value with representative transmission modes, and adds an exposed compartment in an SEIR model. It then scores resource needs by disease stage and ranks potential aid countries using distance and GDP relative to Indonesia (4880, pp. 1–2, 9–35). Team 5295 makes the incubation period visible with five states—susceptible, infected, contagious, removed and deceased—and revises mortality and contact parameters for children and elderly people. Its doctor and prevention scenarios answer the allocation part of the prompt (5295, pp. 1–2, 5–15). Team 5325 separates prediction from action most explicitly: an SIR baseline forecasts spread, an SIRD model adds deaths and vaccine protection, and a decision tree maps individual and community risk to treatment, transport or restriction (5325, pp. 1–3, 5–23).

These are not four competing claims about one true epidemic. They are four ways to make sparse evidence useful. The cards support a disciplined sequence: define compartments and time step; identify parameters that are genuinely unknown; run a range or Monte Carlo experiment; ask the field team for observations that would change the decision; then update the model when the new contact-transmission and age-risk information arrives. A response threshold should be labelled as a policy rule, while an R0 or mortality parameter should be labelled as an estimate or scenario input. Resource scores and donor rankings also need visible weights. The strongest reusable idea is modularity: the forecast can be replaced or recalibrated without rewriting the intervention logic.


## Detailed comparison

The papers also differ in what they call evidence. Team 4637’s repeated runs are useful when the contact and recovery parameters are uncertain, but they do not replace case-data calibration. Team 4880’s R0 classification is a compact communication device; the resource and donor model then asks a different question about logistics. Team 5295’s contagious sub-state prevents an incubation period from being treated as an immediate transmission event, while its age-stratified revision shows how a new fact changes the equations. Team 5325’s decision tree makes the policy trigger visible, but its thresholds remain choices that officials should revisit.

A new response can borrow all four layers without claiming more precision than the data support. Use a baseline SIR or SEIR curve to show uncontrolled growth. Add age, latency, deaths or vaccination only when the prompt or field team supplies a reason. Run low, central and high parameter cases, and report peak cases, deaths, duration and the time at which an intervention changes the trajectory. Ask questions that discriminate among those cases: number of close contacts, incubation duration, infectious period, age-specific fatality, and whether the nearby island has imported cases. Then connect the output to scarce resources: beds, doctors, transport, isolation space and vaccines. The news synopsis should say what action is justified now, what evidence would change it and which results are scenario outputs rather than measurements (4637, pp. 22–31; 4880, pp. 25–43; 5295, pp. 12–17; 5325, pp. 14–24).

One further lesson is to preserve the distinction between an observation and a model state. “Fifteen deaths in a week,” an assumed latent period and an estimated R0 should not be placed in the same table without labels. The cards’ best sections make that distinction visible before the policy recommendation.
