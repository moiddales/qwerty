# 2021A: Storing the Sun

## The task in brief
1. For an off-the-grid 1600 sq ft home: (a) list questions that determine energy needs and discuss the range of answers; (b) build a model or algorithm to choose the "best" battery storage system; (c) apply it to the sample batteries (a five-battery table is provided, "not all" options) and discuss the choice.
2. Adjust and generalize the model to any home's needs and preferences; discuss the changes and evaluate the model.
3. Cement/concrete batteries: (a) advantages, disadvantages, how they could be used; (b) what additional information would be needed to model and compare them (no model required).
4. A one-page non-technical news article about the decision model and the future of cement batteries.

Format: 25 pages total, including summary sheet, table of contents, article and references.

## What makes this problem hard
- **"Best" is undefined.** Battery specifications pull in different directions (cost vs capacity vs power vs efficiency vs lifetime), and the given table has gaps (missing instantaneous ratings, incomparable continuous-power formats).
- **Demand is the hidden half of the problem.** A choice only makes sense against a household's energy and peak-power needs, which the team must estimate (appliances, schedules, nights, cloudy days).
- **Units versus systems.** Small lead-acid batteries and large lithium units are not comparable one-to-one; any fair comparison has to consider how many units a home needs.
- **Generalization must be real.** Requirement 2 asks for a model that adapts to different homes, so the inputs have to be explicit and adjustable.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| [2021A-11462](../papers/2021A-11462.md) | Sizing + cost: how many units of each battery cover the worst case, and which system is cheapest | Appliance energy budget; worst case = longest night + one cloudy day; heat-loss HVAC model; NOAA night length; ceiling-function unit count | Physics-based generalization (latitude, temperature, area, occupants) and a Python calculator; market price check |
| [2021A-11900](../papers/2021A-11900.md) | Multi-criteria choice across eight battery attributes | AHP; entropy weight method tested and rejected; location-specific weight sets | Generalization through weights that depend on climate and wealth across nine world locations |
| [2021A-11931](../papers/2021A-11931.md) | Requirement matching: penalize each battery's shortfall from the household's needs | "Disparity" penalty model with calibrated constants; autonomy days with necessary vs optional loads; pack size n optimized | Questionnaire-driven inputs; 16 real batteries; sensitivity analysis on inputs and weights |

These three papers show three different meanings of "best". 11462 asks which battery meets the worst case at the lowest cost, an engineering sizing answer with one interpretable number (dollars). 11900 asks which battery has the best weighted attributes, a classic multi-criteria decision answer where the judgment lives in the weights. 11931 asks which battery pack falls least short of what this household wants, a requirement-matching answer that is personal by construction. All three reach defensible but different recommendations: Tesla Powerwall+ ×4, Discover AES 7.4 kWh, and a lead-acid pack under a $12,000 budget. This is evidence that the modeling choices, stated clearly, matter more than reaching a particular answer.

## Common ground
- **Demand estimated before the choice is made.** Each paper builds an appliance table or household profile (family size, monthly kWh, peak load) and uses it to answer Requirement 1a.
- **Explicit assumptions with justifications**, often citing US EIA data.
- **Requirement-labelled structure.** Sections tagged "(For question 2)", "Question 1a/1b" or similar make it easy for a reader to see where each requirement is answered.
- **Concrete battery handled qualitatively plus a quick estimate**, ending with a structured list of missing information (durability and repair, structural strength, energy density over cycles, price, continuous power, safety).
- **A designed news article** with a headline and images, written for homeowners.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Cost-based sizing** (11462) vs **weighted scoring** (11900) vs **penalty-based requirement matching** (11931).
- **Generalization mechanism:** physical formulas in the model inputs (11462), location-dependent weights (11900), or user-supplied requirements and weights (11931).
- **Candidate set:** the five given batteries (11462, 11900) vs an expanded list of 16 real products (11931). Both were acceptable; expanding the set showed initiative and gave the generalized model more to choose from.
- **Validation:** comparison with a commercial kit price (11462), comparison with an alternative weighting method and engineering arguments (11900), and ±10% sensitivity on every input and weight (11931).

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- **Define "best" in one sentence early**, then build the model to compute exactly that. Each paper's clarity came from this.
- **Turn "choose a product" into "choose a system".** Let the number of units be a variable, so products of different sizes compete fairly (11462, 11931).
- **Design for a stated worst case** (longest night, cloudy day, days of autonomy). It makes sizing defensible and gives a natural place for sensitivity analysis (11462, 11931).
- **Separate essential from optional demand.** A fallback mode ("essentials only") yields graded rather than pass/fail scores (11931).
- **Generalize by exposing inputs.** Location, household and preference parameters should enter the model explicitly, through formulas, weights or questionnaires, so that Requirement 2 is answered by the same model.
- **Sanity-check against the real world:** market prices, practitioner advice and engineering facts such as cycle life or thermal-runaway risk.
- **For "what information would you need" questions**, give a structured list with a sentence on why each item matters for the model.
