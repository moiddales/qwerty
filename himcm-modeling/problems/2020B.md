# 2020B: Funding Biodiversity Conservation

## The task in brief
The Florida Rare Plant Conservation Endowment must fund recovery projects for 48 imperiled plant species over decades while annual funds are limited and project costs vary by year. Teams must identify objectives and plant characteristics, define factors for a decision model, produce a fundraising schedule and priority order, and write a one-page memo to the FRPCE Board. The supplied rows contain benefit, taxonomic uniqueness, feasibility of success and year-by-year costs. The missing spreadsheet attachment in this repository means the cards describe methods and reported results; a new application needs the original data.

## What makes this problem hard
- “Best” can mean saving the most species, maximizing ecological value, protecting taxonomic uniqueness, reducing expected failure, or keeping annual fundraising stable.
- Projects last different numbers of years and cannot all start at once. Starting one project consumes future budget, so this is a scheduling/portfolio problem rather than a single knapsack.
- Delay may reduce feasibility of success, but the statement gives no species-specific population trajectories.
- The objective is long-term and multi-dimensional: a smooth budget can sacrifice benefit, while a high-benefit schedule can create years the endowment cannot finance.
- The memo must turn a 25-year, 48-project schedule into an actionable recommendation with a clear annual fundraising number.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| [2020B-10839](../papers/2020B-10839.md) | Random search over project start years | Time-decaying benefit and feasibility; risk-adjusted cost; stochastic plans | Benefit threshold, cost × annual-cost-SD score, and Excel schedule export |
| [2020B-10876](../papers/2020B-10876.md) | Cost-effectiveness under an annual cap | Population-to-feasibility fit; priority-order Monte Carlo; greedy decoding | Budget sweep identifies a point of diminishing returns |
| [2020B-10997](../papers/2020B-10997.md) | Biological urgency plus stable endowment spending | Allee-effect feasibility decay; TOPSIS/entropy; DP + greedy + GA | Two-stage optimizer and $500k versus $2m funding-cap recommendations |

The papers agree that start year is the main decision variable and that delay should have a cost. They differ in how that cost is represented and how schedules are searched. 10839 samples complete schedules and filters them by benefit. 10876 searches over priority orders and greedily decodes a feasible timetable. 10997 builds year-specific priorities, solves a baseline by dynamic programming, then uses a genetic algorithm to smooth spending. All three report budget scenarios rather than one unexplained “minimum.”

The numerical recommendations illustrate three legitimate meanings of “minimum fundraising.” Team 10839 treats the all-projects-start-in-year-one plan as a maximum-benefit benchmark, keeps schedules above a chosen benefit percentage, and ranks the survivors by risk-adjusted total cost multiplied by the standard deviation of annual spending. Its reported plan runs 34 years, retains 89% of the benchmark benefit and has an adjusted cost of about $107.1 million. Team 10876 uses the FRPCE annual report to motivate a flat cap of about $1.05 million; its priority-order search completes 47 projects in 31 years and identifies roughly $1.71 million as a point where additional annual funding has diminishing returns. Team 10997 recommends a lower $500,000 cap when omission is allowed (43 of 48 projects), and $2 million when all 48 should finish within 40 years. The models answer different board questions, so their figures should not be compared as if they optimized the same objective.

The biological assumptions also vary in useful ways. 10839 uses exponential rules for threatened level, uniqueness and feasibility, with rates exposed as user parameters. 10876 fits a log relationship between population size and survival probability, then combines it with exponential population decline to obtain a linear feasibility penalty per year of delay. 10997 uses a strong Allee effect in a modified logistic model, making sparse populations especially vulnerable and translating population density into feasibility. In every case the decay curve is a scenario representation of urgency; none supplies species-specific trajectories for all 48 projects. A team using the problem card should therefore justify its biological form and test the schedule under faster and slower decline.

## Common ground
- Define the Board’s objectives before selecting a score: benefit, feasibility, cost, coverage and annual stability.
- Keep project costs continuous after a start unless the problem explicitly permits pausing.
- Treat delay as a modeled assumption, make its parameters visible and run sensitivity on them.
- Show the complete timetable or project × year schedule, plus annual totals and horizon.
- Distinguish cash raised from risk-adjusted or expected cost.
- Use a feasible baseline before a stochastic or heuristic optimizer, and compare objective components after optimization.
- Explain the recommendation in the memo with one or two headline funding caps and their project counts.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Objective:** risk-adjusted total cost and funding SD (10839), sum of benefit × feasibility per dollar (10876), or TOPSIS priority plus expected benefit divided by funding fluctuation (10997).
- **Biological delay:** exponential threatened-level/feasibility rules (10839), a fitted log-survival versus population plus exponential decline (10876), or logistic decline with strong Allee effect (10997).
- **Search:** random start-year plans (10839), Monte Carlo priority permutations with greedy earliest starts (10876), and dynamic programming followed by genetic algorithm (10997).
- **Recommendation:** target benefit percentage and three best schedules (10839), flat/growing/knee-point budgets (10876), or $500k and $2m caps for partial versus complete coverage (10997).

The solution mechanics are complementary. 10839’s random start-year model is easy to expose to a board: change the benefit threshold or decay rates, then export the three best timetables. 10876’s random-key search separates a priority order from a greedy timetable decoder, guaranteeing that the decoded plan respects the annual cap; its convergence plot shows that the best score stops improving after roughly 2,200 sampled orders. 10997’s dynamic-programming seed gives the genetic algorithm a feasible, high-priority starting point. Its chromosome is the vector of project start years, and mutation is constrained so the optimized plan keeps at least 95% of the initial expected benefit. These design choices show how to balance explainability, feasibility and search quality under a long horizon.

The outputs should be read together. A project list without annual cash totals does not answer the fundraising question, while a smooth cash curve without feasibility or expected benefit can postpone urgent species. 10839 reports the benefit threshold, adjusted cost and annual-cost spread; 10876 reports the project count, horizon and budget knee; 10997 reports expected benefit, annual-cost standard deviation and total score for both caps. A good new solution can borrow this reporting pattern even if it chooses a different optimizer.

Validation is similarly varied. 10839 uses distributions of scores over many feasible random plans and perturbs four user parameters. 10876 checks Monte Carlo convergence, varies the population-decline rate and sweeps the annual budget. 10997 compares DP/greedy and genetic-algorithm schedules, tests a random versus seeded initial condition, and changes the extinction-rate coefficient by 1% and 5%. These checks do not prove that a schedule is biologically optimal; they show how dependent the recommendation is on assumptions and whether the budget constraint is respected.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Use a project × year table so starting a project, its duration and annual cash burden can be checked visually.
- Make the schedule evaluation multi-part: report expected ecological value, number completed, annual mean/SD, maximum annual spend and completion horizon.
- Compare a flat budget with a growing budget or a knee of the benefit–funding curve; Boards often need options rather than one answer.
- Use domain evidence for feasibility decay when available, and state clearly when a common rate is only a scenario parameter.
- Seed heuristic optimization with a feasible schedule, preserve a minimum-benefit constraint, and test multiple initial conditions.
- Write the memo as a decision document: objective, recommended cap, projects completed, timeline, and what changes if the Board chooses a different priority.
