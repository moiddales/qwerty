# 2014A: Unloading Commuter Trains

## The task in brief
The task asks for time from train seat to street level for one and two full trains, then asks where to place stairs, how many stairs to build and how capacity changes the answer. The hard part is the burst of passengers: door release, walking distance and stair service interact, so an average walking time alone misses the queue bottleneck.

## What makes this problem hard
All five papers decompose the journey into train exit, platform travel, stair waiting and ascent. They state occupancy, car dimensions, walking speeds, platform width and staircase capacity before calculating. Every paper reports a practical letter to a transportation decision maker.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Route and result |
|---|---|
| [2014A-4671](../papers/2014A-4671.md) | queueing plus cellular automaton; six-case cross-check; congestion-aware routes |
| [2014A-4813](../papers/2014A-4813.md) | closed-form distance/rate equations; queue threshold; middle or paired stairs |
| [2014A-4823](../papers/2014A-4823.md) | object-based passenger/stair simulation; repeated layout search |
| [2014A-5086](../papers/2014A-5086.md) | analytic plus simulation model; widening from two to six columns |
| [2014A-5089](../papers/2014A-5089.md) | normal walking speeds; arrival distributions; safety-first tie-break |

## Common ground

- Every team separates travel to the staircase from the staircase queue or service process; this is where the bottleneck appears.
- All five use a full-train burst and make car/platform/stair dimensions explicit.
- The papers distinguish an average passenger metric from a last-passenger or queue metric, even when they emphasize different endpoints.
- Each turns a layout result into advice for a transportation director.

## Different routes that all worked

The papers span closed-form queue formulas, discrete passenger simulation, and stochastic arrival distributions. A useful adaptation keeps the queue-rate baseline even when a more detailed pedestrian model is added, and reports both mean and tail time.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Define which endpoint is being optimized (stair foot versus street level).
- Use a queue or service-rate comparison; stairs are the bottleneck when arrivals exceed capacity.
- Treat layout and capacity as separate levers and test one and two trains.
- Cross-check a formula with a spatial simulation or arrival distribution.

For similar evacuation and transit problems, separate a transparent rate model from a spatial or stochastic model. Report average and last-passenger times separately, because a design can preserve the mean while creating a dangerous fan. Use a second objective—queue waiting or crowding—to break ties between layouts.

## Comparative synthesis

The five cards show why the same prompt supports different but compatible objectives. Team 4671 defines “unloading time” as the instant the final passenger reaches the foot of a staircase, and models the four stages with a d/d/s queue and a congestion-aware cellular automaton. Its reported example is about 261 seconds for one train and about 498 seconds for two trains when each stair accommodates two people; the simulation and formula agree across six test cases (4671, pp. 7–23). Team 4813 instead emphasizes the average passenger’s seat-to-street time. Its rate comparison identifies a fanning threshold at about 19 rows per car, recommends a single middle staircase, and pairs opposite-facing stairs when several are installed (4813, pp. 15–29). Team 4823 writes an object-based simulator with person and staircase objects, then searches layouts; its M8-train and Grand Central analogues make the scale concrete (4823, pp. 3–4, 20–31). Team 5086 gives the clearest capital intervention: in its 600-passenger, ten-car worked case, increasing the staircase from two to six columns roughly halves clearance time (5086, pp. 1–2, 17–21). Team 5089 treats walking speed as random and gives a lexicographic objective: first mean seat-to-street time, then queue waiting, because two layouts can have similar means but different arrival bunching and perceived safety (5089, pp. 2, 10–18).

The common modeling lesson is to expose the service rate. A formula that only averages platform distance will miss a stair queue; a simulation that only shows a crowd will not explain why widening helps. A strong new solution can therefore use a rate inequality to locate the bottleneck, then use a spatial or stochastic model to compare layouts. It should state whether its headline time is an average, a last-passenger time or a full evacuation time. It should also keep one-train and two-train cases separate: a second train changes the arrival burst and may change which staircase is limiting. The old papers are especially useful as examples of how to make a public recommendation conditional on assumptions rather than presenting one layout as universal.


## Detailed comparison

A useful way to organize a new solution is to make the queue threshold the first decision. Compute the passenger release rate from car rows, door count and occupancy. Compute the staircase service rate from step width, number of columns, climbing speed and the number of stairs. If arrival exceeds service, shortening the walk to the stairs has little effect until capacity changes; if service exceeds arrival, location and walking distance matter more. This explains why 4813’s row threshold, 4671’s rate comparison and 5086’s widening experiment can all be right for their own parameter sets. It also explains 5089’s second objective: even when two layouts have similar average travel, one can produce a tighter arrival bunch and a less comfortable queue.

For two trains, preserve the common-platform geometry and report whether stairs are shared or independent. The cards show that the two-train case is more than doubling a one-train time: platform walking, simultaneous door release and queue overlap change the shape of the arrival curve. A reproducible report should include a small baseline table with train count, car rows, passenger count, platform length, stair count and capacity, then show how each design change affects average and tail time. It should also state whether a reported recommendation is a build decision, such as widening a stair, or an operational decision, such as changing train stopping position. The letters in these papers succeed because they do not hide the endpoint definition or present a simulation parameter as a universal station fact (4671, pp. 3–6; 4813, pp. 4–8; 5086, pp. 2–5).
