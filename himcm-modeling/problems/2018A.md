# 2018A: Roller Coaster

## The task in brief
1. Create an objective, quantitative algorithm (or set of algorithms) for a descriptive roller coaster rating/ranking system, based **only** on numerical and descriptive specification data (speed, duration, steel or wood, drop, …), for coasters currently operating.
2. Use it to produce a "Top 10 Roller Coasters in the World" list, and compare and discuss the results with **at least two** other rating/ranking systems found online.
3. Describe the concept and design of a user-friendly app that uses the algorithm to help a potential rider find a coaster they would want to ride (no coding required).
4. Write a one-page non-technical **News Release** describing the algorithm, results and app.
- Submission: one-page Summary Sheet + one-page News Release + solution of at most 30 pages (32 total); references and appendices excluded from the limit.
- Data: `COMAP_RollerCoasterData_2018.xlsx` (300 coasters whose height, speed and/or drop are above the worldwide average; columns include park and location, construction, type, status, year opened, height, speed, length, inversions yes/no and count, drop, duration, G-force, vertical angle). The statement warns that real databases contain "missing, noisy, or inconsistent data." Suggested sources: RCDB, Ultimate Rollercoaster, Coasterpedia.

## What makes this problem hard
- **Defining "best" without subjective input.** The algorithm must be objective, but any ranking implies a judgement about what makes a coaster good. Teams had to decide how to orient and weight variables, and whether "more extreme" is always better.
- **Heavy missingness.** Drop, duration, G-force and vertical angle are missing for large shares of the 300 coasters (for example 53% of drop and about 73% of G-force, as reported by 8744, p. 8; 216 of 299 G-force values in 8992, p. 6). The imputation method strongly affects the result.
- **Noisy and inconsistent entries.** Some Type cells contain "Wood"/"Steel"; some numeric values disagree with online sources (8554 p. 5; 8874 pp. 9–10).
- **Strongly correlated variables.** Height, drop and speed are nearly collinear (r ≈ 0.95–0.97, 8744 p. 7), so naive equal weighting counts the same feature several times.
- **Categorical variables** (construction, type) need a defensible numeric treatment.
- **Weighting vs objectivity.** Purely data-driven weights (entropy) may not reflect what riders value; preference-based weights (AHP, surveys) bring back subjectivity. Teams had to choose and justify a balance.
- **Comparison is not validation.** Online lists are subjective, often US-centred, and include coasters missing from the data. Teams had to interpret disagreement rather than simply count overlaps.
- **App task is open-ended.** It needs to connect back to the algorithm (personalization) rather than be a free-standing feature list.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2018A-8554 | Rider experience as five mechanism-based sub-indices (ups-and-downs, vertigo, sustainability, stimulation, nostalgia) | Cluster-based and nearest-neighbour imputation; two-layer entropy weights; preference coefficients | Separate treatment of inverting vs non-inverting coasters; subsample (top-200) robustness check |
| 2018A-8700 | Screened objective indicator system plus a full recommender product | BP neural-network imputation; grey relational screening; entropy weights; ItemCF + tag-based recommender; fuzzy evaluation | Explains disagreement with online lists via radar-chart profiles and geographic/age bias |
| 2018A-8744 | Net experience = Thrill − Discomfort | Regression imputation; Box-Cox + percentile scaling; Borda count for type; logistic discomfort penalties; AHP from own survey | Nonlinear penalty for excessive G-force and inversion frequency, calibrated to tolerance literature |
| 2018A-8874 | Excitement, safety and innovativeness, balancing subjective and objective weights | Physics-based imputation (energy conservation, centripetal force); EM; LFPP fuzzy weights blended with entropy weights | Used the disagreement with online lists to find and fix a modelling artefact (±1 truncation) |
| 2018A-8992 (NCTM) | Two user types → general and personalized models on one base | Linear Iterative Imputation (significance-pruned regressions); grouped linear rating; 3×3 AHP | Variable groups derived from the imputation regressions cut personalization to 3 questions |

All five built a weighted composite score, but they differ in where the weights come from. 8554 and 8700 rely mainly on entropy weights, 8874 blends a survey-based fuzzy method with entropy, 8744 uses an AHP analysis of its own survey, and 8992 assigns group weights by stated reasoning and tests them for sensitivity. Their Top 10s differ markedly: Steel Dragon 2000 leads 8554 and 8744, Kingda Ka leads 8700 and 8992, and 8874's initial list starts with GateKeeper. This shows how much the weighting and the imputation, rather than the data alone, shape an "objective" ranking.

The strongest papers make the model's structure do double duty. 8992's groups come from the imputation analysis and make the app simple. 8744's penalty term gives a substantive reason for its ranking. 8554's sub-indices become the app's "feelings." 8874 stands out for treating the external comparison as a diagnostic, and 8700 for analysing *why* the online lists look the way they do.

## Common ground
- **An explicit data-repair stage before modelling**: missing-value counts reported, different methods for lightly vs heavily missing columns, and correction of mislabeled Type entries (all five).
- **Normalization before aggregation** (min-max, z-score, or Box-Cox percentile), with variables oriented so that larger means better or explicitly penalized.
- **Handling construction and type deliberately**: separate treatment or numeric coding with a stated rationale (data-derived ratio in 8554, multiplier vector in 8700, Borda poll in 8744, regression correction in 8992).
- **Some form of personalization in the app driven by the same scoring formula**: preference coefficients or AHP weights replace the default weights (all five).
- **Comparison with two or more online lists using overlap counts**, plus reasons for differences (subjectivity, US-centred voters, factors excluded by assumption such as price, seat position or location).
- **Strengths/weaknesses or evaluation section** in every paper, and four of five include a sensitivity or robustness check.
- **Code in appendices** (Python in 8700 and 8744, MATLAB in 8874, R in 8992).

## Different routes that all worked
- **Imputation:** similarity donors from clustering (8554), neural network (8700), correlated-pair regression (8744), physics plus EM (8874), significance-pruned regression chain (8992). The physics route (8874) and the statistically documented route (8992) are the most transparent.
- **Weighting philosophy:** fully data-driven (entropy: 8554, 8700), survey-driven (8744), blended (8874), or reasoned group weights with sensitivity (8992).
- **What "good" means:** monotone "more is better" (8554, 8700, 8992), net benefit with a discomfort cost (8744), or multiple criteria including safety and innovativeness where one variable can count both ways (8874).
- **Dimensionality:** two-layer index hierarchies (8554, 8874), indicator screening by grey relational analysis (8700), excluding redundant variables by correlation (8744), and grouping by regression structure (8992).
- **App depth:** compact preference questionnaires (8554, 8992), tiered customization with AHP consistency checks (8744), or full product designs with recommender algorithms, maps, ticketing and social features (8700, 8874).
- **News release style:** fan-facing hype with a Top 10 table (8554, 8700), press-wire format with dateline and quotes (8744, 8874), or a short plain-language note (8992).

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- **Design for distinct user types from the start** (8992, 8744). A default ranking for casual users plus a personalization layer for experts answers both "objective ranking" and "app" requirements with one model.
- **Let data structure justify groupings** (8992, 8554). Grouping correlated variables avoids double-counting, gives sub-scores interpretable names, and keeps preference questions few. 8992's argument that 3 questions replace 28 pairwise comparisons is a model of justifying a modelling choice by usability.
- **Model costs as well as benefits** (8744). When "more" is not always better, a saturating or logistic penalty anchored to cited thresholds makes a ranking more defensible.
- **Use domain physics to fill or check data** (8874). Simple mechanical relations (v = √(2gh), centripetal acceleration) can recover missing fields and flag inconsistent ones.
- **Show your imputation works** (8992, 8700). Report R² or test error, and check that correlations are preserved after imputation.
- **Treat external comparisons as diagnostics** (8874, 8700). If your list disagrees with well-known lists, look for a modelling artefact (8874's truncation) and characterize the other lists' biases (8700's profiles and geography) before concluding.
- **Match the robustness test to the method** (8554, 8992). Entropy weights depend on the sample, so re-run on a subsample; hand-set weights need perturbation tests with the full changed list shown.
- **Score categorical variables from evidence, not arbitrary codes** (8744's Borda count over real polls; 8554's ratio from mean specifications; 8992's regression-based correction).
- **Keep text, formulas and code consistent**, and document any survey that sets weights (questions, sample size, results). Papers that did this (8744's appendix of 62 responses) are easier to trust than those that did not.
