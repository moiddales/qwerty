# 2015B: City Crime and Safety

## The task in brief
The task supplies two weeks of detailed reports and asks for a city safety rating and a mayoral report. A good model must choose severity weights, normalize exposure, handle underreporting and sparse comparison-city data, and explain what a single score means.

## What makes this problem hard
The papers use four different weighting philosophies: fear, cost, sentence severity and logistic calibration. They all clean and group offenses, normalize by population or exposure, benchmark against other cities where possible and add a within-city analysis.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Route and result |
|---|---|
| [2015B-5473](../papers/2015B-5473.md) | fear and cost indices; 22-region danger ratings |
| [2015B-5967](../papers/2015B-5967.md) | portable base score plus richer local score; city benchmarks |
| [2015B-6018](../papers/2015B-6018.md) | sentence-weighted score; FBI z-score; economic and district analysis |
| [2015B-6129](../papers/2015B-6129.md) | 0–100 logistic index; genetic calibration; district model |

## Common ground

- Each paper builds a composite score from offense categories, an exposure denominator and severity weights.
- All four acknowledge that the two-week reports and comparison-city data are incomplete proxies for safety.
- Benchmarking or a reference distribution is used to make the score interpretable.
- The mayoral deliverable links the score to categories, districts or data improvements.

## Different routes that all worked

Fear, cost, legal sentence and logistic calibration are all workable weighting philosophies. The portable-versus-rich local score distinction and the choice of a bounded versus unbounded index are modeling decisions to expose, not hide.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Separate reported incidence from true incidence and state the observation window.
- Publish the crime categories, exposure denominator and weight source.
- Use comparison cities or a reference distribution to make a score interpretable.
- Map districts or beats only after accounting for population and data completeness.

The reusable pattern is a transparent index plus a diagnostic layer. Show how changing weights changes ranks, and label theoretical comparison data. A percentile or 0–100 score is a communication device, not a probability of safety. Pair the city score with categories and locations that a mayor can act on.

## Comparative synthesis

The four cards offer a useful map of what “safety” can mean. Team 5473 builds a fear model from victimization-survey weights and a separate cost approach; it then divides My City into 22 regions and combines crime-category probabilities with fear weights to show local danger (5473, pp. 7–43). Team 5967 prioritizes portability. Its base score uses crimes, victims and severity so that a city with less detailed data can still be compared, while an extended score adds arrest efficiency, proximity to residences and distribution. The paper reports a My City score of 4.4 and uses Minneapolis, Detroit, Seattle and Los Angeles as context, while warning when comparison figures are theoretical (5967, pp. 1–3, 20–31). Team 6018 uses median sentence length as a transparent severity proxy. Its rating for My City is 1370.9268 against an FBI metropolitan mean of 1462.467 and standard deviation 564.8396; the resulting z-score is about −0.162, or safer than about 56.43% of the modeled comparison set (6018, pp. 1–3, 20–45). Team 6129 bounds the score from 0 to 100 with monotone logistic functions, calibrates the functions with a genetic algorithm, and reports a simple-model index of about 43.28; its richer district model is kept separate because other cities do not provide equally detailed offense data (6129, pp. 1–3, 20–45).

The shared ground is not the numerical scale. It is the audit trail: offense definitions, exposure denominator, weight source, benchmark distribution and observation window. A city score should be paired with category-level and district-level diagnostics so a mayor can act. The teams also show why comparison can mislead. A two-week police report is a sample of reported incidents, not a complete measure of victimization, and a score based on detailed local data is not directly comparable to a sparse benchmark score. Sensitivity to fear, cost, sentence or logistic weights should be shown. The most reusable design is therefore a transparent index plus a diagnostic layer and an explicit statement of uncertainty.


## Detailed comparison

The choice of severity weight is the central modeling decision. 5473 asks how afraid residents are and what crimes cost; 6018 uses median sentence as a reproducible proxy; 6129 uses a monotone logistic response so additional crime lowers a bounded index; 5967 keeps a base score portable and adds richer local variables only when available. None of these is automatically correct for every city. A strong report can present a primary score, a sensitivity band from alternative weights and a category profile showing which offenses drive the result.

The comparison also clarifies the role of benchmarking. 5967’s Minneapolis, Detroit, Seattle and Los Angeles anchors make a local score legible, but its own letter warns that theoretical comparison numbers are less reliable. 6018’s FBI mean, standard deviation and z-score give a distributional context, but the normal approximation should be checked. 5473’s 22-region model and 6129’s district model make the result actionable, yet beat populations and visitor exposure may differ. A new analysis should distinguish reported incidents from underlying incidence, state the two-week window, harmonize offense definitions across cities and mark missing arrest or domestic-crime fields. Economic correlation should be described as association. The mayoral product is strongest when it says which category or district merits attention, what data would improve the score and how much a plausible change in rates would move the index (5473, pp. 13–43; 5967, pp. 1–3, 20–31; 6018, pp. 20–45; 6129, pp. 20–45).

A practical safety card should end with an action table: category or district, modeled signal, possible intervention and data gap. That format preserves the useful detail in the four papers while preventing a single composite number from becoming the entire policy.

The score should be accompanied by a short sensitivity table: primary weights, an alternative weighting, resulting rank and the categories that moved it. That small addition is enough to show whether a mayor should act on the number or on the underlying pattern.
