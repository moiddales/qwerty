# 2016B: Shop and Ship

## The task in brief
The company must cover the 48 continental states with one-day UPS ground shipping, then weigh state sales tax and clothing tax. Coverage can mean land, ZIP codes or population, and minimizing warehouses conflicts with taxes and practical road access.

## What makes this problem hard
All teams build a reachability representation, solve or approximate a set-cover problem, then add tax as a second objective. The strongest papers disclose the difference between complete land coverage and useful population coverage and offer alternatives.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Route and result |
|---|---|
| [2016B-6475](../papers/2016B-6475.md) | state coverage matrix; greedy and 0-1 programming; vector maps |
| [2016B-6829](../papers/2016B-6829.md) | map-pixel crawler; greedy approximation; tax-adjusted coverage |
| [2016B-7037](../papers/2016B-7037.md) | population/tax ratios; quartile replacement heuristic |
| [2016B-7057](../papers/2016B-7057.md) | central ZIP and major-road candidates; 25-site inhabited coverage |
| [2016B-7211](../papers/2016B-7211.md) | genetic algorithm; 32-site land cover versus 23-site population cover |

## Common ground

- All teams represent one-day reachability as a finite coverage problem before adding tax.
- Warehouse count, coverage and tax are reported as competing criteria.
- Each paper lists candidate sites or ZIP codes so the recommendation is auditable.
- Population, uninhabited land, roads and clothing tax are handled as explicit modeling choices.

## Different routes that all worked

The papers use state matrices, crawled pixels, ZIP candidates, population/tax ratios and genetic search. Exact optimization is attractive on a small abstraction; greedy or genetic methods become useful when the candidate set is large, but should be benchmarked on reduced cases.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Define coverage units and candidate locations before optimizing.
- Use greedy, integer or genetic methods and report approximation quality.
- Treat tax and clothing as explicit objectives or constraints.
- List warehouse locations, coverage and tax for at least one alternative plan.

For similar facility-location problems, keep a minimum-cover baseline and a tax or cost scenario. Validate map extraction and distinguish area from demand-weighted coverage. Heuristics are appropriate when exact search is infeasible, but report complexity, run variability and the gap to exact solutions on small instances.

## Comparative synthesis

The five papers make different choices about what a warehouse “covers.” Team 6475 reduces the map to a 27-by-27 binary state matrix and combines a greedy search with 0-1 programming; its minimum-count plan uses 26 warehouse locations, with duplicate warehouses in California and Texas, and then supplies alternative tax-aware vector maps (6475, pp. 1–3, 4–27). Team 6829 treats the problem as set cover at ZIP/pixel scale. It documents failed exact approaches, then uses a greedy approximation; it reports 23 warehouses for 95.89% land and 99.6% population coverage, and a tax-adjusted 22-warehouse plan covering 95.26% of land (6829, pp. 1–3, 8–29). Team 7037 selects 28 warehouses for populated coverage and ranks locations with an out-of-state population divided by in-state population times tax ratio, replacing low-quartile sites; clothing tax changes the ratio in a second pass (7037, pp. 1–3, 4–27). Team 7057 chooses central ZIP codes near major roads, removes three sites serving only uninhabited areas, and recommends 25 warehouses while reducing tax liability (7057, pp. 1–3, 4–24). Team 7211 uses a genetic algorithm: 32 sites cover all mapped land, while a 23-site plan covers 95.89% of land and 99.6% of population; tax-focused sets trade coverage for lower tax (7211, pp. 1–2, 4–65).

These results should be compared as different objective choices, not as a single vote on the “correct” number. State matrices are fast but coarse; pixel or ZIP extraction is detailed but vulnerable to map-reading errors; population coverage is often more useful than land coverage but may violate a literal all-land requirement. Greedy and genetic methods are justified when exact set cover is infeasible, but their approximation gap should be checked on small instances. A strong new solution reports warehouse list, coverage unit, population served, tax exposure, uncovered locations and at least one alternative. Clothing should be introduced as a second tax schedule after the baseline coverage is understood.


## Detailed comparison

The first question to settle is what “service the continental United States” means. 6475’s state matrix and 7037’s populated-area plan treat states or populated regions as units. 6829 and 7211 work closer to the UPS map using pixels or ZIP codes. 7057 explicitly removes three locations that would cover uninhabited land, reducing its recommendation to 25 warehouses. These choices explain why warehouse counts range from 22 to 32 without implying that one team made an arithmetic error. A new paper should state whether the requirement is literal all-land coverage, all inhabited ZIPs, or a demand-weighted population threshold.

The second question is how to combine coverage with tax. 6475 offers alternative vector plans; 6829 modifies greedy marginal coverage with tax; 7037 replaces low-quartile ratios; 7057 uses population and road access before tax; 7211 lets a genetic fitness function trade coverage against tax. The best practice is to show a small frontier of alternatives, with warehouse list, coverage unit, population served, sales/apparel tax and construction count. Approximation methods should be checked against exact solutions on a reduced candidate set, and map extraction should be spot-checked because a misread pixel or ZIP can change the set cover. Clothing tax belongs in a second scenario because it can change the preferred state without changing the one-day network. The president’s letter should explain what is being sacrificed when a lower-tax plan covers fewer people or leaves remote land outside the guarantee (6475, pp. 4–27; 6829, pp. 8–29; 7037, pp. 4–27; 7057, pp. 4–24; 7211, pp. 4–65).

For implementation, include a staged build option. A company may prefer a slightly larger tax bill with high population coverage or may build the highest-value sites first. Reporting that sequence makes the optimization useful even when capital or map data change.

A reproducible facility card should include the coverage unit, candidate generation rule, objective weights, final locations and uncovered demand. That is more useful than reporting only the warehouse count.
