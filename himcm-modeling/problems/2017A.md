# 2017A: Drone Clusters as Sky Light Displays

## The task in brief
Teams must place and animate drones for a Ferris wheel, dragon and an original image, then specify launch area, airspace, safety, duration and a mayoral memo. The geometry is only half the problem: transitions, collisions, battery life, audience buffers and regulation determine feasibility.

## What makes this problem hard
The papers represent each display as a point cloud or geometric primitive, define transformations for animation, and then add a transition or packing method. They report drone counts and operational dimensions before discussing costs and legal constraints.

## Common ground

- Displays are represented as sampled geometric points with an explicit coordinate system and spacing rule.
- Animation and transitions are modeled as transformations or matching/path-planning steps.
- Safety, launch area, airspace and duration appear alongside the display geometry.
- A memo to the mayor reports a drone count and operational recommendation.

## Different routes that all worked

Exact bipartite matching, polar-angle grouping and geometric primitives are different answers to the transition problem. Hexagonal launch packing and explicit cost models add operations that a purely visual animation would omit.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Choose a coordinate system, sampling resolution and display orientation.
- Minimize a transition objective such as maximum flight distance.
- Check inter-drone separation, launch packing, battery and audience buffer.
- State airspace and FAA assumptions and separate geometry from regulation.

The reusable pattern is point-cloud choreography: define targets, match them between frames, apply transformations, then test operational constraints. A visually attractive model still needs a safety margin, a collision method and a duration budget. Report sensitivity to point spacing and buffer distance, and treat costs as estimates unless sourced.

## Comparative synthesis

The four drone papers solve different parts of the same choreography problem. Team 7879 traces display contours in MATLAB, assigns 480 drones and minimizes the largest transition distance with bipartite matching plus binary search; it reports 120–125 m display height, a 430 m by 330 m restricted ground area and a show duration of about 329 seconds (7879, pp. 1–4, 6–31). Team 8028 represents launch, translation, rotation and transition as reusable 3-D actions. It improves simple polar-angle sorting by grouping angles and matching within groups by radius, then proposes multilayer or time-delay collision avoidance (8028, pp. 3–5, 8–21). Team 8040 uses explicit geometric primitives: circles, spokes and triangles for a Ferris wheel, a sinusoidal dragon body and parabolic facial features; its MATLAB evaluation uses up to 427 drones and specifies a 9 m by 9 m launch area and 100 m by 50 m by 80 m airspace (8040, pp. 1–3, 4–35). Team 8393 allocates about 400 drones to a Ferris wheel, dragon and solar-system display, uses five-foot air spacing and hexagonal ground packing, and compares purchase and rental costs (8393, pp. 1–3, 4–29).

The common design contract is point-cloud choreography. A display needs a coordinate system and sampling rule; an animation needs a time-indexed transformation; a transition needs a matching/path rule; and an operational plan needs battery, separation, launch, audience and regulatory margins. Exact matching, polar heuristics and simple geometric formulas can all be appropriate if the objective is stated. Safety should be checked along the flight path, not only at the endpoints. A mayoral memo should state drone count, maximum altitude, restricted ground footprint, display and transition times, battery margin, cost basis and the assumptions requiring FAA or vendor confirmation.


## Detailed comparison

The cards also show three scales of drone planning. At the display scale, 8040’s circles, sine curve and parabolas make the image easy to specify; 7879’s contour extraction gives a general point-cloud method; 8393’s planets and explicit Ferris-wheel planes show how color and depth can be designed. At the transition scale, 7879’s bipartite matching seeks a maximum-distance guarantee, while 8028’s polar grouping is a simpler heuristic that can be tested by group size. At the operations scale, 8393’s hexagonal packing, 7879’s audience exclusion zone and 8040’s launch/airspace dimensions show why a beautiful figure is not yet a feasible event.

A new model should state the number of drones needed for each display, the sampling distance, the altitude and orientation, the maximum and total path distance, the transition and display times, and the battery margin. Collision checking should cover intermediate positions and account for a dropped or delayed drone. Regulatory and vendor assumptions should be separated from geometry: FAA altitude, exclusion zones, pilot capacity, rental price and battery life are external constraints. The mayoral memo should give one recommended show plus a contingency that drops a display or reduces resolution if weather or battery margin fails. The four papers make that decision reviewable by putting the drone count, footprint and duration on the first pages and supporting them with coordinate diagrams and code or calculations (7879, pp. 1–4, 22–31; 8028, pp. 8–21; 8040, pp. 1–3, 29–40; 8393, pp. 1–3, 21–29).

A final presentation check is to put one scale bar beside every display diagram. The four cards use different buffers and heights, so a reader needs the same units for point spacing, audience exclusion, launch footprint and airspace before comparing proposals.

The model should include a failure case such as a delayed drone or weather hold. A contingency that reduces resolution or drops one display makes the mayoral recommendation operationally credible.

The cards also support a simple audit: trace one drone from launch to display one, through the first transition, and back to landing. Its maximum distance, separation and battery use should agree with the headline feasibility claim.
