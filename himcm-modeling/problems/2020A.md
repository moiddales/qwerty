# 2020A: The Best Summer Job

## The task in brief
1. List and describe the factors a high school student should consider when choosing a summer job (quantitative or qualitative, constant or variable, deterministic or probabilistic, with units).
2. Build a model or algorithm that evaluates a student's job options, with the student's own situation and preferences as inputs.
3. Test it on at least ten fictional persons with reasonable data; explain how the persons were built and analyze the results.
4. Describe and lay out how the model would be presented to a user (webpage, app, newspaper article); no real app is required.

Format: 25 pages total, including summary sheet, table of contents and references. No data set is provided.

## What makes this problem hard
- **"Best" depends on the person.** The statement asks for a model driven by each student's situation and preferences, so a single global ranking of jobs does not answer it.
- **No data are given.** Teams have to build a job database (types or real postings) and decide which attributes can be measured.
- **Mixed factor types.** The statement names qualitative, variable and probabilistic factors explicitly, so the model has to turn interest, physical activity, risk or overtime into numbers in a defensible way.
- **Money versus recreation.** The statement wants both earnings and time for recreation, which is a built-in trade-off.
- **Testing without ground truth.** There is no "correct" job for a fictional person, so validation has to rely on plausibility, discrimination between personas and sensitivity.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| [2020A-10549](../papers/2020A-10549.md) | Recommendation system that returns a *cluster* of suitable jobs | AHP blended with entropy weights; k-means on scores; age filter | Algorithm that repairs an inconsistent AHP matrix so the user need not re-enter preferences |
| [2020A-10550](../papers/2020A-10550.md) | MCDM with interacting criteria; "best" includes the chance of getting the job | ANP with 4 clusters and 15 criteria; limiting supermatrix; Monte Carlo personas | Real UK postings; sensitivity on both user weights and the modeller's structural weights |
| [2020A-10656](../papers/2020A-10656.md) | Simple, presentable score for a teenage audience | Multiplicative score: 0/1 gates for hard limits, exponential penalties for shortfalls | Every constant argued; BLS/OSHA/child-labor data pipeline; separate model for unpaid internships |
| [2020A-10701](../papers/2020A-10701.md) | Indices computed from both the job and the student | AHP weights; TOPSIS ranking; hierarchical clustering for a diverse shortlist | Net salary after commute and meals; 12-hour day as a recreation budget; expected overtime |
| [2020A-11135](../papers/2020A-11135.md) | Two-level search: job category, then specific posting | Decision-tree filter; AHP with inconsistency pointer; Gaussian ideal-point indices; benefit/cost by hours | Bell-shaped preference for social interaction and physical activity; lexicographic baseline comparison |

All five use a weighted multi-criteria core, yet the papers differ in what surrounds it: how factors are turned into numbers, how infeasible jobs are removed, what the output looks like (one job, a ranking, a cluster, a diverse shortlist) and how the result is checked. The judges rewarded several quite different designs. What they have in common is that each design choice is tied to a stated view of what a student needs.

## Common ground
- **Hard constraints separated from trade-offs.** Age limits, prerequisites or unacceptable risk remove jobs first (decision tree, filter or 0/1 gate); the remaining factors are weighted.
- **User input mapped to model input.** Questionnaires, pairwise comparisons or sliders feed the weights and the personal parameters directly, which answers Requirements 2 and 4 with one design.
- **Personas that differ on purpose.** Ten or more fictional students with contrasting priorities, at least one worked through every step, the rest summarized in a table.
- **Result reading against the persona.** Each recommendation is checked against the persona's story, and surprising results are explained from the index values.
- **A designed presentation page** (app screens, website questionnaire, poster) whose inputs match the model's inputs.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Weighting method:** AHP + entropy (10549), ANP (10550), hand-built multiplicative formula without weights in the usual sense (10656), AHP + TOPSIS (10701), AHP + benefit/cost ratio (11135).
- **Turning factors into numbers:** monotone normalization (most), exponential shortfall penalties (10656), person-specific net values (10701, 11135), ideal-point Gaussian (11135).
- **Probabilistic factors:** expected values for overtime (10701), infection-risk tiers (10656), Monte Carlo for persona generation (10550).
- **Output form:** full ranking (10550), top 10 (10656), top cluster (10549), best job per cluster for diversity (10701), category and specific posting (11135).
- **Job data:** real postings (10550, 11135 A2), government occupation data (10656, 11135 A1), a constructed table (10549, 10701).
- **Validation:** sensitivity on weights or inputs (10550, 10656, 10701, 11135), baseline comparison (11135), consistency repair (10549), discrimination between near-identical personas (10550).

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- **Write down what "best" means for this user** before choosing a method, including things the statement does not say (e.g. the chance of being hired).
- **Gate, then trade off.** Use filters or zero-valued factors for conditions that cannot be compensated, and weights only for the rest.
- **Not every criterion is "more is better".** Use an ideal point or range where a moderate value is preferred.
- **Compute indices from the person and the option together** (net pay after costs, time left for recreation) so personalization is real, not only in the weights.
- **Plan for the real user's mistakes.** Inconsistent pairwise judgments can be detected, pointed out or repaired.
- **Design the output to be useful:** a shortlist, a diverse set or a ranked list with reasons.
- **Validate without ground truth** by persona plausibility, near-identical personas that should differ, weight sensitivity (where does the top choice flip?) and comparison with a naive baseline.
- **Make the interface and the model share scales**, so the presentation is something that could actually be built.
