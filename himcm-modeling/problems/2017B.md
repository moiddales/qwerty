# 2017B: Ski Slope

## The task in brief
The design must provide at least 160 km of trails with approximately 20/40/40 difficulty, then rank the resort against North American sites. Terrain resolution, trail safety, lift capacity and subjective quality all matter.

## What makes this problem hard
The teams extract candidate paths from topography, classify difficulty by slope and additional terrain features, select or score a network, and then benchmark the resort. Some add lift queues or Olympic-readiness weights.

## Common ground

- Terrain data generate candidate slopes before the teams rank or select a final network.
- The 160 km total and approximate 20/40/40 difficulty split are treated as design requirements.
- All papers make ranking criteria and weights visible.
- At least one operational or robustness check supplements the static map.

## Different routes that all worked

The teams use a Trail Quality Index, binary route programming/AHP, or gradient/fall-line GIS with lift queues. These are complementary: a good design can use one route-selection method and a separate stakeholder ranking.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Define buildable terrain and exclude rivers, outparcels and unsafe grades.
- Meet total length and difficulty distribution as explicit constraints.
- Use a transparent quality or resort-ranking index with weights.
- Check path stability with alternative terrain methods and test lift capacity.

For terrain-design problems, separate geometric feasibility from stakeholder ranking. Use multiple path-generation or terrain checks, then report a map and a table of trail totals. A 20/40/40 distribution is a target, not proof of a good resort; include width, curves, grooming, lifts and user flow where data allow.

## Comparative synthesis

Team 8121 emphasizes a Trail Quality Index. It generates hundreds of candidate paths, scores steepness, curve sharpness, width, roughness, directional variation and kinetic-energy loss, then uses selected trails for a resort score of 7.44 and an Olympic-readiness score of 7.8. A skier/lift agent model adds waiting and crowding (8121, pp. 1–3, 4–24). Team 8124 reduces the terrain to candidate smooth nodes, clusters peaks and bowls, and solves a binary program for approximately 20 routes and five lifts. It then applies AHP to size, vertical drop, variety, convenience and quality, checking consistency ratios for its criteria weights (8124, pp. 1–4, 8–26). Team 8201 uses raster gradients, fall-line vector fields and solar aspect to filter safe paths, manually removes river crossings, ranks the resort against North American sites and simulates lift queues in NetLogo. Its watershed analysis provides a second path-generation check (8201, pp. 1–3, 7–24).

The comparison separates design feasibility from stakeholder ranking. A route can meet the 160 km and 20/40/40 requirements yet be poor because it is narrow, hard to groom or badly served by lifts. Conversely, a high resort score cannot repair a map that misses the required trail mix. A reusable workflow is therefore: generate and filter terrain; select a network under length and class constraints; locate lifts and test queues; then rank the resort with visible weights. Terrain resolution, solar exposure, rivers, outparcels and construction feasibility should be reported as assumptions.


## Detailed comparison

The cards illustrate three answers to the tension between terrain fidelity and decision simplicity. 8121 preserves many candidate paths through a Trail Quality Index and then simulates lift queues, so its result speaks to skier experience. 8124 reduces terrain to a 13-by-13 binary node structure and uses 0-1 programming, which makes route selection auditable and compact; its AHP stage makes stakeholder preferences explicit. 8201 keeps richer GIS layers—gradient, fall line and solar aspect—and checks path stability with a second watershed method, then adds lift simulation.

A new design should report both constrained feasibility and quality. The constraint table should show total trail length, beginner/intermediate/difficult kilometers, number and location of lifts, and any excluded land. The quality table should list weights for width, curves, roughness, grooming, solar exposure, vertical drop, convenience or Olympic readiness. A map should distinguish candidate paths from selected buildable trails. Sensitivity should vary terrain resolution, gradient thresholds, lift number and weights; if a trail disappears under a small change, it should not be presented as a certain recommendation. Finally, a resort ranking is comparative and depends on the reference resorts and criteria. The strongest letters state what the proposed resort can claim, what remains to be engineered and how future Olympic facilities could use the unselected land (8121, pp. 4–24; 8124, pp. 8–28; 8201, pp. 7–24).

The final memo should identify uncertainty in the terrain data and reserve land for access, lodging or future events. That keeps a mathematical trail network from being mistaken for a construction-ready master plan.

A compact feasibility table should list total kilometers, class shares, lift count, excluded terrain and ranking score. It gives the reader a direct check on every official requirement.

A ranking score should never substitute for the trail map. The map shows whether a route crosses a river or outparcel; the score shows how the proposed resort compares with other sites. Keeping those outputs separate makes the design easier to review.

For future work, connect selected trails to lift stations and access roads before claiming an Olympic-ready plan. This is where the queue and GIS checks in the cards add value beyond a static difficulty percentage.

That separation helps a reader borrow the route-generation method without inheriting an untested ranking weight, and it keeps the final memo honest about what remains to be engineered.

This preserves the distinction between a modeled comparison and a build permit.

The map, score and operations table should be read together.
