# 2019A: Charge!

## The task in brief
The problem asks teams to explain how free public charging for phones, laptops and electric vehicles is changing, model the resulting costs and impacts, compare public places, recommend cost-reduction initiatives, and write a one-page newspaper article. No common data set is supplied. A successful solution must therefore connect a demand forecast to equipment requirements and then to who pays. The format is a summary sheet, solution and article within the stated page limit; references and appendices follow.

## What makes this problem hard
- “Free” describes the visitor experience, while hosts still pay electricity, equipment, labor, space and queue costs.
- Devices have different mechanisms. An EV occupies a parking charger for a long service time; a phone needs a socket but a user may leave the seat or walk to a kiosk.
- Demand is changing, but ownership, mileage, battery capacity, public charging share and utilization are uncertain.
- A school, café, airport, mall, library and park have different visitor counts, stay distributions, floor areas and commercial incentives.
- The statement asks who pays and asks for initiatives, so a descriptive energy calculation alone is incomplete.
- The article must communicate a multi-variable model without equations or a long methods section.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| [2019A-9564](../papers/2019A-9564.md) | Serve urgent demand and quantify dissatisfaction | Poisson EV queue simulation; battery-flow and impatience indices | Parameterized place model, including mobility and reciprocity |
| [2019A-9824](../papers/2019A-9824.md) | Forecast device growth and account for host cost | Linear/quadratic least-squares trends; cost/profit equations | Break-even attraction and outlet-ratio plots for café, mall and school |
| [2019A-9844](../papers/2019A-9844.md) | Convert ownership and batteries to public energy | Energy accounting; Bass diffusion; queue-time and investment cost | Four representative scenarios and five-year adoption forecast |
| [2019A-10057](../papers/2019A-10057.md) | Treat charging as a welfare problem | Regional energy forecasts; four cost categories; Cobb–Douglas and Lagrange optimization | Monetary, space, security and convenience costs kept separate |
| [2019A-10174](../papers/2019A-10174.md) | Simulate visitors and devices over time | NetLogo agent-based model; destination attendance distributions; EV projection | Outlet-density thresholds and destination-specific time patterns |

The papers share a two-stage structure: estimate energy/demand, then map it to facilities and money. They differ in what “requirement” means. 9824 and 9844 use target ratios and average utilization; 9564 uses dissatisfaction and queue outcomes; 10174 models individual access; 10057 includes space and security externalities. This range is useful evidence that the statement accepts different operational definitions when they are explained.

The numerical outputs show how much the framing matters. Team 9564 gives the decision-maker a service target: its EV example recommends an average dissatisfaction of 0.05, while the personal-device analysis advises keeping the index below 0.2. Team 9824 instead gives break-even levers: in its coffee-shop example, the attraction parameter α must exceed about 0.011 for potential profit to exceed cost, while the mall example becomes beneficial around α = 0.28. Team 9844 validates its Bass forecast against an external 2020 pure-EV estimate of 3.3 million, close to its 3.2 million prediction, and then allocates costs differently by setting: parking fees for EV users, a coffee-price share, an airline-ticket share, or a school budget. These are different answers to “who pays,” yet each is tied to an explicit model variable.

The two computationally richer papers make the service experience visible in different ways. Team 10057 keeps convenience as a negative cost and uses a Cobb–Douglas demographic function with Lagrange optimization to identify a likely customer group; it also tracks space hogging and security separately. Team 10174 simulates individual visitors and devices 100 times for each year and outlet-density setting. Its results identify thresholds of three outlets per 576 square feet for airports and three per 144 square feet for cafés under the tested assumptions, and its 2018 public-EV estimate of 0.618 TWh is close to a 0.57 TWh benchmark. A reader can therefore choose a simple accounting model, an optimization model or an agent simulation based on the decision detail and data available.

## Common ground
- Separate EV charging from small-device charging before writing equations.
- Define inputs with units: device counts, battery capacity, energy per mile, charging power, public share, visitors, area, operating hours and utilization.
- Use historical data and cite sources, then state which values are assumptions or representative scenarios.
- Compare at least two or three public-place types with different scales and incentives.
- Include infrastructure and electricity costs, and explain whether commercial revenue, prices, advertising, taxes or public budgets pay them.
- Show sensitivity or scenario plots so a host can choose a capacity rather than accept an unexplained number.
- Put a readable article near the front and preserve code/data details in appendices.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Forecasts:** linear/quadratic least squares (9824), Bass diffusion (9844), polynomial/regional regression (10057), or direct trend inputs to an agent simulation (10174).
- **Service model:** queue simulation for EVs (9564), battery-flow equations for devices (9564/9844), accounting identities (9824/10057), or NetLogo agents with seats and outlet locations (10174).
- **Cost scope:** equipment/electricity and commercial-profit accounting (9824), investment, land, electricity and queue-time costs (9844), four cost domains including convenience (10057), or place-specific total cost (10174).
- **Behavior:** dissatisfaction and impatience (9564), customer attraction α (9824), demographic targeting (10057), and time-varying attendance (10174).
- **Recommendations:** adjust ratios and prices, advertise to increase sales, build renewable generation, charge for idle vehicles, or reduce outlet density only where access remains adequate.

One useful comparison is how each team validates a recommendation. 9564 repeats its EV simulation 500 times for each station count and displays cost versus dissatisfaction. 9824 varies attraction and outlet-ratio parameters in cost/profit plots. 9844 uses four contrasting sites and a parameter sensitivity table. 10057 checks forecast error against online laptop-sales predictions and perturbs convenience and space variables. 10174 repeats the agent simulation and compares its EV estimate with a known 2018 value. None has a universal ground truth for “enough charging,” so the strongest practice across the set is to expose assumptions, show a benchmark or repeated-run check, and let a host inspect the tradeoff curve.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Make the causal chain explicit: adoption → energy → service requirement → cost → payment or benefit.
- Treat a public place as parameters or agents, not as one national average. If peaks matter, use a time-varying arrival distribution.
- Keep user convenience and host expenditure as separate terms; a policy can lower one and raise the other.
- Use a small worked scenario and then a table or surface for all scenarios.
- Report assumptions beside equations and give readers replacement inputs.
- Explain the article’s headline number using the same model as the technical paper.
