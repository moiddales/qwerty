# 2019B: Bottle Battles

## The task in brief
The problem asks for a model of the positive and negative impacts of banning sales of plain, non-sparkling, non-flavored PET water bottles of one liter or less. Teams must identify data and collection methods, apply the model to Concord and San Francisco, adapt it to an airport, recommend changes to Concord’s bylaw, assess generalizability, and write a newspaper article. The attached FAQ defines the legal scope and enforcement details; it also makes clear that free water may still be provided.

## What makes this problem hard
- A ban removes one product, so the effect depends on what people buy instead. Tap water, large bottles, soda, juice, coffee/tea and alcohol have different prices, health effects, packaging and energy footprints.
- Tap and fountain consumption is usually unmetered. A model needs surveys or a defensible baseline rather than pretending that exact counts are available.
- Concord and San Francisco differ enormously in population, infrastructure and policy scale; an airport adds travelers, security, time pressure and captive demand.
- “Impact” has several dimensions—litter, fossil fuel use, emissions, water access, health, business revenue and enforcement—so a single unexamined score can hide tradeoffs.
- The statement asks for a modified and generalizable policy, which means implementation and exceptions matter as much as arithmetic.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| [2019B-9548](../papers/2019B-9548.md) | Treat the ban as a shock to a competing beverage market | Modified competitive Lotka–Volterra equations; before/after impact formulas | Builds from two to five beverage categories and varies parameters for towns, cities and SFO |
| [2019B-9869](../papers/2019B-9869.md) | Market substitution and utility | Utility/marginal substitution; polynomial surfaces; entropy weights | Explicitly warns that sugar or alcohol substitution can offset environmental gains |
| [2019B-10337](../papers/2019B-10337.md) | Locational fluid shares and lifecycle impacts | Survey-based locational base rate; per-liter energy/waste; impact vectors | Provides city and airport questionnaires for replacing national assumptions |
| [2019B-10407](../papers/2019B-10407.md) | Beverage volume and three-domain impact | Price-adjusted locational rates; environmental/economic/health vectors | Side-by-side Concord, San Francisco and SFO scenarios |

Across the papers, two design choices recur. First, teams estimate pre-ban beverage shares and then model substitution instead of counting only avoided PET bottles. Second, they separate locations: an airport is not simply a larger town. The papers differ in whether impact weights are subjective, entropy-based, or shown as separate vectors. That variation is useful: the statement permits a policy dashboard as well as a scalar ranking.

The team-specific pipelines make the tradeoffs concrete. Team 9548 builds a modified competitive Lotka–Volterra system in stages: two competitors introduce the analogy, three categories give a manageable market, and five categories distinguish small PET water, larger water, other beverages, glass water and tap water. Market shares before and after the ban feed environmental, social, government-budget and individual-impact formulas. Team 9869 uses utility and marginal-substitution curves, then ranks policies with entropy weights. In its airport-based comparison, distributing refillable bottles scores 81.35, a PET tax 70.97, a broad bottled-drink ban 69.01 and a vending-machine ban 31.57. The ranking rewards replacement by direct drinking water and exposes why a narrow ban can perform poorly if consumers switch to sugary or alcoholic drinks.

Teams 10337 and 10407 put more emphasis on measurement. 10337 supplies questionnaires for residents and airport passengers, then combines a locational beverage base rate with per-liter energy, packaging and health terms. Its airport example starts from about 0.51 liters per fluid-consuming passenger per day and approximately 183,000 such passengers. Team 10407 adjusts its mapped rates again for price differences using a prospect-theory-inspired variance function. It reports scalar Ban Overall Impact values of 1,704 for SFO, 874.5 for Concord and 30,410 for San Francisco; the corresponding impact vectors retain negative health components even though the scalar totals are positive. These results are useful as an illustration of a model pipeline, while the cards make clear that the impact scalars and substitution rates are modeling choices.

## Common ground
- State exactly which bottles are banned and which beverages remain legal, using the FAQ.
- Define a baseline beverage mix and a post-ban substitution rule; document surveys, sales data or external studies.
- Include both environmental and adverse outcomes, especially health and packaging from substitutes.
- Treat Concord and San Francisco as case studies with local population, sales and infrastructure inputs.
- Adapt airport assumptions for passengers, water fountains, reusable bottles, prices and security.
- Recommend complementary measures—fountains, reusable containers, public education, enforcement and emergency exceptions.
- Present environmental, economic and health components before aggregating, so decision-makers can see the tradeoff.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Behavior:** utility curves and marginal replacement rates (9869), survey-derived locational base rates (10337), price-adjusted rates and national baselines (10407).
- **Impacts:** entropy-weight comprehensive score (9869), per-liter lifecycle accounting and impact vectors (10337), three-domain vector aggregation (10407), and the additional sustainability structure in 9548.
- **Data plan:** use observed city sales, national consumption, airport passenger counts, surveys, or a mixture. The statement does not require teams to collect every datum, but it rewards a credible plan.
- **Policy scale:** staged promotion from a town to a city or country (9869), airport infrastructure and reuse programs (10337/10407), and targeted bylaw revisions.

The papers also differ in how they turn a model into a policy. 9548 recommends location-specific measures such as fountain fees or beverage taxes in a town, anti-sugar measures in San Francisco, and careful fountain placement at SFO. 9869 recommends staged promotion and evaluates both refillable-bottle distribution and taxes before a full ban. 10337 treats the survey itself as part of implementation: a municipality can replace national rates with local answers and rerun the impact vector. 10407 recommends cold, filtered, easy-to-find fountains and measures that make lower-impact alternatives more convenient. A strong response to this problem therefore includes an adoption plan and an exception policy, rather than presenting the ban as a single switch.

For validation, 9548 compares stable market states and varies the time between announcement and enforcement. 9869 performs an entropy-weight sensitivity analysis; health is the most robust index over roughly a 3–4% range, while environmental protection is the most sensitive. 10337 provides a parameter-by-parameter data-collection plan, and 10407 compares three locations and reports impacts per 1,000 people. These are different forms of evidence, but all help a reader distinguish an observed or surveyed input from a value chosen to make the scenario calculable.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Start from the intervention’s mechanism: what product disappears, what substitutes appear, and how much volume moves.
- Use a location-specific baseline when unmetered tap water or airport behavior matters.
- Keep units per liter and total liters visible; this makes lifecycle calculations auditable.
- Give emergency or inaccessible-water exceptions explicit treatment.
- Use a score only after reporting the component impacts; a weighted sum is a policy choice, not a natural law.
- Let the article lead with one concrete tradeoff, then explain the proposed infrastructure and policy response.
