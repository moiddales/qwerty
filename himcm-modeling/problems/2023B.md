# 2023B: Charging Ahead with E-buses

## The task in brief
1. Evaluate environmental consequences of replacing metropolitan bus fleets with all-electric buses.
2. Model financial consequences and constraints of the transition.
3. Apply the models to a city of choice and then to additional metropolitan areas.
4. Produce a realistic ten-year transition plan, charging strategy and letter or recommendation.

## What makes this problem hard
Environmental benefit depends on manufacturing, fuel/electricity mix, noise and operational mileage, not only tailpipe emissions. Financial feasibility combines purchase, energy, maintenance, funding and revenue over several years. Route replacement creates discrete choices, while chargers require spatial decisions and capacity assumptions. Data are city-specific, future prices and ridership are uncertain, and an apparently good fleet-wide result may hide a poor route plan.

Across the papers, teams first define variables and assumptions, then build a baseline model before adding extensions. They use different mathematical routes but report intermediate quantities, scenario results and sensitivity.

## Common ground
All papers compare diesel and electric technologies across lifecycle or operational measures, then connect the comparison to a fleet transition. They use explicit bus lifetimes, costs and emissions factors, state funding assumptions and provide a multi-year plan. Route or charger decisions are separated from fleet-level totals, and each paper includes some sensitivity or cross-city check.

## Different routes that all worked
13694 combines EWM-TOPSIS ecological ranking with zero-one integer programming for lines and chargers, then simulates Stockholm, Chicago and Shanghai. 13904 uses four Stockholm indicators and a 50%-funding constrained plan. 14087 fits noise and emissions regressions, recursively finances Seattle's fleet, ranks routes by EWM-TOPSIS and clusters chargers with K-means. 14323 uses a twelve-year lifecycle and cost comparison for Washington, D.C., then builds a three-city ten-year schedule and official letter.

## Cross-paper synthesis

### What the comparisons show
The papers differ in the level at which they optimize. Team 13694 uses a lifecycle ecological and financial comparison and then plans fleet replacement across cities. Team 13904 first ranks bus technologies with entropy-weighted TOPSIS, then uses zero-one integer programming to choose Stockholm lines and charging sites under a 50% external-funding constraint. Its ten-year recursion adds system revenue so that the transition can continue after the initial subsidy.

Team 14087 separates fleet-wide impacts from route-level implementation. Logistic, linear, improved logistic and exponential regressions are compared for noise and greenhouse gases using a 90/10 split; the selected CO2 model gives Seattle's full-electric scenario as 6.252×10^10 grams versus 1.416×10^11 grams for the current fleet. A recursive finance model then shows about six years to full conversion with 50% funding and ten years with 20%. EWM-TOPSIS ranks Seattle, San Francisco and New York routes by ridership, length and elevation, while K-means supplies charger centers.

Team 14323 uses a twelve-year and 250,000-mile bus lifetime to make emissions and finance comparable. Its D.C. application reports 27,118,944 kg CO2-equivalent saved and $75,461,591 in long-term savings for a complete transition, then uses projected battery costs to schedule purchases and finish a three-city plan by 2033. The routes differ in mathematical style, but all connect fleet-level totals to an implementation schedule and a decision-maker communication piece.

### Reusable evidence patterns
The most transferable pattern is to separate four layers: lifecycle accounting, city-level fleet finance, route or charger choices, and policy communication. A fleet-wide emissions result cannot determine which line should be converted first; a route ranking cannot prove the city can finance the conversion. The papers that keep these layers linked show funding percentages, bus lifetimes, route criteria and charger assumptions. Cross-city application is valuable when the same equations are reparameterized, while a city-specific result should retain its local data label.

The papers also show why a transition plan needs two kinds of validation. Regression-based 14087 uses a train/test split and error metrics before applying its fleet scenarios. Optimization-based 13904 checks feasibility under funding and charging constraints and transfers the framework across cities. Lifecycle-based 14323 checks consistency by using the same twelve-year lifetime for environmental and financial terms. A reusable solution can combine these: fit impact relationships on held-out observations, verify that the proposed plan satisfies its constraints, and show that cost and emissions use the same service horizon.

A route-level plan also needs to state what happens when chargers, funding or battery prices change. 13904's integer constraints, 14087's funding cases and 14323's projected battery-cost timing provide three ways to expose that dependence. Their letters and summary pages are most useful when they repeat the same emissions, savings and completion-year figures as the model, giving officials a traceable basis for action.

That traceability is essential when environmental and financial objectives pull in different directions.

The strongest papers also distinguish a technology conclusion from a deployment conclusion. Electric buses can rank best on lifecycle indicators while route order still depends on ridership, elevation, distance, charging access and available cash. That distinction is reusable for any infrastructure transition: first establish why the technology is desirable, then show where and when the organization can actually deploy it.

This separation keeps the final recommendation practical and auditable.

This makes the implementation schedule part of the evidence.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Align the bus lifetime across environmental and financial calculations.
- Show both lifecycle totals and route-level priorities.
- Make funding, revenue and replacement timing explicit in the recurrence or optimization.
- Treat charger locations as a spatial decision with stated capacity assumptions.
- Use cross-city application or hold-out data to show what transfers.
