# 2022B: CO2 and Global Warming

## The task in brief
1. Analyze the historical CO2 increase and test the claim about the maximum ten-year rise.
2. Build models to predict CO2 concentration, including the requested future years and 685-ppm question.
3. Model the relationship between CO2 and land-ocean temperature and forecast threshold warming.
4. Discuss drivers, controls and implications in a concise summary or article. No complete future emissions dataset is supplied.

## What makes this problem hard
The headline depends on how 'increase' is defined: moving average, fitted slope, annual step or significance test can disagree. Long-horizon forecasts are highly sensitive to curve family and short histories. CO2 and temperature are correlated but are also driven by omitted forcings and feedbacks. Teams must decide whether to use a direct curve, time-series dependence, an economic/carbon-cycle mechanism or a policy scenario, then expose uncertainty instead of presenting one date as a fact.

Across the papers, teams first define variables and assumptions, then build a baseline model before adding extensions. They use different mathematical routes but report intermediate quantities, scenario results and sensitivity.

## Common ground
All papers recompute the historical trend, compare at least one baseline with a richer model and report future threshold dates. CO2 is linked to temperature through regression, correlation or a joint time series. Assumptions and data sources are listed, and the papers acknowledge extrapolation limits. The best cards show model comparison tables or scenario plots rather than only one fitted line.

## Different routes that all worked
12465 (already carded) uses its own model comparison route. 12646 compares linear, quadratic, exponential, S-type and ARIMA fits with an 80/20 split, then adds radiative and human drivers. 12678 builds a baseline, GDP/carbon-intensity model and changing carbon-sink model, then reruns a policy scenario. 12911 uses Holt/ARI/IMA/ARIMA, ARIMA temperature, VAR(5) and Granger tests. 13010 verifies the step-size claim, compares curves and selects a natural exponential model while warning about its unconstrained horizon.

## Cross-paper synthesis

### What the comparisons show
The five papers differ first in how they define the historical increase. Team 12646 uses ten-year averages, least-squares slopes, t-tests and rank-sum tests before fitting five CO2 forecasts. Team 13010 performs a direct step-size calculation and rejects the supplied 1994–2004 interval in favor of 1993–2003. Team 12465 provides another model-comparison route, while 12678 and 12911 put more emphasis on mechanisms or temporal dependence. This is a useful lesson: the claimed maximum is not meaningful until the statistic and time window are fixed.

The forecast families also answer different questions. 12646 compares linear, quadratic, exponential, S-type and ARIMA(2,2,3) models with an 80/20 split; its exponential model has the best reported RMSE, MAE and MRE, but its 2100 projections still span roughly 442 to 833 ppm across models. 13010 selects a natural exponential curve after considering the preindustrial baseline, while explicitly leaving a future emissions cap outside the data. 12911 uses Holt, ARI, IMA and ARIMA candidates, selects ARI(8,2), and then builds a VAR(5) with Granger tests to model lagged CO2-temperature dynamics.

12678 takes the most policy-oriented route. It nests a trend-only model, a GDP/carbon-intensity model and a changing carbon-sink model, then reruns the same structure under global control policies. 12646 similarly adds radiative and human drivers in a multivariate temperature equation after selecting its CO2 forecast. Across these routes, the papers agree that CO2 and temperature move together but differ in how much physical interpretation they claim.

### Reusable evidence patterns
A strong answer gives a model range, not a single dramatic date. It reports fit and holdout error, shows which forecasts reach 685 ppm, and states whether a threshold is a curve output or a policy scenario. The best sensitivity checks change data size, month, sampling frequency or included greenhouse gases. For communication, 12678's colored policy paths and hotdog analogy, 13010's Scientific Today article, and the summary sheets in 12646 and 12911 all translate technical uncertainty without removing the central numbers.

The papers also differ in how they use sensitivity. 12646 reports the spread of 2100 and 685-ppm dates across five model families. 12911 changes data size, month, sampling frequency and added greenhouse gases. 12678 changes the emissions and sink terms under control policies. 13010 explicitly warns that its exponential fit does not include a data-supported stopping point. These checks complement one another: model-form spread measures structural uncertainty, data perturbations measure sampling sensitivity, and policy scenarios measure conditional outcomes. A future team should label all three rather than calling a single fit robust.

For this problem, a useful comparison table should therefore include the historical statistic, forecast family, training/test design, temperature link and policy interpretation. The result is stronger when the paper reports disagreement openly—for example, the different 2100 concentrations in 12646—or explains why a selected model is preferred despite similar fit, as in 12646 and 12911. The common lesson is disciplined extrapolation: use the supplied data to answer the question, then mark where the model leaves the evidence.

This is why the problem rewards model comparison more than decorative precision.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Define the historical-growth statistic before testing a headline claim.
- Compare functional forms on held-out data where possible; R-squared alone is not enough.
- Report a range of threshold dates when model families disagree.
- Separate predictive association from physical causation and list omitted forcings.
- Make policy scenarios modify a parameter or sink term in the same model used for the baseline.
