# 2024B: Examining the Environmental Impact of High-Powered Computing

## The task in brief
1. **Understand the problem:** describe the scope as the worldwide annual energy consumption of HPC, at full capacity and at average utilization.
2. **Create a model:** a comprehensive model of the carbon emissions from HPC energy use that accounts for how energy is produced (energy mixes).
3. **Apply the model:** (a) explore how it changes with HPC growth, rising energy demand in other sectors, and different energy sources and mixes; (b) give realistic bounds for 2030.
4. **Expand and reflect:** (a) model a rising renewable share and the resulting emission cuts, including a switch to 100% renewables and its challenges; (b) add one other impact area (water, e-waste, resource depletion, land use, air quality, chemicals, socioeconomic, noise, network architecture), explain why it was chosen, and explain how it relates to energy.
5. **Share:** (a) technical and policy recommendations; (b) show how one adopted recommendation enters the model; (c) a one-to-two-page letter to the UN Advisory Board (after its September 2024 report "Governing AI for Humanity") asking for an HPC environmental section in its 2030 goals.
6. **Format:** at most 25 pages including a one-page Summary Sheet, table of contents, solution, letter and references. No data are provided.

Note: the corpus statement lists `HiMCM_Olympic_Data.xlsx` as an attachment. That is the 2024 Problem A file; Problem B has no data attachment.

## What makes this problem hard
- **No direct measurement of "HPC energy".** Global HPC energy is not published as a series. Teams had to choose a proxy:
  - data-centre energy plus crypto mining,
  - bottom-up from machine specifications,
  - or a handful of named facilities.
  Each choice defines what "HPC" means.
- **Full capacity vs average utilization.** Utilization rates and overheads (PUE) are uncertain, and the requirement asks explicitly for both levels.
- **Coupling three moving parts to 2030.** HPC demand growth, efficiency gains and the grid mix all change at once. The growth rate chosen dominates any 2030 number, so "realistic bounds" need ranges, not one value.
- **"Demand from other sectors" is hard to quantify.** Teams turned it into a parameter: a competition factor, or a saturation cap.
- **The extension must connect to energy.** The chosen impact area has to be modeled and also linked back to energy consumption.
- **Policy must enter the model.** Requirement 5b needs a recommendation that maps onto a parameter, constraint or submodel.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Framing / angle | Main models | Distinctive element |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024B-15022 | Top-down: HPC energy = crypto mining + HPC share of data-centre energy; 20 host countries with their own mixes | Logarithmic regression of energy on utilization-adjusted computing power; country-level carbon accounting; fitted exponential + renewable-share time function (MFD-HPC); partial-derivative sensitivity | Baseline cross-checked against the IEA range (289 TWh vs 240–340 TWh); every recommendation written into the model as a term or constraint (caps on carbon, energy, water, e-waste) |
| 2024B-15502 | Bottom-up: societal computing demand (551 EFlops) allocated to TOP500 machines; PUE and AUR as explicit factors | Allocation model; GM(1,1); logistic utilization ODE; ARIMA by generation source; seven-source accounting with load-corrected fossil and life-cycle clean factors; circuit (KCL) model | Every answer as an interval; community "energy rights" via a voltage-drop constraint (<80% access by 2028); island/deep-sea relocation policy |
| 2024B-16226 (NCTM) | Facility case studies (six US/Chinese centres); six-category life-cycle view of HPC energy | Full-load/average formulas; energy structure → carbon accounting → weighted Environmental Impact Index; two-scenario simulation; water-factor model with extended EII | Layered system with a flow chart; carbon–water synergy; the EII reused as a disclosure policy instrument |

The papers differ mainly in **how they estimate the base quantity**. 15022 builds HPC energy from published aggregates and validates it against an agency range. 15502 builds it from machine specifications and spans the uncertainty in PUE and utilization with parameter grids. 16226 works from individual facilities and puts its effort into the breadth of the impact model rather than a global number. Their forecasts follow from these bases. 15022 fits one closed-form time function to its own 2010–2023 series. 15502 chains three specialised forecasts (grey model, saturating ODE, ARIMA) into a calculation model. 16226 runs two contrasting scenarios through every later task.

All three produced 2030 figures of the same order as written: 2.34 Gt (15022), 0.82–1.05 Gt (15502), and "hundreds of millions of tons" under a fossil-heavy scenario (16226). These magnitudes depend heavily on the growth rate each team assumed. The three also differ in what they treat as the decisive driver. 15022 and 15502 conclude that demand growth outpaces decarbonisation. 16226 emphasises that the renewable share bends the curve down.

## Common ground
- **Utilization as a multiplier.** Full capacity vs average utilization is handled with an explicit factor (u, AUR or u_avg), whether in the log fit (15022), the energy formula (15502) or facility formulas (16226).
- **Carbon = Σ energy × mix share × emission factor.** All three used similar fossil factors (coal ≈ 820 g/kWh, gas 450–490, oil 650–720) and treated nuclear and renewables as low but, in two papers, non-zero.
- **The 100% renewable case is computed and then qualified.** Each paper discusses intermittency, storage and infrastructure or cost, and two add grid stability or resource and equity issues.
- **Water appears in two of three extensions** (15022 with e-waste; 16226). Both tie water directly to energy use or the energy mix, which answers the "relation to energy consumption" clause.
- **Recommendations centred on renewable supply and siting**, plus efficiency (cooling, hardware) and policy incentives. Each paper shows the adopted recommendation as a modified share or term.
- **Presentation:** task- or requirement-numbered headings, assumptions with justifications, pipeline or flow diagrams, a strengths/weaknesses section, and a letter that cites the paper's own numbers.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Base estimate:**
  - top-down proxy plus external validation (15022);
  - bottom-up engineering from machine data with parameter grids (15502);
  - facility case studies feeding a broad impact framework (16226).
- **Forecasting:**
  - a single fitted function with sensitivity by partial derivatives (15022);
  - a method-per-driver ensemble (15502);
  - scenario simulation with assumed growth and mix paths (16226).
- **Extension area:**
  - environmental, with water + e-waste (15022) or water + an extended index (16226);
  - socioeconomic, with community energy access via a power-flow model (15502).
  The problem's list of areas left real freedom, and an original choice can be a strength.
- **Impact metric:** tons of CO2 and share of global emissions (15022, 15502), versus a weighted multi-dimensional index (16226; 15022 also builds a log-carbon + water + e-waste index).
- **Policy in the model:**
  - all four recommendations as terms and constraints (15022);
  - a section-by-section "what changes" table (15502);
  - a re-run of the renewable-share scenario with a 60% target (16226).

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- **When the key quantity is unpublished, build it two ways or anchor it.** 15022's check against the IEA range and 15502's bottom-up build show that a baseline needs a stated route and, ideally, an external check. This applies to any "estimate the global footprint of X" task.
- **Report bounds as intervals tied to named parameter ranges.** 15502's PUE × demand and AUR × demand tables turn uncertainty into the answer. This suits any "realistic bounds" requirement.
- **Match the forecasting tool to the behaviour of each driver.** 15502 used a grey model for short, fast series, a saturating ODE for bounded rates, and ARIMA for trend series, and gave a one-line justification for each.
- **Sweep the full policy space, not two points.** 15502's renewable × nuclear heatmap shows every combination and the % reduction at a glance.
- **Choose an extension that runs on your existing outputs.** 15502's grid-access model and 16226's water model are both driven by the same energy forecast and mix. This makes the "how it relates to energy" explanation automatic.
- **Make recommendations model-shaped.** Express each policy as a parameter change, a constraint (15022's caps) or a submodel swap (15502's Table 5) so that Requirement 5b follows directly.
- **Keep one scenario pair through the whole paper and into the letter.** 16226 shows how this gives a coherent story for non-technical readers. 15502's letter shows how to lead with three quantified findings and an equity angle.
- **Caution, from the papers' own weak points:**
  - Apply a growth rate to the quantity it was measured on (15022).
  - Avoid stretching ARIMA or grey models over four data points (15502).
  - Give values for index weights and label generated data (16226).
