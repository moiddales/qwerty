# 2023A: Dandelions: Friend? Foe? Both? Neither?

## The task in brief
1. **Spread model:** a single dandelion in its puffball stage sits adjacent to an open one-hectare plot. Predict the spread of dandelions after 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months.
2. **Climate:** the spread model must incorporate the effects of climatic conditions such as temperate, arid and tropical climates on dandelion growth.
3. **Impact factor:** formulate a model that determines an "impact factor" for invasive species. It must integrate multiple variables, including the plant's characteristics and the nature and extent of the harm it causes.
   - a. Test it by computing the impact factor for dandelions.
   - b. Apply it to two other plant species often considered invasive, and identify the region where each is invasive.
4. **Format:** a PDF of at most 25 pages with a one-page Summary Sheet, table of contents, solution and references. No letter, memo or article is required.
5. **Data:** none is provided. The statement gives a USDA glossary that defines "invasive species" (non-native *and* causing harm) and stresses that "non-native" and "invasive" are not interchangeable. The figures referred to in the statement are two dandelion photos.

## What makes this problem hard
- **Choosing a spatial and temporal mechanism.** Teams must combine wind dispersal (mostly within metres, with rare long jumps), a life cycle of several months before a new puffball forms, and plant mortality. Over 12 months only two to four generations fit, so life-cycle timing matters as much as dispersal.
- **Seasonality and the start date.** The statement does not say when the puffball appears. The result depends heavily on the start month and the hemisphere, so teams had to fix or explore it.
- **Turning climate words into model inputs.** "Temperate, arid, tropical" must become quantities such as temperature series, rainfall, humidity, wind, light or soil properties, with stated effects on germination, growth, survival or dispersal.
- **Geometry of "adjacent".** Teams had to decide where the parent sits, what happens to seeds landing outside the plot, and how wind direction relates to the plot's orientation.
- **Defining "impact factor".** The statement asks for both plant traits and harms, and mentions that dandelions are edible and medicinal. Teams had to decide whether benefits offset harm, whether the factor is species-level or location-specific, and on what scale it is reported.
- **Thin data for Part 2.** There is no standard dataset of impacts, so most inputs are expert-style judgments or proxies, and validation is difficult.

| 2023A-13719 | Data-driven life-cycle simulation at 14 global sites; impact as location-specific invasion risk | Cellular automata; germination curve fitted to lab data; mechanistic wind-dispersal formula; ANP + TOPSIS | Google Earth Engine climate drivers; Part 1 coverage fed into Part 2; train on dandelion, test on two other species |
| 2023A-14076 | Separate "how many" (matrix) from "where" (Monte Carlo); weather acts on dispersal | 4-state Leslie matrix; gamma distance kernel; χ² goodness-of-fit; 7-criterion scoring rubric | Rejected normal kernel shown explicitly; wet/dry-season and prevailing-wind variants; non-invasive control plants |
| 2023A-14140 | Stepwise refinement of a dispersal model; impact on a 0–5 hazard scale | Exponential kernel → Lévy flight; logistic density-dependent mortality; AHP + entropy weights | Mortality from a temperature–precipitation ratio; density paradox explained; blended subjective/objective weights |
| 2023A-14632 | Seed-as-agent model with regional "hyperparameters"; impact as ecological-vs-economic trade-off | Brownian-motion dispersal, exponential death dates, growth indices; Lotka–Volterra; logistic growth; Gordon–Schaefer; Sen welfare | Two-axis impact score and Data-Score-Divide classification; RMSE radar-chart sensitivity (NCTM award) |

All five papers used a **stochastic, individual-level simulation** for Part 1, but they differed in what drives it. 13719 and 13643 tie daily processes to real climate data: reanalysis or satellite series in one case, published monthly or annual statistics in the other. 14632 calibrates index functions to regional inputs. 14140 uses climate averages to set mortality. 14076 keeps the demography deterministic in a Leslie matrix and lets weather act only on the dispersal kernel. Spatial mechanisms ranged from simple distance-and-angle kernels (14076, 13643) to multi-step random walks (14140's Lévy flight, 14632's Brownian motion) and a physics-based dispersal equation (13719).

Part 2 split into three families. Four papers built **weighted multi-criteria indices**: AHP (13643), ANP + TOPSIS (13719), AHP + EWM (14140) and a points rubric with written descriptors (14076). 14632 instead **monetized** benefits and harms and kept ecological and economic scores as separate axes. The verdicts on dandelion differ accordingly. 14076 rates it "moderate". 14140 rates it "not invasive but moderate" (and "not invasive" in a dry climate). 13643 places it mid-high (26th of 226). 14632 calls it "invasive". These are not contradictions. The statement leaves the definition of impact open, and each verdict follows from that team's definition.

## Common ground
- **An explicit life cycle.** Every paper models seed → germination → growth → puffball → new dispersal with durations or rates drawn from cited biology (e.g. roughly 2–4 months from seedling to seed head, and low establishment rates of about 2–4% in 13643, 14076 and 14632), and shows generation "steps" in the population curves.
- **Rare long-distance dispersal.** Several papers calibrate dispersal to the published statistic that about 99.5% of seeds land within 10 m. This is either a gamma or exponential percentile (14076, 13643), an updraft mechanism (13643) or a heavy-tailed walk (14140).
- **Maps at the requested months.** All papers show spatial snapshots, and most also give population or coverage curves, so the 1/2/3/6/12-month requirement is answered visually as well as numerically.
- **Climate via named places or types.** Each paper chose concrete locations or climate classes and stated their parameter values in tables.
- **Plant-by-region pairing in Part 2.** Every paper named the regions where its chosen species are invasive. Several computed region-specific scores (13643 local factor, 13719, 14140).
- **Benefits enter Part 2.** In all five papers edibility or usefulness reduces the impact, either through reversed attribute coding, a reciprocal-benefit criterion, a positive-literature proxy or a dollar benefit.
- **Structured assumptions and self-critique.** Every paper has an assumption–justification list or table and a strengths/weaknesses (or limitations) section.

## Different routes that all worked
- **Deterministic vs stochastic counts.** A Leslie matrix gave exact expected counts by stage (14076), while full simulations gave count distributions with confidence intervals (13643).
- **Climate inputs.** Options included synthetic curves from summary statistics (13643's sinusoid), real daily reanalysis series (13719), regional averages feeding mortality (14140), regional index inputs (14632) and season-dependent dispersal parameters (14076).
- **Where climate acts.** It can act on germination and growth (13719, 14632), on survival (14140), on dormancy and flowering thresholds (13643) or on dispersal alone (14076).
- **Handling the hectare geometry.** Teams used a parent at the edge midpoint (13643, 14140), at a corner (14632), or at the centre of a 1-hectare semicircle with the semicircle's angle treated as a sensitivity variable (13719).
- **Part 2 scope.** One paper ranked a whole database of 226 species (13643). Others studied three plants across 4–14 locations (14140, 13719), or three plants each in a single US state (14632).
- **Weighting philosophy.** Approaches included pure expert judgment (AHP, ANP, rubric points), blending with data-driven entropy weights (14140), and avoiding weights entirely with a two-axis plot (14632).
- **Validation of the index.** Teams used non-invasive controls (14076), a hold-out test against reported invasion sites (13719), distribution statistics across many species (13643) and analytic sensitivity (13643).

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- **Calibrate kernels to published percentiles.** One reported statistic, such as "0.5% beyond 10 m", is enough to pin down a one-parameter distribution. Showing that a naive choice fails (14076's normal with µ ≈ −314 m) makes the final choice convincing.
- **Treat unspecified start conditions as variables.** If the timing is not given, explore it (13643's 12 starting months) or fix it with a stated reason (13719's per-hemisphere year).
- **Derive functional forms from required behaviour.** State the limits a function must have, then derive it (14140's logistic death rate), or fit it to experimental curves (13719's germination model).
- **Link the two parts.** Using Part 1 outputs (coverage, spread distance, growth rate) as Part 2 indicators makes the paper cohesive (13719, 14140, 14632).
- **Validate a constructed index.** Options include negative controls (14076), out-of-sample species (13719) and applying the index to many cases and showing the distribution of scores (13643).
- **Report stochastic outputs properly.** Use many runs, descriptive statistics and confidence intervals, a representative run, and parameter sweeps or Monte Carlo sensitivity with a compact summary graphic (13643, 14632).
- **Make subjective weighting transparent.** Show full matrices, consistency ratios, descriptor tables and raw scores. Papers that omitted raw criterion scores or set thresholds after seeing results are harder to audit.
- **Address the "both" in the problem title.** A harm-only index misses part of the question. Strong papers let benefits reduce impact, or kept harm and benefit visible side by side (14632).
- **Presentation.** Effective devices included workflow and pipeline figures (13719, 14140), month-by-site map grids (13719, 13643), stage-coloured scatter plots (13643, 14076), worked score sums for each species (14076) and a one-line reason for every modeling choice.
