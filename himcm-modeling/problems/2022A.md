# 2022A: The Need for Bees (and not just for honey)

## The task in brief
1. Model the population of a honeybee colony over time.
2. Identify influential parameters and investigate colony collapse or disease effects.
3. Estimate the number and/or placement of hives needed to pollinate a 20-acre parcel.
4. Present a one-page summary and accessible public explanation where useful. The statement supplies basic honeybee lifecycle and foraging facts but leaves data, model scale and pollination definition open.

## What makes this problem hard
The colony is a delayed life-cycle system: eggs, brood and adult roles operate on different timescales. Food is both an input and a feedback state, so low foraging can create further losses. Teams must decide whether to model one aggregate population, worker/drone compartments, or detailed role states. The farm question adds geometry, crop demand and an operational definition of successful pollination. Disease, seasonality, competition and sparse biological data make sensitivity essential.

## Common ground
All four papers make assumptions explicit, define stages or roles, and connect population outputs to the pollination calculation. Each gives a seasonal or scenario result and a sensitivity discussion. They differ in detail but all report a practical hive recommendation, not only a population curve. Tables of variables and a summary sheet are common strengths.

## Different routes that all worked
12600 uses a resource-coupled lifecycle model, reduces its collapse boundary to SCCDM and represents the farm as a weighted graph. 12821 layers an Allee-effect baseline with virus compartments and seasonal fertility/lifespan, then models environmental effects on foraging. 13014 uses separate worker/drone equations, Euler integration and a sinusoidal surrogate, leading to 22 hives. 13405 retains brood, roles and pollen/nectar stores in differential equations and tests optimistic, disease, shortage and competition scenarios; its recommendation is a one-to-two-colony range.

## Cross-paper synthesis

### What the comparisons show
The papers make different choices about the unit of population. Team 12600 keeps eggs, larvae, pupae, hive bees and foragers in a Honeybee Colony Population Model and adds resource storage. Its sensitivity surface is then reduced to a Simplified Colony Collapse Disorder Model, which is useful when a farmer needs a collapse screen rather than a full trajectory. The same paper turns the 20-acre parcel into a weighted graph of 20 nodes, so hive placement and movement are explicit.

Team 12821 uses a more aggregate population equation but adds an Allee effect, a susceptible/infected virus extension and seasonal lifespan and fertility. Its main contribution is a set of extinction and persistence regions: transmission and virus death rates can be read against a critical population rather than only viewed in one simulation. Its pollination section also treats temperature, air pollution and pesticide as factors that change effective foraging.

Team 13014 separates worker and drone equations, integrates them with Euler's method for ten years and fits a sinusoid to the recurring annual pattern. Its pollination calculation is deliberately simple and transparent: flower demand is divided by visits supplied by a hive, with a six-kilometre range. The result is 22 hives, but the broad score curve shows that 20 or 21 may be nearly as effective. Team 13405 keeps the richest internal detail, including brood stages, hive-bee and forager roles and pollen/nectar stores. Its optimistic, disease, shortage and competition scenarios explain why one to two colonies per acre is a range tied to bloom duration and resource competition.

Together, these routes show that the task does not require one canonical bee model. The decision context determines the useful resolution: stage detail for collapse mechanisms, aggregate equations for long-run trends, graph structure for movement, or a resource feedback loop for competition. The common requirement is that every simplification be tied to a measurable output and tested with sensitivity.

### Reusable evidence patterns
A strong bee paper reports both population magnitude and mechanism. 12600's maximum, minimum and mean adult populations provide scale; 12821's critical curves provide a failure threshold; 13014's worker/drone ranges expose composition; 13405's stores and activation functions explain the path to failure. For pollination, teams should state whether the target is flower coverage, visits, survival, or economic cost, because those objectives can produce different hive counts. Public-facing artifacts are also part of the evidence: 12600 makes a blog, 12821 an infographic and website, and 13014 an infographic, each preserving the main model conclusions in simpler language.

The papers also differ in how they turn sensitivity into advice. 12600 varies starting hives and resource stock together, then fits a planar collapse boundary. 12821 varies virus transmission and death rates and reports extinction thresholds. 13014 changes each biological and pollination factor by up to 40%, finding worker lifespan, egg laying and bee range most influential. 13405 changes scenario conditions and uses competition to show that more colonies can reduce survival. These are not interchangeable tests: a local derivative, a two-factor boundary and a scenario comparison answer different decision questions. A reusable card should name the perturbation and the output it changes.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Keep a detailed lifecycle model and a smaller screening model when the decision is collapse versus persistence.
- Carry food or resource states into the population equations so failure has a mechanism.
- State whether hive counts are long-term survival recommendations or short bloom-season stocking.
- Use crop-specific demand, range and visit assumptions, and show sensitivity to the range.
- Report seasonal timing and initial conditions because they can change the answer.
