# 2021B: Tackling the Drought

## The task in brief
1. **Lake Mead volume.**
   (a) Identify the factors behind inflow, outflow and loss, and discuss how they relate and how much each influences volume and water level.
   (b) Explain how you would verify the elevation–area–volume relationships in Table 1 (four rows from 895 to 1229 ft) for an irregular lake: what data you would need and what mathematics you would use. Obtaining the data is *not* required.
2. **Lake Mead water level**, using two attached files: monthly end-of-month elevations 1935–2021, and annual high/low elevations.
   (a) Describe the historical patterns, define drought criteria, date the drought periods, and compare the most recent drought with earlier ones.
   (b) Build two models of level as a function of year and predict 2025, 2030 and 2050. Model 1 uses only the most recent drought period and assumes its pattern continues. Model 2 uses 2005–2020 data. Compare and evaluate the two.
3. Using the predictions, address future water demand and whether recycled wastewater could cover all or part of any shortfall.
   (a) Identify the factors of a recycling plan, including local leaders' decisions and priorities.
   (b) Describe the plan and how its impact would be measured.
4. Write a one-page non-technical news article.

Format: a PDF of at most 25 pages including the summary sheet, table of contents, solution, article and references (appendices count). Both Excel attachments are in the repository.

## What makes this problem hard
- **Part 1 has almost no data.** Only four elevation–area–volume rows are given, and the inflow/outflow/loss factors must be discussed without supplied flows. Teams had to decide whether to describe measurement procedures, bring in external budget figures, or fit models to four points.
- **"Drought" is not defined.** Teams had to choose between a level threshold, a trend or rate rule, or a storage-based index, and justify the choice. The choice then decides what "the most recent drought period" is for Model 1. That window can be months or nearly two decades, and the forecast depends heavily on it.
- **Long-horizon extrapolation from a short window.** Forecasting 2050 from the recent drought invites extreme results. The paper has to show it understands that the two models' disagreement is a consequence of the assumptions.
- **Seasonality versus trend** in monthly data.
- **Linking Part 2 to Part 3.** The statement asks whether recycling could make up the shortfall, which calls for some quantitative connection between the forecasts and the size of the plan.
- **Breadth under a page limit.** Seven sub-tasks plus an article must fit in 25 pages.

The papers split mainly on **where they spent their quantitative effort**. 11576 put numbers into Part 1a (an actual budget) and Part 3 (an optimization with costs). 11552 and 12384 treated Part 1 as a measurement-design question, consistent with the statement's note that data need not be obtained. 11552 used physics formulas, and 12384 used survey practice and a published lake model. 11823 and 11898 each did a small quantitative check of Table 1 using the frustum formula. In Part 2 all five used time-series ideas, but the model *pairs* differed a lot. Some used the same method on two windows (11552, 12384 and largely 11823). Others used a different method for each window (11576: plain ARIMA vs a seasonal decomposition; 11898: weighted moving average vs adaptive filtering with a sine correction). The second route gives a richer "compare and evaluate" discussion.

Drought definitions show the widest creativity. They include a policy-based threshold (11576, 1115 ft), a news-sourced threshold combined with a short-term decline test (11898), an adaptive band on the moving average (11552), a storage-deviation index with severity classes (11823), and a year-over-year change rule calibrated on a reference cycle with a minimum duration (12384). The current drought therefore starts in 2002, 2004, 2013, May 2020 or April 2021, depending on the paper. Model 1's 2050 forecast ranges from 1082 ft (11552, flat) to 734 ft (11898).

## Common ground
- **The flows as a balance equation.** Every paper writes volume change as inflow − outflow − loss and decomposes each term (11552 p. 6, 11576 p. 9, 11823 p. 8, 11898 pp. 6–9, 12384 p. 8).
- **Frustum or slice geometry for Part 1b.** Four of five use frustums or column sums, and 11576 integrates a fitted area function. Most compare against Table 1.
- **Explicit, citable drought criteria**, each followed by a dated list or chart of drought periods and a comparison along at least one dimension (duration, depth, frequency).
- **Time-series forecasting for Part 2b**, with monthly or annual values reported for 2025/2030/2050 (11823 reports them only graphically). All predict decline under at least one model.
- **The article at the front.** Every paper places its one-page article immediately after the summary or table of contents, as a designed poster or magazine page (11576, 11823, 12384) or as "Section 1" (11552, 11898).
- **Assumptions with justifications**, and a strengths/weaknesses or model-evaluation section.
- **A defined metric for Part 3b**: RR = recycled/demand (11552), facility capacity and budget (11576), Score = recycled/gap (11823), environmental/economic/social indicators (11898), a program-metric dashboard (12384).

## Different routes that all worked
- **Part 1a:** qualitative factor taxonomy (11823, 12384), physics formulas for each flow (11552), real budget magnitudes and correlations (11576), or a regression of level on weather (11898).
- **Part 1b:** method description with no fit (12384), a regression on Table 1 (11552), a curve fit through an integral (11576), or a frustum error check (11823, 11898).
- **Drought definition:** absolute threshold, trend or band, storage index, or rate of change. Some tie it to policy (11576), others to statistics (11552, 12384).
- **Model 1 window:** from 6 months (11898) to 3 years (11552) to the whole post-2004 or 2013 period (11823, 11576). Each team's own definition decided its window.
- **Forecast methods:** ARMA/ARIMA (11552, 11576, 11823), seasonal decomposition (11576), weighted moving average and adaptive filtering (11898), regression with a sinusoid on transformed data (12384).
- **Part 3 style:** optimization (11576, 11823), multi-criteria weighting (11898), policy and governance design with evaluation metrics (12384), or a policy list with an index (11552).

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- **Make the event definition defensible and test it.** Anchor a threshold in an official operating rule (11576) or a recognized definition (11898, 12384). Or, if the rule has tuning parameters, show how results change as they vary (11552's window and band-width grid).
- **Profile events on several dimensions.** Duration, mean depth and volatility in one table (11576) or trend, trough and duration (11823) answer "compare" far better than a single sentence.
- **Turn a factor list into numbers when you can.** One budget table with a "ft per year" translation (11576) gives later sections and the article a shared anchor.
- **Choose model pairs that differ in method, not only in window**, then compare them on accuracy, data needs and behavior (11898, 11576). Report hold-out or back-test errors where possible (11898, 11823).
- **Watch long extrapolations.** Restore seasonality explicitly (11898's sine factor, 11576's seasonal term). Compare far-future values with physical limits such as dead pool (11898). State when the training window lacks a full cycle (12384).
- **Connect forecasts to the plan.** Using the predicted level to size required capacity, then solving for an affordable phased build-out (11576), turns the plan into an answer about "how much of the shortfall". Even a simple ratio index (11552, 11823) helps if it is computed.
- **Measure impact with metrics practitioners use**: water-quality standards, share of supply, growth, satisfaction (12384).
- **Design the article.** Lead with one memorable number (12 ft/yr; "up to 200 feet by 2050"; 2050 level versus dead storage) and a short list of recommendations.
