# 2015A: Preventing Road Rage

## The task in brief
The problem asks what drivers should do in two-to-one and three-lane closures at two speed limits, and how signs and education can encourage fair and efficient behavior. The challenge is that throughput, perceived fairness, safe gaps and road rage can point to different actions.

## What makes this problem hard
All teams make driver behavior explicit through safe-gap, speed, lane-choice or agent rules. They compare early and late merging, define efficiency and fairness before scoring, and finish with implementable DMV or transportation guidance.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Route and result |
|---|---|
| [2015A-6057](../papers/2015A-6057.md) | two analytical merge models; late zipper at about 300 ft; three-lane caveat |
| [2015A-6080](../papers/2015A-6080.md) | Newtonian model plus NetLogo; Rawlsian/utilitarian metrics; one-mile sign |
| [2015A-6083](../papers/2015A-6083.md) | late merge plus detector-driven speed harmonization |
| [2015A-6117](../papers/2015A-6117.md) | Monte Carlo Java model; congestion-balancing driver; 8%/10% reported gains |

## Common ground

- Driver rules, safe gaps and speeds are specified before comparing early, late or feedback merging.
- Efficiency and fairness are defined as separate measurable outcomes.
- Two-to-one, three-lane and 35/65 mph cases are treated as distinct regimes.
- Every team ends with signs, education or policy guidance rather than a purely theoretical optimum.

## Different routes that all worked

The teams disagree on early versus late merging because they optimize different behavioral metrics and assumptions. Their methods range from closed-form spacing to NetLogo and Monte Carlo feedback; the choice should follow the available traffic observations and policy objective.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems

- Define safety, efficiency and fairness as measurable quantities.
- Model both lanes and the behavior of drivers who do not merge.
- Test two-to-one, three-to-two, three-to-one and 35/65 mph cases separately.
- Translate the result into signs, education and a pilot policy with limits.

Borrow the decomposition: microscopic vehicle rules generate flow, while a policy layer chooses signs or speed limits. Report the baseline and the objective weights, and test heterogeneous drivers. A “zipper” or “early” label is not enough; show where the merge happens, how safe gaps are enforced and whether the recommendation reduces total delay as well as unequal delay.

## Comparative synthesis

The four papers are valuable precisely because their headline policies differ. Team 6057 compares an extreme two-lane case with a more detailed merge model and recommends a late zipper at roughly 300 feet before a 65 mph closure; its 35 mph calculation places the comparable point about 168 feet away. For three-to-one closures it presents an ideal sequence but admits that real drivers will not execute it exactly (6057, pp. 1–2, 5–27). Team 6080 combines a Newtonian gap model with NetLogo agents, then evaluates fairness with Rawlsian and rule-utilitarian ideas. Its recommendation is early, safe merging with a sign about one mile from the closure in its tested scenarios, and it adds a probability-density method for choosing a sign distance when n lanes and o lanes remain open (6080, pp. 1–2, 18–29). Team 6083 recommends later merging and adds speed harmonization: detectors estimate congestion over a half-mile section and an electronic limit responds to the measurement (6083, pp. 1–3, 15–22). Team 6117 uses a Java Monte Carlo model and a modified Darcy–Weisbach analogy, allowing a test driver to move toward less congested lanes; it reports an 8% individual-time reduction and 10% aggregate-anger reduction for its congestion-balancing strategy (6117, pp. 1–2, 30–80).

The apparent disagreement is a modeling question to resolve, not a reason to copy one slogan. Early and late merging can exchange throughput, speed variance, perceived fairness and the location of the queue. A new paper should define its baseline demand, vehicle dimensions, reaction time, safe gap and closure geometry, then show which objective is being optimized. It should model the drivers in the open lane as well as the drivers in the ending lane. A practical policy should also be simpler than the optimizer: a sign, a speed-control rule or a zipper instruction must be understandable under stress. The strongest cards connect a microscopic rule to a system metric and then to a DMV or transportation recommendation, while acknowledging where a simulation needs field testing.


## Detailed comparison

The cards support a careful comparison of policy granularity. 6057 gives a distance rule for a driver and a warning against designing three lanes into one at once. 6080 gives a sign-placement model that can be applied to arbitrary n and o, but its one-mile value is tied to the sample highway. 6083 adds a feedback controller that requires detectors, a sampling interval and a rule for noisy counts. 6117 gives an adaptive individual strategy rather than one fixed merge distance. These are different deployment levels: driver instruction, static signage, active traffic control and local adaptive behavior. A new paper should say which level it can support with available data.

Fairness also needs a definition. Equal merge turns, equal opportunity to use both lanes, equal delay and low speed dispersion are not identical. The papers use different proxies, so a comparison table should report each metric separately before any weighted score. For a three-to-one closure, it is especially important to distinguish an ideal schedule from a policy ordinary drivers can follow. A field-ready recommendation could combine an early warning sign, a speed harmonization zone, an explicit alternating merge instruction and a safe-gap rule. Validation should vary demand, compliance, reaction time, vehicle length and heavy-vehicle share. The model should report queue location as well as total time: a policy that moves the queue upstream may improve the closure point while worsening the approach. These details turn a contest model into a testable transportation intervention (6057, pp. 5–27; 6080, pp. 18–29; 6083, pp. 15–22; 6117, pp. 30–80).

The papers also suggest a reporting minimum: show a diagram of the closure, one worked vehicle trajectory or queue calculation, and the metric values for each policy. That evidence lets a reader judge whether a recommendation is robust or merely a consequence of one assumed compliance rule.
