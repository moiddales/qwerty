# 2024A: To Play or not to Play: Modeling Future Olympic Games

## The task in brief
1. Define quantitative indicators for the IOC's criteria and score sports, disciplines and events (SDEs).
2. Build a model to rank or classify SDEs for inclusion or removal.
3. Test the model against known Olympic decisions and apply it to Brisbane 2032 and later Games.
4. Analyze influential factors and provide recommendations or a letter to the IOC.

## What makes this problem hard
The six IOC criteria are broad and partly qualitative. Popularity, equity, sustainability, inclusivity, innovation, safety and economic value have different units, missing data and possible double-counting. A ranking can change with normalization or weights, while historical inclusion also contains inertia and host-specific politics. Future candidate data must be estimated. A credible paper therefore needs transparent indicators, benefit/cost direction, validation against known Games and sensitivity to weights and thresholds.

## How the Outstanding teams approached it
| Paper | Main route |
|---|---|

| [2024A-15041](../papers/2024A-15041.md) | Hierarchical AHP-TOPSIS over 27 factors, historical validation and host adaptation |
| [2024A-15504](../papers/2024A-15504.md) | Logistic inclusion probability with base score, inertia and bias correction |
| [2024A-15926](../papers/2024A-15926.md) | 21-factor entropy/AHP TOPSIS, 0.45 threshold and interaction sensitivity |
| [2024A-16159](../papers/2024A-16159.md) | Min-max normalization, correlation checks, entropy weighting and sport ranking |

Across the papers, teams first define variables and assumptions, then build a baseline model before adding extensions. They use different mathematical routes but report intermediate quantities, scenario results and sensitivity.

## Common ground
All five papers turn IOC language into a table of indicators, normalize or score those indicators, and produce a comparative ranking or inclusion score. They test established and newer sports, make a Brisbane recommendation and discuss influential factors. Each uses a summary sheet or letter to translate the model into an IOC decision context.

## Different routes that all worked
14995 combines AHP, entropy, coefficient-of-variation and CRITIC weights with MC-TOPSIS. 15041 uses a hierarchical AHP-TOPSIS over 27 factors and validates against Tokyo, Paris and LA decisions. 15504 treats inclusion as logistic classification with a base score, historical inertia and bias correction. 15926 averages entropy-TOPSIS and AHP-TOPSIS, sets a 0.45 simulated threshold and performs interaction sensitivity. 16159 uses min-max normalization, correlation checks and entropy weights for a compact objective ranking.

## Cross-paper synthesis

### What the comparisons show
The five papers illustrate a spectrum from weighted ranking to probabilistic classification. Team 14995 scores 48 events on seven indicators, combines AHP, entropy, coefficient-of-variation and CRITIC weights, and uses MC-TOPSIS to show how ranks move under simulated uncertainty. Its ordinary top three are athletics, football and basketball; the simulated ranking puts football first.

Team 15041 builds a hierarchy with six IOC criteria and 27 factors, applies AHP at the top level and TOPSIS within and across criteria, and tests eleven sports against Tokyo, Paris and Los Angeles decisions. Karate's changing score across hosts demonstrates why host context matters. Team 15504 instead fits a logistic inclusion probability with a base IOC-value score, historical inertia and a bias correction. This separates current appeal from the persistence of already-established sports and reproduces continuous, added and removed patterns.

Team 15926 identifies 21 factors, averages entropy-TOPSIS and AHP-TOPSIS scores with a 0.6/0.4 blend, and sets a 0.45 threshold from simulated scores. Its test set includes sport climbing, skateboarding, surfing, swimming, gymnastics and fencing; future candidates include Teqball, League of Legends, Field Handball, Baseball and Squash. Team 16159 uses a smaller objective pipeline: factor scoring, min-max normalization, correlation checks, entropy weights and sensitivity. It is especially clear about making the matrix replaceable.

### Reusable evidence patterns
Across the papers, a ranking is persuasive only when the indicator table, benefit/cost direction, normalization and weights are visible. Historical validation helps, but it cannot prove that an IOC decision is caused by the modeled factors. Sensitivity should cover weights and thresholds as well as raw indicators; 15926's factor and interaction plots, 15041's ±10% and ±50% weight tests, and 14995's Monte Carlo simulations show complementary routes. The letters and summary sheets are strongest when they repeat the same candidate ordering and scores as the technical sections.

The papers make different claims about what validation can establish. 15041 compares rankings with known Olympic programs and therefore tests whether its criteria reproduce past choices. 15504 tests categorical inclusion patterns, which fits its logistic formulation. 14995 tests ranking volatility with Monte Carlo rather than historical outcomes alone. 15926 and 16159 emphasize threshold and factor sensitivity. None can prove what the IOC will choose, because host politics and future data are unobserved. The reusable practice is to state whether validation checks fit, rank stability, historical resemblance or decision feasibility, then avoid using one kind of check as evidence for another.

A concise IOC recommendation should therefore name the candidate, the score or rank, the key criteria driving it and the sensitivity margin. The cards show several ways to do this: 14995 gives event scores and Monte Carlo order, 15041 gives host-specific historical comparisons, 15504 gives a probability with inertia, 15926 gives a threshold crossing and 16159 gives an entropy-weighted ranking. Together they make the problem a lesson in decision transparency, not just sport prediction.

These distinctions help reviewers judge the recommendation on evidence, not presentation alone.

A ranking should also expose whether the top candidate wins by broad strength or by one exceptional indicator. The factor tables and sensitivity plots in these cards make that distinction visible.

## Ideas worth borrowing for similar problems
- Define indicators and their benefit/cost direction before viewing rankings.
- Validate against known historical decisions, while recognizing that validation cannot prove future policy.
- Report the effect of alternative weighting, thresholds and host-specific values.
- Keep historical inertia separate from current popularity or IOC-value scores.
- Present both the ranking and the data/weight choices that produce it.
