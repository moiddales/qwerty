# 2019B-10407: The Water Bottle Battle

## At a glance
Team 10407 begins with a simple question: what do people drink when plain single-serving PET water disappears? It estimates a national base rate for bottled water, tap, dairy, juice, coffee, tea, soda and alcohol, then updates the rate for a location using daily fluid volume, beverage prices and local conditions. A three-component impact vector assigns each beverage environmental, economic and health scores per liter. Multiplying those scores by the change in volume after a ban produces an overall impact for Concord, San Francisco and San Francisco Airport. Its scalar Ban Overall Impact is 1,704 for SFO, 874.5 for Concord and 30,410 for San Francisco (with 47, 46 and 34 per 1,000 people); the vector components still show a negative health component in each case (pp. 19–22).

## How they read the problem
The authors separate consumption estimation from impact assessment. They emphasize that tap and fountain volume is hard to observe, so national base rates or surveys are needed (pp. 7–9). An airport is modeled as a distinct location because travelers have different prices, time and access. The paper also treats impacts as multidimensional rather than declaring a ban good or bad from one environmental number. This allows a reader to see a policy’s tradeoff between environmental improvement and health or economic cost.

## Models and methods
The first model estimates fluid intake. USDA and public-health data provide national beverage amounts; bottled and tap water are separated with external rates. Because taps and fountains do not meter use, the paper proposes surveys and a “locational base rate” that adjusts the national mix to an airport or city (pp. 7–9). Beverage prices then modify the rate: people are less likely to replace a low-cost bottled-water liter with a much more expensive drink, so a price-adjusted rate is calculated (pp. 11–14).

The benefit/damage model assigns each beverage a row vector of environmental, economic and health scores. PET water receives a positive economic score for its industry but environmental and health terms are treated separately; soda and alcohol receive negative health values based on cited evidence. Volume change after a ban is multiplied by the vector, and vector components are summed to form a scalar overall impact (pp. 15–19). The approach is intentionally transparent: a reader can change a per-liter score or substitution volume and see the effect.

## Data
Inputs include national intake, beverage prices, San Francisco Airport’s approximately 183,000 daily fluid-consuming passengers, PET lifecycle information, sugar-production energy and public-health studies (pp. 7–14, 21–24). Concord and San Francisco population/ban information supplies case context. The paper lists references and gives an appendix of energy requirements and transport forms, allowing a municipality to substitute local values.

## Assumptions
A fluid-drinking day is 16 hours and total fluid volume remains fixed; the average SSPET is 0.5 L; beverage prices and impact scalars are representative; and behavior responds mainly to availability and price (pp. 5–6, 12–14). Collection/disposal costs are held constant and non-PET single-serving water is omitted from the base case (p. 22). The authors identify these assumptions as limitations and explain how future surveys or bottle technologies would change them.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The model reports mapped and updated locational base rates, then applies the same calculations to three locations so differences can be traced to inputs (pp. 11–14, 18–21). A UC Berkeley beverage-tax study is used to calibrate changes in soda and water consumption (p. 14). The final comparison table places Concord, San Francisco and SFO side by side. The limitations section tests the conceptual boundaries—non-PET bottles, fluid volume and airport behavior—rather than claiming universal precision (p. 22).

## What makes it stand out
- A locational correction makes invisible tap/fountain consumption measurable through surveys and local rates.
- Cost-adjusted substitution acknowledges that a ban changes both availability and spending.
- Impact vectors preserve three policy objectives until the final step, avoiding a premature single score.
- Lifecycle energy and health evidence make the recommendation broader than litter reduction.
- The same table structure works for a town, large city and airport.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
A colorful newspaper-style page precedes the technical paper and uses a thirsty-person scenario to introduce the debate (p. 3). The main text has an explicit assumptions section, then models beverage consumption before impacts, so every later number has a defined base. Tables 1–3 document beverage rates, costs and impact vectors; the final location table gives a compact comparison (pp. 10–21). The recommendations section translates model terms into actions such as fountains and education (p. 20), while the limitations page signals what should be measured next.

## Notes for reuse
- Treat overall impact as a policy choice: report environmental, health and economic components separately before aggregating.
- Measure tap/fountain use locally; unmetered water can dominate the result if national rates are copied without calibration (pp. 7–9).
