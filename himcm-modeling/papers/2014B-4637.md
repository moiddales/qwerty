# 2014B-4637: Modeling an Unknown Epidemic

## At a glance
The team combines SIR compartments with a rumor-spreading probability to simulate an unknown disease in a 300-person village. Repeated runs over uncertain latent period, contact, recovery, and mortality parameters classify severity and containment, then compare isolation, treatment, and travel restrictions. A revised model incorporates the field team’s contact-transmission and age-risk findings. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They interpret disease spread as person-to-person interaction, explicitly separating susceptible, infected, recovered and dead people and allowing a latent stage. Because key parameters are missing, they prefer ranges and repeated simulations over one forecast. The additional information triggers an age-stratified model and a new infected researcher in the capital. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Difference equations update compartments daily. A rumor model supplies the chance an infected person transmits to a susceptible contact. Java simulation samples parameters and runs until infections reach zero; summary statistics classify outbreaks. Intervention scenarios alter contact rates, isolation, treatment, and movement.

## Data
The paper uses prompt village counts, Ebola/public-health literature and assumed ranges. The input is the village case supplied by the prompt. Teams supplement it with Ebola or disease-transmission references, assumed transition probabilities, GDP/distance data, vaccine efficacy or sentencing-style resource proxies as appropriate. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes a closed or nearly closed village during one modeled run, homogeneous contacts unless age groups are introduced, and fixed or sampled transition probabilities. Latent infection, contagiousness, mortality and intervention effects are explicit states or parameters. The teams state that sparse field data make these assumptions provisional (pp. 5–9). Its central choice is SIR/SEIR compartments, supported by rumor-spreading infection probability; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The paper reports distributions across many runs rather than only a single trajectory (pp. 9–21). It analyzes how latent period, contact probability, recovery and mortality alter peak cases and duration, then reruns the system with the added information (pp. 22–31). Strengths and limitations are discussed before recommendations.

## What makes it stand out
The uncertainty-first workflow fits the sparse evidence. The rumor analogy gives a concrete mechanism for a transmission probability, while the SIR backbone remains recognizable to a public-health reader. Recommendations are tied to simulated thresholds and explained in plain language.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
A long contents page and numbered subsections make the 55-page paper auditable. Figures of trajectories, parameter sweeps, recommendations, and a news synopsis translate the simulation (pp. 3–4, 18–31, 40–43).

## Notes for reuse
- The “unknown disease” parameters are assumed rather than estimated from case data. An imitator should distinguish model uncertainty from Monte Carlo sampling error and calibrate to observed incidence before making operational claims.
