# 2024A-14995: Olympic event selection with combined weighting and Monte Carlo TOPSIS

## At a glance
The paper scores 48 Olympic events on seven IOC-aligned indicators. A combined weighting method joins AHP, entropy and coefficient-of-variation information, then CRITIC supplies final coefficients. MC-TOPSIS repeats the ranking under simulated weights to test volatility. Football, athletics and basketball lead the robust ranking; the team evaluates karate, climbing and offshore rowing and recommends cricket, martial arts and squash for 2032, with esports and ultimate frisbee as longer-term candidates (pp. 1, 5–21).

## How they read the problem
The team interprets the five tasks as a single ranking pipeline. It defines seven indicators from IOC priorities, scores 48 events, combines three objective/subjective weighting ideas, and then adds Monte Carlo perturbations to test ranking volatility. Three newly added or removed SDEs and six established categories serve as application checks before the 2032 recommendation (pp. 4–21).

## Models and methods
Indicator values are normalized before the Combined Weighting Method joins AHP judgments with entropy and coefficient-of-variation information. CRITIC supplies the final coefficients using contrast and conflict among indicators. TOPSIS measures distance from positive and negative ideal events. MC-TOPSIS repeats the calculation under simulated inputs, so a sport's rank reflects both central score and volatility (pp. 9–16).

## Data
The 48-event matrix combines IOC criteria with event popularity, participation, gender, sustainability, safety and economic data gathered from cited sources. The selected-event test includes karate, sport climbing, offshore rowing, track and field, shooting, basketball, gymnastics, boxing and aquatic sports (pp. 5–8, 14–17).

## Assumptions
The seven indicators are treated as sufficient proxies for IOC selection, and benefit/cost directions are fixed before TOPSIS. Weighting methods are combined rather than choosing one as objectively correct. Monte Carlo draws represent uncertainty in scores and weights; candidate-event values are estimated when future data are unavailable (pp. 4–9, 14–18).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The paper compares the initial combined ranking with MC-TOPSIS and reports changed top-three order, showing why volatility matters. It applies the model to both newer/removed events and stable traditional categories, then uses repeated simulations to test ranking robustness (pp. 14–21).

## What makes it stand out
The paper makes uncertainty part of the ranking through MC-TOPSIS instead of presenting one deterministic table. Its three weighting families also make the subjective/objective balance visible. Testing both recent and established sports supports the claim that the pipeline can distinguish novelty from durable Olympic appeal.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary sheet gives the seven indicators, both top-three lists and the 2032 recommendation. The paper uses indicator tables, weighting diagrams, ranking comparisons and a final recommendation section so the reader can follow the path from IOC values to sport names (pp. 1, 5–21).

The application set is designed to test both novelty and continuity. Karate, sport climbing and offshore rowing represent recently added or removed possibilities, while track and field, shooting, basketball, gymnastics, boxing and aquatic sports provide familiar categories with established audiences (pp. 14–17). The paper reports individual scores—sport climbing 33.268, karate 22.674 and offshore rowing 9.4959—before moving to the future-event recommendation. MC-TOPSIS changes the top-three order from athletics/football/basketball to football/athletics/basketball, illustrating why uncertainty should be shown rather than averaged away (pp. 13–21).

The multi-method weighting is itself a sensitivity device. AHP captures structured judgments, entropy and coefficient of variation respond to the data matrix, and CRITIC rewards contrast while penalizing indicator conflict. The Monte Carlo stage then reveals whether a high score survives plausible perturbation, which is why the paper reports both ordinary and simulated rankings (pp. 9–16).

The paper also reports candidate scores before making the recommendation, which helps prevent a reader from treating cricket, martial arts or squash as unexplained opinions. The event-level table shows how different strengths—audience, equity, sustainability and economic benefit—can compensate for one another in TOPSIS distance (pp. 14–21).

The paper's use of seven indicators makes trade-offs visible. A sport can gain from popularity and commercialization while losing on sustainability or safety, and TOPSIS records the distance to both an ideal and a negative ideal. This is more informative than a single popularity ranking for a decision explicitly framed around IOC standards (pp. 5–14).

The application section also uses the model diagnostically: the lower offshore-rowing score is not treated as a failure, but as evidence that a sport can be innovative while underperforming on audience or economic indicators. That makes the ranking explainable at indicator level (pp. 14–17).

The paper's recommendation is consequently an evidence-ranked shortlist, not a claim that one indicator decides Olympic membership (pp. 17–21).

The indicator table makes that shortlist auditable by showing the trade-offs behind each event score (pp. 5–17).

## Notes for reuse
- Composite rankings inherit the choice of indicators, proxies and weights; show them and run perturbation tests before treating a rank as definitive.
- Forecasts and cost savings depend on long-horizon assumptions about prices, technology and policy; give scenario ranges.
