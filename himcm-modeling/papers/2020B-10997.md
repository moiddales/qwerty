# 2020B-10997: Funding Biodiversity Conservation

## At a glance
Team 10997 treats the FRPCE decision as a long-horizon schedule rather than a one-year knapsack. Imperiled species can lose feasibility when conservation is delayed, while project costs vary by year. A logistic population model with strong Allee effect produces a feasibility-decay curve. For each project and year, TOPSIS combines benefit, taxonomic uniqueness, current feasibility, cost and duration; entropy weighting supplies data-driven factor weights. Dynamic programming and a greedy rule create an initial schedule under an annual cap, then a genetic algorithm shifts project start years to reduce annual spending fluctuation while retaining at least 95% of initial expected benefit. The recommendation is a $500,000 annual cap for 43 of 48 projects, or $2 million to complete all 48 within 40 years (pp. 1, 18–22).

## How they read the problem
The team identifies four board objectives: maximize conservation benefit, minimize annual spending fluctuation, remain cost-effective and fund selected projects continuously until completion (p. 5). They interpret delayed action as biologically harmful and funding as a repeated annual decision under a stable endowment income. Species-level and habitat-level protection measures are researched before the factors are selected (pp. 5–6). This framing explains why feasibility changes with start year and why a schedule is evaluated by both benefit and budget smoothness.

## Models and methods
The population model modifies logistic decline with a strong Allee effect: when a sparse population falls below a critical density, individual fitness and survival decline more rapidly. Feasibility is assumed proportional to population density, with feasibility stopping its decay once a project starts. A translated closed-form curve supplies each project’s feasibility at each possible start year; the extinction-rate coefficient r controls the speed (pp. 7–9).

The Priority Index uses five factors. Benefit and taxonomic uniqueness are benefit factors; cost and duration are cost factors. Feasibility is treated as an interval factor because the model seeks to avoid the steep middle of the decay curve. TOPSIS normalizes and compares each project to ideal and anti-ideal points. Entropy weighting derives weights from information dispersion instead of relying only on subjective judgments (pp. 9–10).

For each year, dynamic programming selects projects under the remaining annual funding cap. A greedy step orders remaining projects by current Priority Index and starts those that fit. The initial schedule uses a $500,000 cap over 35 years; expected net benefit and the standard deviation of yearly funding are combined into a total score (pp. 10–14). Because the initial schedule has a sharp late decline, a genetic algorithm encodes each project’s start year as a chromosome. Two-point crossover, mutation, tournament selection and a 95%-of-initial-benefit constraint search for a smoother schedule (pp. 14–18).

## Data
The attached 48-row project data provide benefit, taxonomic uniqueness, feasibility of success and costs for up to 25 years (pp. 4–5). Literature supplies characteristics of Florida imperiled plants and protection actions such as habitat acquisition, prescribed fire, invasive-species control, monitoring and reintroduction (pp. 5–7). The appendix lists project priorities by year (p. 25). The paper notes one non-monotone cost row and temporarily transfers a later spike to earlier years to preserve a descending-cost assumption (p. 13). The spreadsheet attachment is not present in this repository, so the reported schedule cannot be rerun from the corpus alone.

## Assumptions
FRPCE is treated as the primary funder; feasibility declines when a project is delayed; benefit and uniqueness stay constant; and the endowment has a relatively constant annual budget (pp. 3–4). A strong Allee effect and r = 0.3 are selected to make urgency visible (pp. 7–9). Projects run continuously after starting, and unselected projects may be omitted when the cap is insufficient. These assumptions are explicit and can be changed in the formulas or algorithm.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The initial schedule is compared with the genetic-algorithm schedule using expected benefit, funding standard deviation, total score and completion duration (pp. 14–20). In the reported schedules, the $500,000 cap completes 43 projects in 32 years with EB 77.801, annual-cost standard deviation $39,215 and total score 1.984; the $2,000,000 cap completes all 48 in 40 years with EB 85.744, standard deviation $126,002 and total score 0.681 (p. 20). GA improves smoothness and completes the same selected set three years earlier. Different initial conditions are tested: a DP/greedy seed outperforms a random seed, showing why a good feasible starting schedule matters (p. 18). Increasing r by 1% and 5% changes expected benefit and total score modestly while leaving annual funding nearly unchanged (pp. 19–21). The weaknesses section flags the idealized decay and treatment of very low/high feasibility (pp. 21–22).

## What makes it stand out
- Biological urgency is connected to scheduling through a feasibility-decay function.
- TOPSIS and entropy weights separate project ranking from the later budget-smoothing objective.
- Dynamic programming gives a feasible baseline; GA improves a known weakness instead of replacing the baseline blindly.
- The 95% benefit constraint prevents a visually smooth schedule from sacrificing too much conservation value.
- Two funding-cap recommendations answer different board priorities: cost-effectiveness versus completing every project.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary sheet names the biological model, ranking method and two-stage schedule before reporting recommendations (p. 1). A variables table and flowcharts make the long pipeline navigable (pp. 4, 9–13). Figures 5–12 show initial versus optimized funding, reversed-cost stress tests and initial-condition comparisons. The memo on p. 23 repeats the recommendation in board language and explains how to read start-year codes. Code and priority tables remain in appendices (pp. 24–25).

## Notes for reuse
- Fit feasibility decay to species-specific evidence when available; a single r and strong Allee effect are scenario assumptions (pp. 7–9, 21–22).
- Keep a seeded feasible schedule before heuristic optimization; random GA initialization can settle at a poorer solution (p. 18).
