# 2020A-10656: Untitled (job/internship matching score for high schoolers)

## At a glance
The team builds a transparent, hand-designed **multiplicative score** that matches a student to jobs. Each of six factors (COVID-19 risk, wage, interests, hours, physical intensity, age) contributes a term between 0 and 1. Terms for "irreversible" conditions (risk, physical exertion, age above the user's limit) drop to exactly 0 and remove the job. "Soft" shortfalls (lower wage, fewer hours) apply exponential-decay penalties. A database of 36 jobs is curated from the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, filtered by federal child-labor age rules, and rated for COVID risk using OSHA guidance. They extend the model to 25 unpaid internships with a three-term variant. Testing covers 17 fictional students (10 job-seekers, 7 internship-seekers), and the system returns each student's top 10. The deliverable is a website questionnaire whose questions map one-to-one onto model inputs (p. 15).

## How they read the problem
- They reduce the task to three questions: which jobs will exist in 2021 and what they involve; which aspects matter to the student, and also what an employer will require; and how to combine these into an evaluation (pp. 2–3). Considering the employer side is what led them to the age and qualification constraints.
- "Keeping in mind the target audience, the model should be simple and presentable" (p. 3). This design goal explains the closed-form score and the use of brackets for inputs.
- They define a summer job carefully (paid, legal, repeated labor while school is out; Assumption 2, p. 3). They then add unpaid internships as an extension with their own model (pp. 1, 10). Recreation time is handled through the user's maximum daily hours, which "reflects the user's level of commitment to the job, versus other summer activities and commitments such as recreation or academics" (p. 5).
- They scope to U.S. students aged 14 and over, under federal labor law, and only to jobs needing no high-school diploma per the OOH (pp. 3–4).

## Models and methods
- **Score structure.** This is not a weighted sum. Every factor is a multiplier in [0, 1], so any failed hard constraint zeroes the score. Rationale: these are "irreversible conditions" (p. 10). Wage is softer, since "we assume the user would still be willing to work for a job with lower wages than they would like, albeit not as willingly" (p. 8).
- **Step functions in closed form.** (|x+½|/(x+½))/2 + ½ equals 1 when x ≥ 0 and 0 when x < 0 for integer x. They derive this property in the text (pp. 7–8). This lets the whole model be one formula and one line of Python.
- **Constants with stated reasoning.** The 1.04^(Ri−Rc) bonus "maxes out at 1.12" and is mainly a tie-breaker (p. 8). 0.7 was chosen because 0.7² = 0.49, so a 2-bracket hours gap "at worst, roughly halve[s] the score" (p. 9). Shared non-interests earn 0.01 against 0.25 for shared interests, again to break ties (p. 8).
- **Preference switch.** The binary Ei raises the wage term or the interest term to the power 2, which emphasizes the student's main goal (p. 8).
- **Internship model.** It keeps the risk and physical terms and drops wage and hours. Its interest term omits the leading 1, so an internship with no matching interest scores near 0, reflecting that "interests [are] far more important for internships" (p. 10).
- **Implementation.** Python reads two CSV files (people, jobs), scores every pair, sorts them, and writes each person's top 10 (pp. 17–20).

## Data
- **Job universe.** OOH occupations requiring no formal education (108 categories). Some broad categories were expanded (e.g. ushers / lobby attendants / ticket takers split, because their COVID risk and physical intensity differ) and redundant ones dropped, giving 107. A diverse sample of 36 covering all age brackets and interest fields was then selected (pp. 6–7). The internship list has 25 entries (p. 7).
- **Ratings.** Age minimums from federal child-labor provisions; COVID risk adapted from the OSHA/HHS guidance, rescaled within its medium/low tiers because few teen jobs are high-risk, with remote jobs at the safe end (p. 7); wages from OwlGuru and O*NET; physical demand and hours from O*NET job descriptions using their own scale (p. 7).
- **Interest fields.** Taken from a BLS article on popular teen summer jobs (Figure 1, p. 6); internship fields from a NYT list (p. 6).
- Full job and persona tables are in Appendices V–VI (pp. 23–24).

## Assumptions
There are seven numbered assumptions, each in a bolded name + statement + "Justification" format (pp. 3–4). Examples:
- **Age ≥ 14:** federal law prohibits non-agricultural work below 14.
- **No diploma:** "Fewer than 3% of high schoolers graduate early", so OOH categories that need a diploma are excluded.
- **COVID guidelines followed:** this lets risk be judged "solely based on the amount of contact on the job".
- **Constant daily hours:** simplifies the time brackets.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Plausibility.** Best jobs are matched against persona narratives (Figure 2, p. 11). For persons A and J, radar charts show that the inputs and the selected job's attributes have similar shapes (pp. 12–13).
- **Sensitivity (one input at a time).**
  - Person B: raising maximum hours from bracket 1 to 2 brings service and sales jobs matching his interests to the top (News/Street Vendor, Technology Consultant).
  - Person D: raising the desired wage bracket from 1 to 2 replaces the remote musician and childcare jobs with Animal Shelter Worker and Freelance Musician (in person).

  Each change is traced back to the specific formula term responsible (p. 13).
- **Self-assessment.** A strengths/weaknesses table organized by stage (interpretation, data, model formation, testing) acknowledges that the "Constants in the formula are somewhat arbitrary" and that commuting was not modeled (p. 16).

## What makes it stand out
- **Hard vs soft constraints built into the algebra.** Multiplying 0/1 gates with decay penalties makes infeasible jobs vanish automatically, while shortfalls only lower a job's score (pp. 7–10). This structure is easy to explain to a non-expert.
- **Every constant is argued.** Instead of fitted or unexplained weights, each number has a stated purpose and a worst-case effect (×1.12 cap, ≈½ worst-case hours penalty, tie-breaking 0.01) (pp. 8–9).
- **Authoritative, traceable data pipeline.** OOH → no-diploma filter → child-labor age brackets → OSHA risk tiers → O*NET/OwlGuru wages (pp. 6–7). Each rating has a named public source.
- **Problem-specific realism.** COVID exposure, vulnerable family members (Person A) and legal age limits make the 2021 context concrete (pp. 4, 7, 11).
- **Sensible extension.** Unpaid internships get a separate, simpler model, and the paper explains why the interest term must dominate there (p. 10).
- **Inputs, model and interface use the same scales.** The website's 1–4 and 1–3 buttons are exactly the model's input scales (p. 15), so the presentation is implementable as shown.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** four paragraphs: context (COVID, intimidating job market) → factors and scoring idea (top 10 returned) → Python implementation and 17 test students → website presentation (p. 1). It gives no numerical results; the emphasis is on usability.
- **Paper structure:** Summary → Introduction (background, interpretation as three questions, assumptions) → Developing the model (variables, data, job formula, internship formula) → Testing (with sensitivity) → Presenting the model → Strengths & weaknesses → Conclusion → References → Appendices (code, full outputs, databases).
- **Devices:** each formula term introduced and then explained in plain language with its range and edge cases; a colour-banded persona input/output table (p. 11); paired radar charts of input vs best job (pp. 12–13); a strengths/weaknesses table organized by modeling stage (p. 16); a full-page website mock-up with colour-graded buttons (p. 15).
- **Website:** aimed at students. It asks short plain questions ("Regarding COVID-19 risk, what is the maximum level of contact of your desired job?") with labelled options such as "no risk (remote)" and "frequent contact" (pp. 14–15). The paper also explains why a website suits better than a newspaper: "We also need to do calculations in the background, so physical options like newspapers do not suffice" (p. 13).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "In other words, if the job has a higher risk than the user is willing to endure, its total index is 0 and the job is thrown out of consideration." (pp. 7–8)
  - "This difference is at most 2, so the constant 0.7 was picked since (0.7)² = 0.49, meaning this term will, at worst, roughly halve the score." (p. 9)
  - "The top ten jobs are printed out as to give the user some choice, because all of the top ten jobs would ideally be suitable fits." (p. 14)

## Notes for reuse
- The penalty and bonus constants (1.04, 0.7, 0.25, 0.01, the squaring by Ei) are reasoned but not calibrated against any data, and the authors call them "somewhat arbitrary" (p. 16). Only two single-input sensitivity cases are shown. If you copy the multiplicative design, test how rankings change with these constants.
