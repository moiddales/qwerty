# 2017B-8121: Trail Quality Index and Ski Resort Design

## At a glance
The team generates hundreds of candidate trails from topography, scores them with a Trail Quality Index using steepness, curves, width, roughness and energy loss, and selects a 160+ km network with the required 20/40/40 difficulty split. A second index ranks the resort and an agent model estimates lift waits. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They separate geometric feasibility, trail quality, resort-level ranking and Olympic readiness. Candidate paths are filtered before selection, and the resort score is tuned to skier versus Olympic priorities. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Topographic paths are generated and scored; selected trails feed a weighted multi-criteria index. A simulation of skiers, lifts and queues estimates waiting and crowding. Reported resort score is 7.44 and Olympic score 7.8 (pp. 4–22).

## Data
The paper uses Wasatch topographic map, resort comparison table and USGS/GIS data. The source is the Wasatch topographic map or USGS/GIS elevation data, together with a resort comparison table and web references. Candidate paths, slope classes and skier/lift scenarios are derived rather than observed. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes the topographic data represent buildable terrain, slope grade is the main difficulty signal, and candidate paths can be filtered for rivers, outparcels and unsafe grades. Ranking weights represent skier or Olympic priorities, while lift simulations use stylized skier mixes and capacities (pp. 4–10). Its central choice is trail quality index, supported by GIS path generation; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
Path counts, difficulty shares, resort rankings and lift sensitivity are reported. The agent model varies lift numbers and skier mix; GIS and watershed comparisons check path stability (pp. 18–24).

## What makes it stand out
The TQI makes an inherently subjective slope choice auditable, while the two ranking indices answer different stakeholders. Combining GIS with queue simulation is a strong systems view.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary and memo state trail totals and scores. Maps, difficulty tables, a lift simulation figure and sensitivity analysis support the narrative (pp. 1–3, 13–24).

## Notes for reuse
- Trail quality weights and the 20/40/40 split are choices. An imitator should validate path safety with domain experts and separate modeled trail length from buildable length.
