# 2021B-11898: Saving Lake Mead: Recycling Wastewater Does Wonders

## At a glance
The team builds each part on a recognized concept and then makes it operational. The drought definition **combines the meteorological and hydrological definitions of drought**. A month counts as drought only if the level is below 1085 ft *and* below the mean of the previous three months, which puts the most recent drought at April 2021 to present (p. 12). The two forecasting models are deliberately different time-series tools. Model A, a weighted moving average with a bias correction, is fitted to the short recent-drought window. Model B, adaptive filtering with a sine-function seasonal correction, is fitted to 2005–2020. Their annual means are about 1016 / 952 / 734 ft (Model A) and 1054 / 1035 / 962 ft (Model B) for 2025 / 2030 / 2050 (Fig 11, p. 17). Model A falls below the ~900 ft dead pool by 2050. Part 1 includes a small multiple regression of elevation on temperature, precipitation and a decomposed wind vector, and Part 3 ranks plan factors with AHP. Wastewater quality comes first, with weight 0.4185.

## How they read the problem
- The question is decomposed into three components and then into sub-questions labelled Question 1–3 (pp. 4–5).
- Part 1a is read as needing both qualitative relationships (Fig 1 diagram, p. 6) and a quantitative measure of each factor's influence on level. That leads to the regression with monthly weather inputs (pp. 7–8).
- The irregular shape in Part 1b is handled by splitting the lake vertically. The smoother upper part is a frustum, and the rugged bed is a collection of triangular cones and quadrangular prisms (pp. 9–10). Only the upper part can be checked against Table 1. The lower part would need sonar bathymetry (p. 10).
- In Part 2a they take recognized definitions of drought from the National Weather Service and adapt each into a test on water level (p. 12).
- Part 3 is framed as prioritization. Which plan elements matter most is answered with AHP, and impact is measured on three dimensions: environment, economy, society (pp. 19–22).

## Models and methods
- **Weather–level regression (1a).** Daily 2019 weather (meteoblue ERA5) is averaged to months. Wind is projected onto the southwest direction (eq. 2, p. 8), on the reasoning that winds toward the southwest boost inflow from the northeast-lying Colorado River (pp. 6, 8). All inputs are min-max normalized (eq. 3) and regressed on 2019 monthly elevation with statsmodels OLS (code, p. 24). Evaporation is left out as a regressor because Dalton's law makes it a function of wind and temperature (p. 7).
- **Volume decomposition (1b).** A frustum for the upper body and cones/prisms for the bed (eqs. 6–8). The shape choices are justified by the sloping shore and by grooves with flat or sharp bottoms (pp. 9–10).
- **Drought criterion (2a).** The meteorological idea (current versus recent average) becomes "below the previous three months' mean". The hydrological idea (below a locally significant threshold) becomes 1085 ft, the level at which the Bureau announced a 2015 warning (p. 12).
- **Model A.** ĥ(t+1) = (3h[t] + 2h[t−1] + h[t−2])/6 (eq. 9, p. 13). The rationale is that the climate of consecutive months is more alike, so recent months deserve more weight. After a check against Jul–Sep 2021 (predicted values averaged 0.18% high), predictions are divided by 1.0018 (p. 14).
- **Model B.** Adaptive filtering iteratively adjusts N = 3 weights, Ŵ = W + 2k·e·y, until the improvement in error falls below a tolerance (eqs. 11–12, Fig 9 flowchart, pp. 14–15). To restore the within-year cycle that a long-horizon time series flattens out, the forecast is scaled by a sine fit to the 2020 monthly levels (eqs. 13–14, Table 5, p. 16).
- **AHP (3a).** A 5×5 pairwise matrix on Saaty's scale. The text says a consistency test was performed (p. 20) and gives normalized eigenvector weights (Table 9).

## Data
The team brought in one year of external weather data (2019 daily temperature, precipitation, wind speed and direction from meteoblue ERA5) and states that only 2019 was accessible (p. 7). Everything else uses the attachment elevations and Table 1. The 1085 ft threshold and the ~900 ft dead-storage level come from cited news and agency sources (pp. 12, 14).

## Assumptions
There are three numbered assumption–justification pairs (p. 5):
- Monthly residential water use is constant within a year, because population change within a year is small.
- No extreme events such as dam failure, because they have very small probability.
- Recent climate patterns persist to 2050, because emission-reduction effects take a long time to mature.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Regression fitted versus actual for 2019 (Fig 4, p. 8).
- The frustum compared with Table 1 differences (Table 1, p. 10).
- A hold-out check of Model A on Jul–Sep 2021, used to derive a bias correction (Table 3, p. 14). An error-rate comparison over 2005–2020 (A 0.4%, B 0.3%) (p. 17).
- The sine fit compared with actual 2020 values (Fig 10, p. 16).
- Sensitivity to the lag length N, 3 → 5, for the 2025 prediction (Fig 17, pp. 22–23). Model B proves more stable.
- A strengths/weaknesses section that names its own limitations: drought based only on level, time series inaccurate over the long run, no high/low forecasts (p. 22).

## What makes it stand out
- **A drought definition built on recognized concepts.** Each half of the two-part test is traced to an official definition of drought type, and the threshold is sourced (p. 12). This makes the criterion easy to defend.
- **Two models that differ in kind.** The models differ in method, not just data window. Their comparison covers data needs, seasonality, error rate and computation (pp. 17–18), which fully answers "compare and evaluate".
- **Seasonality restored.** The team noticed that long-horizon moving-average forecasts flatten into a line, so it multiplied by a fitted annual sine cycle (pp. 16, 18). The fix is simple, explained and visualized month by month (Figs 12–14).
- **Errors measured and used.** The short-window model is checked on held-out months and its bias is corrected (p. 14).
- **Physical forecasts tied to stakes.** 2050 predictions are compared with dead storage (~900 ft) in the paper and the article (pp. 3, 14).
- **Structured prioritization.** AHP turns a list of plan factors into ranked weights shown in a rose diagram (pp. 19–20). The impact measurement is organized by environment, economy and society (pp. 21–22).

## Notes for reuse
- The Part 1a regression rests on 12 monthly points from one year (2019). The wind mechanism, winds "blow water from the river into the lake" (p. 6), is the team's own hypothesis with no hydrological source. The ranking "winds have the greatest effect" (p. 9) should not be read as an established finding.
- Model A extrapolates six months of data (Apr–Sep 2021) to 2050. The 734 ft result reflects the short window's slope carried forward roughly 29 years, not a physical process. The AHP pairwise judgments are the authors' own, and the consistency ratio is not reported numerically.
