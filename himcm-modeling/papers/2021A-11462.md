# 2021A-11462: Untitled (battery-sizing and cost model)

## At a glance
The team turns "choose the best battery" into a sizing problem: for each candidate battery, compute how many units are needed to cover the house's worst-case energy and power demand, then pick the battery whose system is cheapest. Demand is built bottom-up from a researched appliance table, split into always-on and usage-dependent loads, and integrated over the longest night of the year plus a fully cloudy day. For the base 1600 ft² house (45° latitude, four occupants) the model calls for 41.59 kWh and 13.24 kW and selects 4 Tesla Powerwall+ units for $34,000 (p. 8–10), which the team checks against a $35,177 commercial 40 kWh backup kit (p. 10). The generalized model adds night length from latitude, HVAC from heat loss and indoor temperature, and lighting/appliance use from occupants and floor area, and is implemented as a Python calculator (p. 11–16, 21–23).

## How they read the problem
- Reframes "best" as "cheapest system that is sufficient": any battery "can be tiled to be sufficient", so cost is "the best metric in our case to compare the relative efficacy of different batteries" (p. 1).
- Reduces the four statistics in the problem to two binding requirements, usable capacity and continuous power rating (CPR); instantaneous power rating is "deemed less important" because it matters only for short bursts (p. 6). Round-trip efficiency enters as a capacity derating in the code (p. 23).
- Defines the design scenario precisely: a cloudy day is "24 hours plus one night", so the battery must cover one day period plus two nights, and the night used is the longest of the year (winter solstice) at the chosen latitude (p. 4, 8).
- Splits each day into 16 "productive" hours and 8 "rest" hours, and splits appliances into constant (water heater, HVAC, fridge) and nonconstant (lights, cooking, laundry, TV, etc.) (p. 4, 6–7).
- Part 2 is read as "turn fixed constants into functions of user inputs": latitude, indoor temperature, square footage and number of people (p. 11, 17).

## Models and methods
- **Demand model.** e_app and e_c are sums of nonconstant and constant appliance energy per day (p. 7). Night energy E_n = (n/24)e_c + ((n−8)/24)e_app; day energy E_d = ((24−n)/24)(e_c + e_app); E_cloudy = E_d + 2E_n (p. 7–8). With n = 15.24 h (45°, 21 December, NOAA) this gives E_n = 15.43 kWh, E_d = 10.73 kWh and E_cloudy = 41.59 kWh (p. 8).
- **Power model.** cpr = (sum of nonconstant draws)/2 + sum of constant draws = 13.24 kW. The 1/2 factor was introduced because with every appliance on the output "almost completely hing[ed] on cpr" (p. 8–9).
- **Battery-count model.** len_parallel = ceil(E_cloudy/cap_batt), len_series = ceil(cpr/cpr_batt). The team noticed that a single string meets one requirement but not the other, so it added Q_series (enough strings to run at cpr for 1/10 of the day) and len_min (a minimum string size sized for cpr_min with 1/8 of nonconstant load). batt_Tot = len_min(len_parallel − Q_series) + len_series·Q_series; Price = cost_batt·batt_Tot (p. 9–10). The 1/10 and 1/8 constants are stated as chosen by the team ("arbitrarily decided", p. 9).
- **Generalization (p. 11–16).** Night length from the NOAA hour-angle equation with declination ±23.45° and a hemisphere switch, n = 24 − 2(ha/15). HVAC energy from conductive heat loss through walls, windows and roof (area/R-value terms × temperature difference × 24 h), divided by SEER or HSPF ratings in a piecewise function for cooling vs. heating. Lighting power from 116 lm/W LEDs, 35 lm/ft² and 150 ft² lit per person, capped by floor area. Appliance energy is held at 5.84 kWh for p ≤ 4 and scaled by p/4 above that.
- **Implementation.** A Python script prompts for latitude, indoor temperature, square footage and occupants, then prints count and cost for each battery in a dictionary (p. 16, 21–23). A flowchart summarizes the pipeline (Figure 3, p. 16).

## Data
- Appliance energy and power come from Energy Guide labels, retailer product pages, energy.gov and Penn State course notes (Table 3a, p. 6). The team notes that "many of these values had to be approximated or found using yearly averages" (p. 6).
- Battery data: the COMAP table plus Enphase Encharge-10, Fortress Power 48 and an LFPGC2-12175 LiFePO4 battery from datasheets (Table 3c, p. 10). A note under the table says which rows came from COMAP.
- Night length from NOAA solar equations. Insulation and thermal values come from Home Depot and Penn State sources. Outdoor temperature is fixed at 32°F in the code (p. 22).

## Assumptions
The paper gives eight numbered assumptions, each with a "Justification:" paragraph (p. 3–4). Representative examples:
- Solar panels fully power the house and fully recharge the batteries on a sunny day. This scopes the task to storage only (p. 3).
- No generation on a cloudy day. The justification is that cloudy-day output depends on the unknown array and varies by severity, and that the assumption "also gives our system redundancy" (p. 4).
- A cloudy day lasts 24 hours plus one night, because the batteries must carry the house from the previous sunset until the panels recover (p. 4).
- All batteries can be combined in series and/or parallel. This is acknowledged as a simplification (p. 4).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **External benchmark:** the $34,000 / 4-unit output is compared with a commercial 40 kWh backup kit priced at $35,177 (p. 1, 10).
- **One-at-a-time sensitivity** around a base case (45°, 72°F, 1600 ft², 4 people), with results expressed in "number of Tesla batteries" (p. 17–18). About 15°F of indoor-temperature change shifts the count by one battery (minimum 3). Every 4 additional people adds one. ±60° of latitude changes the count by only one, which the team calls "somewhat disappointing" and then explains. Each extra 1000 ft² adds one battery, a linear relation.
- A frank strengths/weaknesses section identifies the "shaky" coefficients and single-battery-type systems as the main weaknesses (p. 18).

## What makes it stand out
- **A clear engineering definition of "best".** The team converts the comparison of mixed specifications into one interpretable output per battery (units needed and total dollars), which makes batteries of very different sizes directly comparable (p. 1, 10).
- **An explicit worst-case design scenario.** Winter-solstice night at the chosen latitude plus a full cloudy day is a defensible, checkable sizing basis, and it is the reason night length is modeled at all (p. 4, 8).
- **Physically grounded generalization.** Night length, HVAC and lighting become functions of location, temperature, area and occupants through standard physics (NOAA solar geometry, conductive heat loss with SEER/HSPF), not arbitrary scores (p. 11–15).
- **Self-correction shown in the text.** The paper shows why the naive parallel/series counts fail ("a crippling issue, especially with small batteries") and introduces constraints to fix it. This makes the model's logic easy to follow (p. 9).
- **Reality check against the market.** The final price is compared with a real product of similar capacity, a simple validation that readers find convincing (p. 10).
- **Sensitivity reported in decision units.** Results are given as "+1 battery per 1000 ft²", which a homeowner can read directly (p. 17–18).
- **A working tool.** The Python calculator and flowchart (p. 16, 21–23) show that the generalized model can actually be run for any home. New batteries are added by appending to a list (p. 17).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** three paragraphs (p. 1). The first sets the problem context and the task, the second explains the modeling logic (why cost is the comparison metric and which inputs are considered), and the third gives the headline result with numbers (4 Powerwall+, $34,000) and the market check. It closes with an honest caveat.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction → Preliminary Information (assumptions, definitions, variables) → Formulating a Model (considerations, research, interpretation, equations, final evaluation) → Generalization (light, night, HVAC, appliances, implementation) → Conclusion (results, sensitivity, strengths/weaknesses) → Supplemental Writings (concrete, news article) → Appendix (code) → Works Cited.
- **Devices:** assumption–justification pairs; a variables table with units (Table 2a, p. 5); a research table of appliances with bracketed citations (p. 6); a constant vs. nonconstant classification table (p. 7); a battery table that marks external sources (p. 10); a code flowchart (p. 16); worked numerical substitution inside the equations (p. 8–9).
- **Article:** written for general readers. It opens with everyday batteries ("When you panic over a dead T.V. remote…") and moves to night-time storage for an off-grid home. It names the winner and its unit price, mentions grid costs ("upwards of $140 a month!") and ends with a forward-looking paragraph on cement batteries (p. 20).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "Any battery can be tiled to be sufficient, but some batteries will do this more efficiently than others." (p. 1)
  - "Thus, we only need to solve for a single n, specifically the maximum." (p. 8)
  - "By accounting for the worst-case scenario, we ensure that our battery system can last through just about anything." (p. 18)

## Notes for reuse
- The paper states that parallel connection sums capacity but not CPR and that series does the opposite (p. 9). With a common inverter, total energy and power both scale with the number of identical units whichever wiring is used (wiring sets voltage and current), so imitators should not copy this electrical premise or the batt_Tot formula built on it.
- Several coefficients that directly change the battery count (1/2 appliance simultaneity, 1/8 for cpr_min, 1/10 of the day for Q_series, 150 ft² lit per person) are set by judgment without a cited source (p. 9, 11, 15). The paper acknowledges this, but none of them is included in its sensitivity analysis.
