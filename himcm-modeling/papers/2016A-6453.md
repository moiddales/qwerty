# 2016A-6453: Clustered Triathlon Wave Scheduling

## At a glance
Using prior-race segment times, the team clusters participants into six comparable groups, uses a genetic algorithm to order and time waves, then applies linear programming to account for course distance and congestion. The proposed schedule keeps the event within about 4.5 hours while avoiding same-speed interference. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They reduce the original divisions when too many categories create congestion, preserve recognizable awards, and sequence similar speeds. The three models are staged: data grouping, wave timing, then course-distance refinement. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Means and standard deviations classify athletes; clustering produces six groups. A genetic algorithm minimizes total completion time subject to noninterference. Linear programming adjusts segment distances and start offsets (pp. 4–25).

## Data
The paper uses HiMCM triathlon dataset and prompt constraints. The source is the prior-triathlon spreadsheet of age, gender, category, segment and transition times. Teams derive quartiles, clusters or fitted distributions and generate simulated entrants and schedules. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes prior-race times represent this year’s entrants, segment speeds can be summarized by groups or distributions, and wave starts do not change individual ability. Congestion is represented by passing, density or speed overlap; road closure is a hard limit or an explicitly weighted term (pp. 4–10). Its central choice is cluster analysis, supported by genetic algorithm wave schedule; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The schedule is stress-tested against different group sizes and course distances; the paper reports road-closure and completion-time totals and compares the optimized schedule to the prior event (pp. 24–30).

## What makes it stand out
The staged decomposition keeps a hard scheduling problem tractable and gives the organizer a concrete timetable. Cluster analysis avoids pretending that every historical category has a distinct pace.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
A mayoral letter and printed wave timetable precede model details. Tables, flowcharts and an appendix of schedules make the optimization reviewable (pp. 1–5, 23–33).

## Notes for reuse
- Cluster centroids hide within-group variation. An imitator should simulate individual athletes after optimizing waves and report the chance that a nominally safe schedule still produces overtakes.
