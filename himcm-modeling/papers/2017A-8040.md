# 2017A-8040: Geometric Ferris Wheel, Dragon and Smile

## At a glance
The team uses 3-D coordinates to model a Ferris wheel, a detailed sinusoidal dragon and a poker-face-to-smile display. MATLAB estimates up to 427 drones, a 9×9 m launch area and an 80 m high, 100×50 m airspace; rotations and periodic motions animate the figures. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They choose geometrical primitives that can be sampled into drone points, then define a motion rule for each display. The maximum drone count drives launch and airspace requirements. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Circles, spokes and triangles form the Ferris wheel; sin(x) circles form the dragon body; straight facial features are mapped to parabolas. Matrix rotation and coordinate substitution create animation (pp. 4–24).

## Data
The paper uses display sketches, drone spacing and FAA/safety references. The figures are team-designed point sets or traced display contours, supplemented by drone spacing, battery, safety, FAA and cost references. Coordinates and sampling resolution are generated within the model. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes a drone can hold a prescribed 3-D coordinate and follow a planned path, with a fixed safety separation and battery duration. Display point spacing, altitude, audience buffer and launch packing are explicit design choices; regulatory constraints are discussed as requirements to verify (pp. 4–10). Its central choice is circle/triangle geometry, supported by sinusoidal dragon path; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
MATLAB calculations check point counts, spacing and display dimensions. The paper compares static and animated frames and reports duration and safety buffers (pp. 25–35).

## What makes it stand out
The models are easy to communicate and visually faithful. Each display has a named mathematical primitive, and the smile transformation shows how a small formula can produce a clear animation.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
Repeated diagrams, a mayoral letter and MATLAB plots keep the geometry accessible. The summary gives drone count, launch area and airspace before derivations (pp. 1–4).

## Notes for reuse
- Reported durations and drone counts depend on point spacing and a fixed safety buffer. An imitator should specify sampling resolution and test visibility from multiple audience angles.
