# 2018B-8506: Untitled (smart climate control system "ThermostaX")

## At a glance
The team framed the single-zone thermostat as a constrained multi-objective optimization: choose tomorrow's 96 quarter-hour set-points to maximize a user-satisfaction function while minimizing air-conditioner power (p. 1, p. 6). User preferences are learned from two months of set-point history: autocorrelation finds the period of the user's schedule (1–31 days), and an adaptive weighted average of in-phase past days predicts the ideal temperature (pp. 7–10). A thermodynamic room–wall–environment model gives power as a function of the temperature curve, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), borrowed from game-playing AI, searches the curve space; humidity, allergen and indoor–outdoor-gap rules act as constraints (pp. 12–19). On self-generated data they report mean absolute prediction error below 1°C, one-day reaction to trend changes, and energy reductions of roughly 9–38% depending on climate and weights (pp. 30–31). The multi-zone extension learns inter-zone "interdependency indices" through a calibration mode and propagates temperatures with a row-stochastic matrix (pp. 25–27).

## How they read the problem
- They restated Problem 1 as two functions: "temperature prediction and schedule anticipation" (p. 4), and explicitly flagged schedule irregularity as the core difficulty for both.
- They chose to make the thermostat's decision variable the room temperature only; humidity, allergens and pollution are handled indirectly via temperature limits, justified by the assumption "Our system should focus on temperature control" (p. 5, p. 17).
- Key reframing: rather than assume a 5+2 work week (as they say Nest does), the period of each user's routine is *detected* from data, illustrated with a doctor couple working 9 days and resting 5 days in two weeks (p. 7).
- Absence is handled probabilistically: the probability of return in each time block weights the satisfaction term, so the system keeps running at reduced effort rather than switching off (p. 21, p. 23).
- For Problem 2 they listed similarities and three differences between single- and multi-zone systems: inter-zone heat flow, unmeasurable parameters, and multiple occupants with different preferences (pp. 24–25).

## Models and methods
**Data representation (p. 7).** Each day is 96 fifteen-minute blocks; two months of set-points are stored. One database marks "off" with −99999 for period detection; another fills absences by extrapolation from adjacent blocks for prediction.

**Period detection (pp. 7–8, 10–11).** Autocorrelation R(τ) is computed for τ = 1…31 days of lag; the smallest τ with R > 0.7 is the period, because shorter periods use more recent data (their "adjacency effect" assumption, p. 5). Re-run daily so changes (e.g. school holidays) are caught. Start-up flowchart (Figure 4.1, p. 11): manual input for two days, default period of one week if none found. The 0.7 threshold is later examined in the robustness section.

**Temperature prediction (pp. 8–10).** For each block, the five most recent in-phase values are combined. Weights come from how close each older value is to the most recent one ((max diff + 0.001) − diff, normalized); the most recent value gets the same weight as the next one; an adjusting term is added or subtracted when the latest value lies outside the range of the older ones (to follow seasonal trends); finally a drift term δ (average difference between the last two periods) captures short-term shocks such as a cold wave. Rationale given: adapt to both long-term shifts and short-term anomalies without assuming a schedule type.

**Satisfaction (pp. 11–12).** S is a normal density centred on the predicted ideal temperature; σ² ∈ [0.5, 3.5] is set from a 0–5 user slider. Justified by literature on a ~1°C comfort tolerance and on 0.1°C sensitivity.

**Power (pp. 12–16).** COP of cooling derived from enthalpies of HFC-134a in a reversed Rankine cycle (theoretical ≈ 5.9; they use COP ≈ 4; COP_heat = COP_cool + 1). Heat capacities of air, furniture and concrete walls are estimated for a 6 m × 5 m × 3 m flat; because the concrete term dominates, they model three components (room, wall, environment) with coupled ODEs and solve for the heat the AC must remove, giving a piecewise power function P(Tr, Ta, Ṫr) (Eq. 28). Because P is non-differentiable at Qr = 0, Euler–Lagrange methods are ruled out and the problem is discretized. Solar and thermal radiation are estimated (Beer–Lambert, Stefan–Boltzmann) and then explicitly dropped as too variable to compute.

**Optimization (pp. 16–17).** U = αS + βP with β < 0. Brute force is shown infeasible (39^96 ≈ 5.5 × 10^152 curves; 9^96 ≈ 4.0 × 10^91 with a ±2°C step limit), motivating MCTS by analogy with Go. Constraints enter as restrictions on legal moves. Ruby implementation in the appendix (pp. 35–39).

**Return anticipation (pp. 19–21).** Conditional pmf of absence duration given leave block, set to 1 after the latest observed return; this probability multiplies the satisfaction term (Eq. 36).

**Multi-zone (pp. 25–28).** Houses are graphs of zones (outdoors is a zone); edge weights k are learned by switching off one zone's AC while holding neighbours fixed and solving linear equations. K is made row-stochastic so T(t + Δt) = K T; the gap between this "natural" vector and the desired temperatures, passed through Eq. 28, gives total work. For shared rooms they maximize the *product* of occupants' Gaussian satisfactions, showing with a 24°C/26°C example that the sum gives two peaks while the product peaks at 25°C (p. 28).

## Data
No real usage data were available; the team generated thermostat logs for archetypal users (a student on school days, pp. 44–55; a retired man, pp. 56–67; NA marks absence) plus scenario sets for gradual shifts, tapering and anomalies (pp. 30–31). Physical constants come from cited tables (HFC-134a, concrete density and specific heat, emissivity; pp. 13–16). Competitor savings figures come from the Nest white paper (735/624 households) and Ecobee's site (p. 23). The allergen database (p. 18) cites literature for dust mites, pollen and mould. An hourly ambient-temperature profile is hard-coded in the MCTS code (p. 36).

## Assumptions
Presented as five "Assumption / Justification" pairs (p. 5) followed by a bulleted variable list (pp. 5–6). Representative examples:
- Users change the set-point when uncomfortable, so the set-point history reveals preference (p. 5).
- Satisfaction vs deviation is bell-shaped; skewness neglected because σ is small (p. 5).
- Inter-zone thermal interaction can be summarized by one constant per zone pair, since furniture and walls rarely change (p. 5).
- "Adjacency effect": more recent data are more predictive, because preference-changing events are less likely in shorter intervals (p. 5); this assumption drives both the period choice and the weights.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Threshold test (pp. 28–29):** synthetic data sets with different R(τ) are scattered against the average peak of the return pmf (Figure 6.1, with a fitted line and a red 1/3 reference line). Below R ≈ 0.6 peaks are mostly under 1/3, which would make return anticipation useless; this supports R = 0.7 and the advice to switch to manual return input.
- **Prediction robustness (pp. 30–31):** gradual summer-to-fall shift (MAE 0.28°C, max 0.92°C, no day above 1.5°C error over 21 days); tapering off, gradual vs abrupt (MAE 0.57/0.67°C, reaction time 1 day); anomalies lasting 2, 4, 8 days (MAE 0.91–1.01°C, max up to 4.92°C, reaction time 1 day).
- **Utility weights (p. 31):** Table 6.4 reports energy reduction for (α, β) = (5000, −1) and (5000, −10) in tropical, temperate and frigid weather (9.1 ± 0.7% to 38.2 ± 0.7%), and notes that changes of less than a factor of 10 are masked by MCTS randomness.
- **Comparison:** qualitative benchmarking against Nest and Ecobee (pp. 21–24).

## What makes it stand out
- **A coherent pipeline from learning to control.** Forecasting (what the user wants), physics (what it costs) and search (best compromise) are connected through one utility function and shown in a single flowchart (Figure 4.4, p. 19). Each requirement in the prompt is located somewhere in that pipeline.
- **Schedule-agnostic learning.** Detecting the period by autocorrelation answers the "no matter how irregular your schedule" requirement directly, and the 9-on/5-off doctor example (p. 7) makes the need concrete.
- **Physics derived, not just asserted.** COP from an enthalpy table and a three-body heat model with the dominant concrete heat capacity (pp. 12–15) give power a real basis; the team also shows why calculus-of-variations fails (non-differentiable P), which motivates the numerical search.
- **Arguing the algorithm choice with numbers.** The 10^152 → 10^91 count and the chess/Go analogy (p. 16) justify MCTS clearly, and the authors present it honestly as a "proof of concept" with run counts and standard error (p. 17).
- **Probability of return as a soft weight.** Multiplying satisfaction by P(return) (Eq. 36, p. 21) replaces an on/off rule with a graded trade-off, and the pmf figure (p. 20) makes the idea easy to follow.
- **Testing their own design parameters.** The R-threshold scatter (p. 29) and the scenario tables with "reaction time" (pp. 30–31) tie validation back to user-facing behaviour (how many days of manual input are needed).
- **Elegant multi-occupant rule.** Product of Gaussians vs sum (p. 28) is a small, well-argued modeling insight.
- **Balanced comparison.** They concede Nest is more transparent and that their system may need up to three days of manual changes to make a permanent shift (p. 22).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** four paragraphs: context and framing as a constrained multi-objective problem → single-zone method chain (autocorrelation, weighted average, satisfaction, thermodynamics, utility, MCTS) → multi-zone extension → test results with numbers (< 1°C error, learns a new trend in about two days, 10% to more than 30% power reduction) (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** News Release → Introduction & Restatement → Assumptions & Variables → Single-zone (1a model, 1b evaluation) → Multi-zone (Problem 2) → Robustness & Sensitivity → Strengths & Weaknesses → References → code and data appendices.
- **Devices:** table of contents (p. 2); assumption–justification pairs; variable list; section headings labelled by task ("Model (1a)", "Evaluation (1b)"); two flowcharts (start-up, full algorithm); pmf bar chart; illustrated zoned-house figure (p. 26); comparison sub-sections each ending in an "Evaluation" paragraph (pp. 21–24); error tables with consistent metrics (MAE, max error, MPE, n, reaction time); numbered strengths and weaknesses (pp. 31–32).
- **News release:** placed first in the paper (p. 3), styled as a product launch with an invented company, CEO and chief scientist quote, and bold sub-heads (Learning ability, Energy-saving feature, Holistic consideration). Technical results become plain claims ("within 1°C", "up to 30% of energy").
- **Phrasing:**
  - "Fortunately, 10^91 is not large compared to the number of possible games of chess or games of go." (p. 16)
  - "Intuitively, the user does not care about the temperature when he is not at home!" (p. 21)
  - "Nest is superior to our learning algorithm in terms of transparency." (p. 22)
  - "Unlike its competitors, ThermostaX does not assume anything about its users' schedules and can achieve the same prediction accuracy for users with different schedules." (p. 3)

## Notes for reuse
- The energy-reduction percentages (14.6% on p. 17; Table 6.4 on p. 31; the climate-zone ranges on p. 24) are not accompanied by an explicit baseline (e.g., holding the preferred temperature constantly), and the climate-zone figures on p. 24 are stated as "theoretically" achievable without showing their derivation. An imitator should define the comparison baseline and show how such numbers are computed.
- All accuracy results come from self-generated data whose generating rules are only partly described (the authors acknowledge they cannot run field tests, p. 24); the < 1°C error demonstrates internal consistency rather than real-world performance.
