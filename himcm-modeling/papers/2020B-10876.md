# 2020B-10876: Limited Budget, Unlimited Future

## At a glance
The team turns the conservation-funding problem into a **scheduling problem under an annual budget cap**: all 48 projects cannot start at once, and **delay is costly** because the species' feasibility of success falls while it waits. They define each project's cost effectiveness as expected benefit per dollar, B·S(k)/P, where S declines with the start year k. They maximize the sum of cost effectiveness over a timetable that never exceeds the annual budget. The search uses random priority orders (Monte Carlo) decoded greedily into start years. With about $1.05M per year, 47 projects finish within 31 years (Plant-415 has to be set aside). A budget of $1.71M (1.3× current giving) is identified as the point of diminishing returns (pp. 1, 7–17).

## How they read the problem
- Names three conflicting objectives (save more species, lower the annual fundraising burden, shorten delays) and resolves the conflict with one composite index, **integrated cost effectiveness** (pp. 5–6).
- Introduces the key modeling insight that **postponing a project lowers its chance of success**, which is what makes scheduling matter (Assumption 3, p. 3; Section 5).
- Separates three outputs the Board needs: a **fundraising schedule** (money per year), a **timetable** (which projects run in which year), and a **priority order** (pp. 4, 6).
- Uses the data to motivate the design: three uniqueness levels, four benefit levels, an outlier project (Plant-415) and costs that fall over each project's life (Fig. 3, p. 6).

## Models and methods
- **Objective and constraints.** Cost effectiveness R_i(k_i) = B_i·S_i(k_i)/P_i. Maximize Σ R_i subject to Σ_i f_i(j − k_i + 1) ≤ u_j for every year j (spending each year within the budget limit), with each project funded continuously once started (Eqs. 1–11, pp. 7–8).
- **Feasibility decay.** Survival probability vs population size is fitted as S = α ln N + b (α = 0.5582, b = −2.4252). The fit uses Münzbergová's data for one plant, rescaled to a minimum viable population of 500 (the 50/500 rule) (Tables 2–3, pp. 8–9). Population declines exponentially, N = N₀e^{rt}, with r = −0.02 taken from a field study (p. 10). Combining the two gives a linear penalty: S(j) = S(1) − 0.0112(j − 1) (Eq. 18, p. 10). This turns a biology assumption into one interpretable number per year of delay.
- **Solution method.** A priority order determines a unique timetable: each project starts as early as possible once all earlier projects have started and the budget allows it. Because 48! orders cannot be enumerated, random permutations are sampled and the best is kept. The best value stops improving after about 2,200 samples (Fig. 9, pp. 10–12).
- **Worked demo.** A 4-project toy example with colored cost blocks shows how one order is decoded into a schedule (Figs. 6–8, pp. 11–12).

## Data
- COMAP's 48-project dataset: benefit, uniqueness, feasibility and 25 years of costs.
- The budget limit comes from FRPCE's 2019 annual report ($1,316,040 of giving), assuming at most half goes to these 48 plants, which gives $1,052,800 (p. 13).
- Budget growth of $100,800 per year comes from the 2018–2019 change (Eq. 20, p. 14).
- Biological parameters come from cited studies (pp. 8–10).

## Assumptions
Five assumptions with justifications (pp. 3–4). Examples:
- Funds raised each year equal that year's project costs; nothing is carried over, because donors expect funds to be used (Assumption 1).
- Postponement keeps cost and benefit unchanged but lowers feasibility, at the same rate for all species (Assumption 3).
- A project must be funded continuously until it ends; an interrupted project exposes the plants to threats again (Assumption 4).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- A convergence plot shows the Monte Carlo search approaching a stable best value (Fig. 9, p. 12).
- Sensitivity of the objective to the population growth rate r (Fig. 14), to the constant budget level from 0.5× to 3× current giving (Fig. 15), and to the yearly budget increase (Fig. 17). Fluctuations are attributed to the randomness of the search (pp. 15–18).
- The budget sweep is turned into a **recommendation**: the knee of the curve at $1.71M per year, with its own timetable (Fig. 16, pp. 16–17).

## What makes it stand out
- **Delay has a price.** Linking postponement to falling feasibility through a population model gives the scheduling problem its real trade-off (pp. 8–10).
- **A clean decision encoding.** Searching over priority orders and decoding each one greedily into a feasible timetable guarantees budget feasibility and is easy to explain (pp. 10–11).
- **Scenario thinking for the client:** constant, growing and optimized budgets, plus a quantified answer to "how much more should we raise?" (1.3×) (pp. 13–17).
- **Outlier handling stated plainly.** Plant-415 is excluded under a flat budget because its first-year cost is twice the limit, and it is reinstated when the budget grows (pp. 13–14).
- **Visual outputs a board can read:** timetable heatmaps and annual-budget-vs-limit charts (Figs. 10–13, 16).

## Notes for reuse
- The objective adds up per-project ratios B·S/P, which favors cheap projects even when an expensive project would save more benefit for the money spent. The paper itself notes that the model "prioritized the less costly projects" (p. 13). If you reuse this objective, also consider total expected benefit under the budget, and compare the two schedules.
- A single decline rate r = −0.02, from one non-Florida species, drives the delay penalty for every project (p. 10). The sensitivity sweep on r helps; with more time, species-specific rates would be better (as the paper's own weaknesses list says).
