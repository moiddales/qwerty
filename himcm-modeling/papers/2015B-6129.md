# 2015B-6129: Logistic Safety Index with Genetic Calibration

## At a glance
The team maps crime rates into a 0–100 safety index using monotone logistic functions, with crime severity categories and weights calibrated by a genetic algorithm. The simple model uses seven major felonies and reproduces published city rankings; an extended model uses My City’s detailed offenses to compare districts. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They insist that a bounded index is easier to explain than an unbounded total. The paper separates a portable seven-felony model from a detailed local model, then tests how closely rankings agree with published lists. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Each crime category contributes a decreasing logistic function of rate; weighted contributions sum to the index. A genetic algorithm searches function parameters. Beat numbers define districts for the extended model, and punishment severity supplies weights (pp. 4–30).

## Data
The paper uses My City crime data, published city rankings and punishment references. The primary dataset is the two-week My City police report. Teams group offense descriptions and combine it with survey fear weights, crime-cost references, FBI comparison distributions, sentencing data or published city rankings. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes the two-week reports are a usable sample, crime categories can be grouped consistently, and severity can be represented by fear, cost, sentence or a calibrated response. Population or comparison-city denominators are treated as exposure proxies. Each paper flags underreporting and external-data limits (pp. 4–12). Its central choice is bounded logistic safety functions, supported by genetic algorithm weight fit; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The paper compares rankings with two published lists and tests parameter changes and district groupings (pp. 23–39). It reports My City’s index near 43.28 in the simple model and discusses why the extended model is not portable.

## What makes it stand out
The bounded index, monotonicity argument and genetic calibration make the score intuitive while retaining flexibility. Separating simple and extended use cases is excellent scope control.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary and mayoral report state the index scale and ranking before formulas. Tables show logistic parameters, city comparisons and district results; the appendices document the algorithm (pp. 1–3, 20–45).

## Notes for reuse
- Genetic calibration can fit the reference list without proving causal validity. An imitator should use cross-validation, publish the objective function, and treat the 43.28 result as a model score rather than a literal probability.
