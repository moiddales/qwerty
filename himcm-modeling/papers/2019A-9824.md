# 2019A-9824: Free Charging in Public Places

## At a glance
Team 9824 builds a compact forecasting-and-costing framework for free public charging. Phone users are forecast with a linear least-squares trend and electric vehicles with a quadratic trend; the reported 2020 growth rates are 8.45% and 23.38%, respectively (p. 1). Those forecasts feed requirements for phone/laptop connections and EV outlets. Costs include electricity, purchase, installation, maintenance, land and (for commercial places) additional customer profit generated by the service. The same accounting is instantiated for a coffee shop, shopping mall and school, showing that commercial sites can sometimes offset costs through increased sales while schools mainly provide a public service. The final section turns the equations into practical levers: prices or advertising and outlet ratios (pp. 7–23).

## How they read the problem
They treat “free” as free to the visitor but not to the host. The analysis is deliberately limited to phones, laptops and EVs, and to data after 2010 for consistency (p. 6). Public places are grouped by function, then one representative site is selected for each: a coffee shop for a commercial indoor site, a shopping mall for EV-focused retail, and UCLA for a school/university (pp. 4–5, 14–18). The team separates demand growth (how many devices need energy) from service design (how many outlets and EV stations are required), then separates expenditure from possible revenue. This decomposition keeps the equations readable and permits a different set of terms for a school.

## Models and methods
The trend model uses least-squares regression on annual phone-user and EV counts. A linear fit is used for phones and a quadratic for EVs, with predicted counts and percentage increases passed to the cost model (pp. 7–10). The team then defines requirements using a 10:1 EV-to-connection target and representative charging rates, capacity and utilization. For mobile devices, phone ownership and user growth determine the number of charging connections; the analysis uses a representative phone battery and charging rate (pp. 9–11).

The cost model is an accounting identity rather than an optimization black box. Mobile-device cost Pcost combines purchase and installation, operating electricity and maintenance. EV cost Ecost adds the analogous equipment, construction, electricity and land components. For commercial sites, potential profit depends on additional visitors or purchases attributed to free charging, so the net cost can be negative. Schools remove the profit terms because their objective is convenience for students and faculty (pp. 11–14, 18).

Sensitivity is presented graphically. In cafés, α represents the fraction of customers attracted by charging; the authors show profit exceeds cost once α is above about 0.011. In malls, α and β₂ (an outlet/charging parameter) trade off customer attraction against station expense; a break-even attraction of about 0.28 is reported in one scenario (pp. 20–22).

## Data
Phone and EV series from 2010–2018 are tabulated and fitted. Equipment prices, electricity rates and maintenance values are taken from cited reports and company data. Site data include representative customer or student counts, car occupancy, operating hours, and usage ratios. The paper acknowledges that some coefficients for real public places are unavailable, so representative values are used (pp. 6, 23, appendices 26–31).

## Assumptions
The team assumes post-2010 data are sufficiently consistent, demand trends continue over the forecast horizon, and phones/EVs represent the relevant charging demand (p. 6, p. 23). It uses representative battery capacity and utilization, a 10:1 EV-to-outlet target, and average occupancy in EVs (pp. 9–11, 17). Commercial charging may increase purchases; schools have no corresponding profit. These assumptions are visible next to the equations, which helps a reader replace them.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The linear and quadratic fits are plotted against observations, and the paper reports the resulting 2019–2020 counts and growth rates (pp. 8–10). Scenario graphs vary attraction, utilization and outlet parameters rather than reporting only one point estimate (pp. 20–22). The strengths/weaknesses section explicitly notes missing site-specific data and the limited device scope (p. 23). A useful robustness practice is the separate school equation: removing revenue terms tests whether the cost result depends on a commercial assumption.

## What makes it stand out
- Forecasts are directly connected to requirements and dollars; the reader can follow a device count into an outlet count and then into cost.
- The accounting makes “who pays?” concrete. Commercial hosts can use additional sales or prices, while schools accept a deficit as the cost of service.
- Three site archetypes demonstrate transfer without pretending that one universal equation captures every place.
- Break-even plots turn policy choices into interpretable thresholds: advertising/attraction α, outlet ratios and price changes.
- The paper is transparent about omitted devices and uncertain coefficients, making the framework easier to adapt.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary gives the forecast method and two headline growth rates before presenting costs (p. 1). The newspaper article starts from a dead phone and asks who pays, then explains demand, costs and initiatives without equations (p. 2). The technical paper uses a repeated pattern—definition, formula, representative scenario, figure—so the reader can compare coffee shops, malls and schools. Figures 13–16 show cost/profit surfaces and make sensitivity legible (pp. 20–22). Appendices retain MATLAB calculations and source data while the main text remains focused (pp. 26–31).

## Notes for reuse
- Refit trends when newer observations or a different region are available; extrapolated linear/quadratic curves are scenario tools.
- State the source and units of site coefficients explicitly, since the authors identify these as the largest uncertainty (p. 23).
