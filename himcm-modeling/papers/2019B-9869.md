# 2019B-9869: The Mathematical Model for The Bottle Battles

## At a glance
Team 9869 frames a PET water-bottle ban as a market-substitution problem. Removing single-serving plain water changes the shares of tap/free water, large bottles, soda, juice, coffee/tea and alcohol; those substitutions create economic, environmental, health and welfare consequences. A utility model and marginal substitution rate describe consumer choices, while polynomial fitting estimates replacement patterns for Concord and San Francisco. The authors then build an entropy-weight evaluation system that scores alternative ban policies: distributing refillable bottles ranks first (81.35), a PET tax second (70.97), a broad bottled-drink ban third (69.01), and a vending-machine ban last (31.57) in their airport-based comparison (pp. 1–2, 5–19). Their strongest lesson is to model unintended substitution explicitly rather than equating “fewer PET bottles” with an automatically positive outcome.

## How they read the problem
They list positive and negative impacts before selecting measurable indicators. The paper’s six indicator groups are economy, convenience, health, environmental protection, public welfare and flavor; each group is broken into quantities such as spending, carbon, recycling, sugar and jobs (pp. 4–7, 14–16). A ban is therefore a change in a beverage market, not only a waste intervention. Concord and San Francisco are treated as empirical cases with different populations and sales histories, while an airport is treated as a high-throughput location with different beverage access and emergency needs (pp. 9–13).

## Models and methods
The utility section represents direct drinking water and substitute beverages with a utility surface. A marginal replacement rate describes how much of one beverage consumers trade for another while holding utility approximately constant (pp. 8–10). The team uses observed Concord and San Francisco sales data to estimate substitution and fits a polynomial surface g(x,y) to approximate the relationship (pp. 9–13). This is a pragmatic bridge from economic theory to limited local data.

For comprehensive policy evaluation, raw indicators are normalized. Entropy weights are calculated from dispersion in the data, then weighted indicator scores are aggregated for each ban policy. Radar charts compare policies such as a broad ban, a vending-machine ban and targeted restrictions (pp. 14–16). Sensitivity analysis perturbs weights and model parameters to see whether the ranking is stable (p. 17).

## Data
Sources include IBWA industry reports, beverage sales, consumer safety and health publications, municipal solid-waste data and energy information (p. 5, pp. 20–22). Concord and San Francisco sales series are plotted from 2008–2019 (p. 9). The airport section uses drinking-station distribution and beverage-access assumptions to represent a location where travelers may have less time and fewer alternatives (pp. 11–14). The appendix records data tables and fitting code.

## Assumptions
Group demand and macro market conditions are treated as predictable; indicator relationships are summarized by smooth functions; and a policy’s effects can be represented by beverage-share changes (p. 6). The team assumes that utility is maintained through substitution and that the selected indicators capture the principal effects. They also recognize that disasters and inaccessible tap water can require bottled water, which motivates staged or conditional policy rather than a universal immediate ban (pp. 1–2, 17–19).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The model is checked by comparing fitted surfaces with observed city data, then by applying the resulting substitutions to Concord and San Francisco. Radar charts make the multi-indicator scores visible. A sensitivity figure tests parameter changes and shows whether a policy remains preferable (pp. 9–10, 16–17). The strengths section emphasizes competitive-market substitution and stability; the limitations section notes data availability and indicator simplification (p. 19).

## What makes it stand out
- The substitution mechanism captures the possibility that people switch to sugary drinks or alcohol, creating health costs.
- Entropy weighting reduces dependence on one subjective set of weights while retaining a transparent indicator hierarchy.
- City and airport cases share a model but use different access and consumption assumptions.
- Policy promotion is staged: community infrastructure and education support broader adoption instead of treating a ban as one binary action.
- Radar and sensitivity plots help a nontechnical reader see tradeoffs.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary sheet announces the market-substitution idea and lists six impact dimensions before giving recommendations (p. 1). The article uses headings and figures to make a technical issue readable (pp. 1–2). The main paper’s sequence—indicators, data, utility, city cases, airport, evaluation, promotion—matches the causal logic. Tables of sources and indicator definitions (pp. 5–7, 14–16) reduce ambiguity, while the appendix preserves code (pp. 20–22).

## Notes for reuse
- Define emergency exceptions and access conditions before generalizing a bottle ban; water availability is not uniform (pp. 1–2).
- Validate substitution rates in the target community; polynomial fits should not be extrapolated far beyond observed sales (pp. 9–13).
