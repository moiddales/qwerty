# 2018A-8874: How to evaluate various kinds of roller coaster for customers?

## At a glance
The team organized the specifications under three criteria (excitement, safety, innovativeness) and weighted them by blending subjective weights from a questionnaire, solved with Logarithmic Fuzzy Preference Programming (LFPP) on triangular fuzzy comparison matrices, with objective entropy weights (pp. 3, 10–18). Missing values were filled partly with physics (energy conservation for speed and drop, a centripetal-force relation for G-force) and partly with the EM algorithm (pp. 7–9). Its most instructive feature is a self-correction: the first ranking agreed poorly with two online lists, the team traced this to truncating standardized scores at ±1 (which capped exactly the record-breaking coasters), removed the truncation, and obtained a list much closer to the online rankings (pp. 3, 22–24). The app FUN ROLLER combines survey-based personalization with maps, ticketing, community and a voice assistant (pp. 24–32).

## How they read the problem
- They assumed users care about three broad things, "safety, excitement and innovation" (p. 5), and built a hierarchy in which some variables appear under more than one criterion with different meanings: high top speed adds excitement but reduces safety, so speed is scored separately in each role (p. 15; Fig. 1, p. 16). This yields 16 scored factors from 11 variables.
- They argued that the problem calls for a balance: objective data, but weights that reflect "potential users' priorities and needs" (pp. 1, 14). The stated goal is "a balance between subjectivity and objectivity" (p. 3).
- They noted from the data that the given speed is a *top* speed, so speed, length and duration cannot be linked by simple kinematics (p. 7), which steered their imputation choices.

## Models and methods
- **Physics-based imputation.** Ignoring friction and air resistance, the top speed at the bottom of the drop satisfies v = √(2gh), used to fill speed from drop (p. 7). For G-force, the reading M at the top-speed point on a curve satisfies v²/r + g = Mg; radii back-calculated from coasters with known G-force are regressed on height (r = 2.4652h − 189.01), which then gives M for coasters with missing G-force (p. 8).
- **EM imputation** in IBM SPSS Statistics 25 for duration and vertical angle, where "we cannot get a specific equation" (p. 9).
- **Error correction and coding.** Type entries reading wood/steel reset to sit down; other rows corrected after online checks; construction coded wood 1/steel 2, type sit-down 1/inverted 2/others 3 (pp. 9–10).
- **LFPP.** Pairwise judgements become triangular fuzzy numbers (Table 7, p. 11); following Mikhailov's approach, weights solve a nonlinear program maximizing the minimum membership λ in log space, with non-negative deviation variables to guarantee feasibility; w_i = exp(x_i)/Σ exp(x_j) (pp. 11–13). Fuzzy matrices are given for the three criteria and for the factors within each criterion (Tables 8–11, p. 17), and solved with MATLAB's fmincon (pp. 34–38).
- **Entropy weights** for nine numeric variables; construction and type are excluded because their codes are not ratio-scaled (p. 15). Weights are in Table 14 (p. 18).
- **Blending.** Final weight = b·entropy + (1 − b)·LFPP, with LFPP deliberately larger (p. 15); the code uses 0.2 and 0.8 (pp. 38–39). Factor values are z-scores, initially truncated to [−1, 1] (pp. 15, 19).
- **Comparison metrics.** Kendall tau distance is defined (p. 15); a "consistency between head and tail" index S = (x + y)/(0.4m), counting agreement in the top and bottom 20%, is used to compare lists (p. 23).

## Data
COMAP data, corrected and completed as above; missing-data examples and counts are shown as screenshots (Tables 1–2, p. 7). Weights depend on a questionnaire that "hundreds of people" answered, mainly on type and construction preferences (p. 16). Online comparison lists: Coaster Buzz and a combined steel-plus-wood "wiki 2018" list (p. 22).

## Assumptions
Three numbered assumptions, each with an "Explanation" (p. 6): data are correct and stable over a couple of years; there are no children's or family coasters in the data; and the online ranking systems do not change over two years. A symbols table follows (p. 6).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Sensitivity:** each of the 16 factor weights is raised by 20% and by 50% (others renormalized), and a per-factor measure of ranking change is plotted (Figs. 6–7, p. 21), identifying which weights matter most.
- **External comparison and model repair** (pp. 22–24): the initial agreement was low; the team checked whether the two online lists agree with each other (high head–tail consistency), then looked for what in *their* model penalized well-known coasters. Two cases (Fury 325 and Soaring Dragon & Dancing Phoenix, Tables 21–22, p. 23) showed factor scores capped at 1. Removing the cap produced a list closer to the online ones (Table 23, p. 24). Location bias of the online lists is also discussed (p. 22).

## What makes it stand out
- **Diagnosing a disagreement instead of explaining it away** (pp. 3, 22–24): the comparison with online rankings became a test that found a real modelling artefact, and the summary reports the fix openly.
- **Physics used for data completion** (pp. 7–8): energy conservation and centripetal acceleration give mechanistic relations for filling speed, drop and G-force, with regression used only where physics leaves a free parameter (curve radius).
- **Dual-role variables** (p. 15): letting speed or vertical angle count positively for excitement and separately for safety is a neat way to express trade-offs within a linear score.
- **Fuzzy pairwise judgements** (pp. 10–13): triangular fuzzy numbers with LFPP acknowledge the vagueness of survey comparisons and avoid AHP's consistency fixes.
- **Explicit subjective–objective blend** (pp. 14–15): stating which share of the weight comes from users and which from data dispersion makes the "objective" claim transparent.
- **An ambitious, coherent app vision** (pp. 24–32): onboarding survey, re-weighting from behaviour (browsing 20%, ticket reservation 80%, p. 28), a 300 km "best near me" map list (p. 29), crowd-sourced data correction (p. 29) and a conversational assistant example (p. 32).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary/abstract:** the news release occupies the front pages (pp. 1–2) and the abstract is on p. 3. The abstract is structured as model → weighting method → comparison result and the repair → app features; it narrates the model correction as a finding.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction → Restatement → Assumptions → Symbols → Data preprocessing (filling v, G-force, other data, error correcting) → New evaluation system (LFPP, entropy, combination, Kendall tau; solving; simulation; sensitivity) → Compare and improve ranking → App → Conclusion → References → MATLAB code.
- **Devices:** hierarchy diagram of criteria and factors (Fig. 1, p. 16); styled weight tables per criterion; pie and bar charts of weights (Figs. 3–5, p. 20); three-column ranking comparison tables before and after the fix (Tables 20 and 23); numbered, icon-headed app feature sections with full-screen mock-ups.
- **News release:** written as a startup product launch ("FUNROLLER CO. Ltd., a startup composed of 4 ordinary Shanghai high school students", p. 1) with a quote, a plain-language contrast with subjective and single-metric rankings, and a feature tour; no numbers.
- **Phrasing:** "In rare cases which the ranking is objective, the scope of ranking may be too parochial, as these ranks only focus on one detail of the roller coasters" (p. 1); "The ranking system was modified by cancelling the upper and lower limits, and then it shows much better consistency with online rankings." (p. 3).

## Notes for reuse
- The questionnaire that sets most of the weight (LFPP share 0.8) is described only as answered by "hundreds of people" (p. 16). Its questions, sample and the conversion of answers into fuzzy numbers are not documented, so the criterion weights (64.6/22.8/12.6%) cannot be checked.
- The before/after comparison lists rank only the 43 coasters common to both online lists (p. 22), and the fix was judged by agreement with those subjective lists. A full 300-coaster Top 10 after the fix is not reported, so the official Top 10 (Table 19, p. 19) and the improved list measure different things.
