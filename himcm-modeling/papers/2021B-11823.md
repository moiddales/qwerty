# 2021B-11823: Untitled

## At a glance
The team's organizing idea is to **work in volume rather than elevation**. They average the National Park Service capacity tables for 1935 and 2010 and fit a quadratic volume–elevation curve (R² = 0.9975). This converts every annual mean elevation into a volume. After IQR outlier cleaning, a **percentage-deviation drought index** Iₖ = (S − S₀)/S₀ is computed and binned into five severity classes, from "no drought" (≥ −10%) to "extreme drought" (< −80%). The index finds three droughts (1954–56, 1964–68, 2004–onward) and shows the current one as deepest and longest (p. 15). An ARMA workflow with stationarity tests, information-criteria order selection and in-sample RMSE produces monthly forecasts out to 2050 for both data windows, shown as plots. Part 3 classifies cities as "water-starved" or "capital-starved" and optimizes a recycling-rate/cost trade-off. For the Boulder case study the result is a 73.6% recovery rate, Score 86 and cost $273,473 (p. 23).

## How they read the problem
- A colored workflow chart under "Our work" (p. 6) shows the whole pipeline: data integration → factor identification → relationship check → volume conversion → drought index → long/short-term prediction → wastewater scheme.
- Part 1b is read as "check Table 1 with a simple solid". Consecutive rows of Table 1 are treated as frustums of a cone, and the result counts as acceptable if the percentage error is < 5% (pp. 10–11).
- They argue that volume is "more intuitive and accurate than the elevation" for judging drought (article, p. 2). They therefore build the drought criterion on storage relative to the long-run average (p. 13).
- In Part 3 they recognize that local priorities differ. Water-scarce cities maximize the recycling score under a cost cap, and cash-poor cities minimize cost subject to a minimum score (p. 23).

## Models and methods
- **Frustum check (1b).** The lake between two contour elevations is modeled as a frustum, with the two areas taken from Table 1 (pp. 10–11). Assumption 3 (straight-sided "riverbed") justifies the shape. The team observes that the error grows with the height of the slice, because more bed is approximated as straight (p. 11).
- **Volume conversion and drought index (2a).** The 1935 and 2010 NPS capacity tables are averaged "to balance out the data from the water abundant times and drought times" and fitted with V = 195.87x² − 33364x + 1.444×10⁸ (p. 13). Annual mean volumes for 1935–2021 are then cleaned with Q1/Q3 ± 1.5·IQR. 1935 is removed as an outlier, and 1936 is removed by reasoning about the reservoir's filling (pp. 13–15). S₀ is the cleaned mean, and Iₖ is classified using the thresholds in Fig 4.3 (p. 15), which also gives the equivalent elevation and volume bands for each class.
- **Forecasts (2b).** Monthly levels are first-differenced, tested with ADF, and an ARMA order is selected by AIC/BIC/HQIC. The fit is checked in-sample (RMSE values shown in the figure headers, p. 19), then forecast forward for 29 years (pp. 16–20). The rationale is that time series suit "the only non-hypothetical data we got", the elevation record (p. 16). The strengths section mentions a back-test using 1937–2010 to predict 2011–2021 (p. 24).
- **Recycling optimization (3b).** Score measures the share of the supply gap filled by recycled water. Cost uses tiered unit prices of $0.38, $0.45 and $0.53 per m³ for recovery bands 0–50%, 50–70% and 70–80% (p. 22). Cities are classified by per-capita consumption (>120 L/day means water-starved) and per-capita GDP (p. 22). The appendix code solves the problem with SciPy COBYLA (pp. 30–31).

## Data
Attachment elevations are combined with NPS capacity tables to make the elevation→volume conversion. Literature and news sources supply the qualitative factor weights (Climate Hot Map, Lutus). Case-study inputs (per-capita use of 150 L/day, wastewater 914,380 m³/yr) come from city and reference websites (p. 23). Data cleaning is explicit and justified both statistically (IQR) and historically (dam completion) (pp. 14–15). The appendix code fills missing or zero cells with column means (p. 27).

## Assumptions
There are three general assumptions with justifications (p. 6): the capacity data and fitted trend line are reliable; oil, plants and animals have negligible effect on the flows; the riverbed is smooth so a frustum applies. A two-page variables table follows (p. 7). Assumptions specific to the forecasting and planning steps are stated inside the sections, e.g. the city-type thresholds.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Percentage-error test** of the frustum formula against Table 1 with an explicit 5% acceptance rule (p. 11).
- **R² = 0.9975** for the volume–elevation fit (p. 13).
- **Time-series diagnostics:** ADF test tables, information criteria, in-sample comparison plots with RMSE (pp. 17–19). A back-test on 2011–2021 is claimed in the summary and strengths (pp. 1, 24).
- **3-D sensitivity surface** of the final score over recycle rate (40–80%) and money spent ($240k–$320k). The score is more sensitive to recycle rate, with an inflection near 74%, which they connect to the point where the shortfall is filled (p. 23).
- Strengths/weaknesses, then "Conclusion and Future work" (p. 24).

## What makes it stand out
- **Changing the measured quantity.** Converting elevation to volume before defining drought means the index measures the water actually stored, not feet. The conversion is made reproducible with a fitted polynomial (p. 13).
- **A graded drought index.** Five severity classes with explicit Iₖ cut-offs, and matching elevation and volume bands (Fig 4.3, p. 15), give a richer answer than a yes/no threshold. Fig 4.4 colors the whole volume history by class (p. 16).
- **Transparent data cleaning.** The IQR rule and the decision to drop the filling years are written out step by step with the numbers (pp. 13–15).
- **The comparison uses three named dimensions.** Overall trend, trough depth and duration (3 → 5 → 18 years) are compared one by one (p. 15).
- **A careful forecasting workflow.** Stationarity tests, order selection, in-sample RMSE and a stated back-test (pp. 16–19, 24).
- **The plan is shaped to the decision-maker.** Separate optimization objectives for water-starved and capital-starved cities reflect the problem's prompt about leaders' priorities (pp. 22–23).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** a context sentence, then one paragraph each for factors and frustum verification, drought index, forecasting (naming the tests and the back-test), and the recycling plan with its two indices and sensitivity finding. Keywords follow. It contains few numeric results.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, restatement, our work flowchart) → Assumptions and Variables → Factors and relationships → Drought Definition Model → ARMA Prediction Model → Wastewater Reuse Planning → Sensitivity Analysis → Strengths and Weaknesses → Conclusion and Future work → References → code appendices.
- **Devices:** a full-pipeline flowchart (p. 6), a variables table, worked percentage-error lines, a drought-class table that pairs index, elevation and volume, a color-banded history chart, ADF result tables, and a 3-D sensitivity surface.
- **Letter/memo/article:** a newspaper-style page dated November 16, 2021, titled "DROUGHTS OF LAKE MEAD" (p. 2). Four numbered steps (I analysis of past data, II forecast, III formulate formula, IV end by protection) each have an icon in a "Steps Towards Solution" sidebar. It ends by advocating water conservation beyond recycling. The tone is plain-language and step-by-step.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "If the subtraction of the two volumes on the left is close to the answer derived from the formula on the right, that is, the percentage error is less than 5%, the formula is proved to be applicable and successful." (p. 11)
  - "In order to balance out the data from the water abundant times and drought times, we averaged out the capacity data of the two set at the same controlled elevation." (p. 13)
  - "Finally, in terms of drought duration, each drought is longer than the last, it lasts for 3 years in 1954, 5 years in 1964, and lasts for 18 years in the drought that began in 2004." (p. 15)
