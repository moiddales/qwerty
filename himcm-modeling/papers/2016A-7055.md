# 2016A-7055: Eighteen-Division Triathlon Schedule

## At a glance
The team divides 2,000 athletes into 18 gender/age/status groups, samples speeds from normal distributions, and evaluates 15 wave schedules. A weighted score balances congestion, road closure and finishers; the recommended schedule closes roads from 8:00 to about 1:30 and maximizes finishers within 5.5 hours. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They preserve familiar divisions, then compare wave timing patterns and cutoff rules. Race-distance changes are explored only after selecting a best and worst baseline schedule, which prevents a distance recommendation from being detached from scheduling context. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
NetLogo agents traverse swim, bike and run segments; individual times are sampled rather than fixed. Schedule score combines passing congestion, closure duration and nonfinishers. Fifteen schedules and distance variants are simulated (pp. 4–28).

## Data
The paper uses HiMCM triathlon dataset and synthetic schedule scenarios. The source is the prior-triathlon spreadsheet of age, gender, category, segment and transition times. Teams derive quartiles, clusters or fitted distributions and generate simulated entrants and schedules. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes prior-race times represent this year’s entrants, segment speeds can be summarized by groups or distributions, and wave starts do not change individual ability. Congestion is represented by passing, density or speed overlap; road closure is a hard limit or an explicitly weighted term (pp. 4–10). Its central choice is NetLogo agent simulation, supported by normal speed sampling; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
Repeated simulation trials compare four schedules and three timing patterns; cutoff-time and distance sensitivity plots show how congestion and finishers change (pp. 23–35).

## What makes it stand out
The probabilistic athlete generation is more realistic than deterministic averages, and the composite score makes the Mayor’s competing objectives explicit. The paper also surfaces worst-case schedule behavior.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary table gives the recommended waves; the letter explains the policy in plain language, followed by NetLogo figures and sensitivity charts (pp. 1–5, 23–37).

## Notes for reuse
- Normal speed distributions and fixed course geometry are assumptions. An imitator should report Monte Carlo uncertainty and ensure the chosen weights reflect the Mayor’s priorities.
