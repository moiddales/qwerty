# 2024A-15926: TOPSIS model for Olympic event potential

## At a glance
The team identifies 21 factors under the six IOC principles, computes entropy- and AHP-weight TOPSIS scores, and combines them 0.6/0.4. A simulated-score distribution sets an inclusion threshold of 0.45. Tests on new and established sports match observed Olympic trends. For 2032, Teqball, League of Legends and Field Handball exceed the threshold; Baseball and Squash are candidates for 2036. Single-factor, interaction, principle and weight sensitivity identify continental spread, injury probability, athlete gender ratio and inclusivity as influential (pp. 1, 7–22).

## How they read the problem
The team builds a 21-factor evaluation model under the six IOC principles, then combines two weighting routes instead of committing to one. A simulated score distribution sets an inclusion threshold of 0.45. Recent new sports and established sports are used as checks before the model scores 2032 and 2036 candidates and performs single-factor, interaction, principle and weight sensitivity (pp. 1, 7–22).

## Models and methods
Traditional TOPSIS is run twice. Entropy weighting uses dispersion in the 21-factor matrix; AHP supplies a second weighting structure. Their 0.6/0.4 weighted average is tested on simulated data, and the 0.45 threshold is chosen from the simulated-score distribution (pp. 10–15). Sensitivity then sweeps each factor, selected pairs, the six principles and the two combination weights (pp. 19–22).

## Data
The matrix contains 21 factors derived from popularity/accessibility, gender equity, sustainability, inclusivity, relevance/innovation and safety/fair play. The test set includes sport climbing, skateboarding, surfing, swimming, artistic gymnastics and fencing; future scores use estimated data for candidate sports (pp. 7–18).

## Assumptions
Scores are comparable after normalization and the six principles can be represented by the selected 21 factors. The 0.45 threshold is a decision rule calibrated on simulation, not an IOC standard. Estimated candidate data are treated as scenario values, and the AHP/entropy blend is assumed to balance judgment and dispersion (pp. 10–18).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The model reproduces a sensible distinction between recent additions and long-standing sports: all six test subjects remain above the threshold and established sports show steady scores. Sensitivity identifies continental spread, injury probability, female/male athlete ratio and inclusivity as influential; the ranking is examined under factor interactions and weight changes (pp. 15–22).

## What makes it stand out
The paper makes the threshold explicit and tests it against both new and traditional sports. Combining entropy and AHP gives two auditable views of importance, while the multi-level sensitivity analysis shows where a recommendation can move.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary gives the 21-factor construction, 0.45 threshold, candidate rankings and sensitivity findings. Tables and plots are organized by problem number, and the final IOC letter carries the same ranked recommendations into a concise policy format (pp. 1, 15–22).

The threshold is grounded in the model's own simulated score distribution rather than imported from an external rule. In the historical test, sport climbing, skateboarding and surfing all exceed 0.45, while swimming, gymnastics and fencing remain steadily above it, giving the team a check across newer and established sports (pp. 15–17). For future candidates, Teqball leads League of Legends and Field Handball for 2032, while Baseball and Squash exceed the threshold for 2036. The factor sensitivity plots identify Continental Spread, Injury Probability and F/M Athlete Ratio; the principle-level analysis finds Inclusivity most sensitive (pp. 17–22).

The combined score is intentionally not presented as a probability. It is a thresholded suitability measure, so the paper reports which candidates cross 0.45 and why rather than claiming that the model predicts an IOC vote. This distinction keeps the interpretation aligned with the available data and with the sensitivity analysis (pp. 10–22).

The paper's interaction plots are useful because they show that factor effects need not be additive. A candidate can improve when two factors move together even if neither one alone crosses the threshold. Reporting single-factor, pairwise, principle-level and weight sensitivity gives readers several ways to audit that behavior (pp. 19–22).

The selected test subjects also cover both sides of the decision. New sports score above the threshold because of popularity and accessibility, while established sports remain steadily high across all six principles. This supports the authors' claim that the score captures both current appeal and durable Olympic alignment (pp. 15–18).

The final letter uses the same threshold and candidate ordering as the results section, preserving traceability from the sensitivity analysis to the recommendation (pp. 22–25).

That interpretation keeps the model faithful to the information available in the historical and estimated candidate data (pp. 17–22).

The threshold and sensitivity plots together show both the decision rule and its practical margin (pp. 17–22).

The stated threshold is therefore best read as a transparent screening rule, not a forecast probability.

## Notes for reuse
- Composite rankings inherit the choice of indicators, proxies and weights; show them and run perturbation tests before treating a rank as definitive.
- Forecasts and cost savings depend on long-horizon assumptions about prices, technology and policy; give scenario ranges.
