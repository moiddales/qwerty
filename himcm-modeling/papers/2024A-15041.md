# 2024A-15041: Hierarchical multi-criteria model for Olympic events

## At a glance
The team builds a hierarchical MCDM with six IOC criteria and 27 quantitative or qualitative subcriteria. AHP weights the main criteria and TOPSIS aggregates subcriteria upward to an overall score. Testing eleven events against known Tokyo, Paris and LA decisions gives high ranks to athletics, gymnastics and swimming and reproduces karate’s changing status. For Brisbane, skateboarding, sport climbing and breaking rank highest; the ranking remains stable under large weight perturbations (pp. 1–2, 8–25).

## How they read the problem
The team turns six IOC criteria into a hierarchy of 27 factors, then tests the hierarchy against known decisions from Tokyo 2020, Paris 2024 and Los Angeles 2028. It uses the validated hierarchy for Brisbane, where host-specific cultural and strategic factors can change scores, and finally perturbs the main weights by ±10% and ±50% (pp. 1–2, 8–25).

## Models and methods
AHP supplies weights for the six main criteria. TOPSIS is applied first within each criterion's subcriteria and then again to aggregate criterion scores, preserving the hierarchy rather than flattening all 27 factors. The output is a normalized alignment score where higher means closer to the ideal IOC profile (pp. 9–16).

## Data
The factor set includes ticket sales, digital engagement, leadership representation, environmental policy, global reach, youth appeal, injury rate and anti-doping compliance, with host-country factors such as cultural relevance. Historical inclusion/removal status for eleven SDEs supplies the validation cases (pp. 3–9, 16–20).

## Assumptions
The hierarchy assumes the six IOC criteria can be represented by the chosen 27 factors and that predefined subcriterion weights are defensible. Host-specific values are allowed to vary, while the aggregation method remains fixed. Rankings are interpreted comparatively among candidate sports, not as an absolute probability of selection (pp. 6–16).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
Known Olympic outcomes provide a direct qualitative validation: athletics, gymnastics and swimming rank consistently high, while karate's score changes across host contexts in the same direction as actual decisions. Weight perturbations of ±10% and ±50% preserve the leading core sports and recommended candidates, with popularity/accessibility and relevance/innovation most influential (pp. 16–25).

## What makes it stand out
The hierarchical aggregation matches the IOC's own layered criteria and makes host context explicit. Testing three historical Games gives the model a meaningful external check, and the large weight perturbations show which recommendations are structural rather than artifacts of one weighting choice.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary and opening letter state the six criteria, 27 factors and Brisbane recommendation. The paper uses a hierarchy diagram, factor tables, historical validation examples and sensitivity plots, then ends with a clear recommendation for the IOC (pp. 1–2, 16–28).

The historical test is more than a qualitative anecdote. The model assigns karate a high score and rank for Tokyo 2020, then lower scores and ranks for Paris 2024 and Los Angeles 2028, matching the changing host programs (pp. 16–20). For Brisbane, the team evaluates eight recently added or removed events and retains host-specific factors such as cultural relevance. The ±10% and ±50% perturbations leave athletics, gymnastics and swimming near the top and keep skateboarding, sport climbing and breaking competitive, which supports the claim that the recommendation is not driven by one exact AHP weight (pp. 20–25).

The host adaptation is a substantive part of the model. A sport can score differently when cultural relevance, audience and strategic priorities change, even though the six top-level criteria remain fixed. The paper uses this flexibility to explain why karate can rank highly for Tokyo but fall for Paris and Los Angeles, then applies the same structure to Brisbane (pp. 3–9, 16–25).

The hierarchical design supports this host adaptation because a Brisbane-specific change can be made at a subcriterion without rebuilding the top-level IOC structure. It also gives the paper a natural explanation for why popularity and innovation are influential while sustainability and safety remain necessary balancing criteria (pp. 9–25).

The validation cases also show that the model can represent removal as well as addition. Comparing karate across three Games gives a changing score rather than a fixed sport label, which is important for a framework intended to support future host decisions rather than simply rank permanent Olympic sports (pp. 16–20).

The results therefore support a conditional recommendation: Brisbane's top sports are the candidates that remain strong after host-specific factors and weight perturbations, not simply the sports with the largest global audience (pp. 20–25).

This gives the model a clear update path when a future host changes its cultural or strategic priorities (pp. 20–25).

The historical comparisons make those host effects visible rather than implicit in one final rank (pp. 16–20).

## Notes for reuse
- Composite rankings inherit the choice of indicators, proxies and weights; show them and run perturbation tests before treating a rank as definitive.
- Forecasts and cost savings depend on long-horizon assumptions about prices, technology and policy; give scenario ranges.
