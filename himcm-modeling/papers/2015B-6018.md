# 2015B-6018: Sentence-Weighted Safety and Economic Correlation

## At a glance
The team weights each major crime by median sentence, multiplies by its two-week rate per 100,000, and obtains My City’s rating 1370.93 versus an FBI metropolitan mean 1462.47. A z-score of about −0.162 places My City safer than roughly 56% of comparison areas. District and economic analyses guide extra law-enforcement placement. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They define severity from sentencing rather than an arbitrary ordinal scale. The workflow is city score, comparison distribution, correlation with economic activity, then within-city allocation. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
For each crime c, rate is multiplied by sentence-derived weight and summed. FBI metropolitan scores are approximated as normal for z-scores. Correlation and district analyses use beat/location fields to identify concentrations and candidate patrol areas (pp. 4–38).

## Data
The paper uses My City crime reports, FBI metropolitan data, U.S. Sentencing Commission. The primary dataset is the two-week My City police report. Teams group offense descriptions and combine it with survey fear weights, crime-cost references, FBI comparison distributions, sentencing data or published city rankings. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes the two-week reports are a usable sample, crime categories can be grouped consistently, and severity can be represented by fear, cost, sentence or a calibrated response. Population or comparison-city denominators are treated as exposure proxies. Each paper flags underreporting and external-data limits (pp. 4–12). Its central choice is sentence-weighted crime score, supported by z-score benchmarking; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
Normality is checked against FBI values; the paper reports mean, standard deviation, z-score and percentile. Alternative weighting and district groupings are compared in appendices (pp. 39–55).

## What makes it stand out
The severity proxy is transparent and reproducible, and the z-score turns a raw total into a comparative statement. The paper answers the placement question rather than stopping at a citywide number.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
Graphs of distributions, correlation plots, beat maps and appendices support the long report. The summary gives the final score and percentile up front (pp. 1–3, 32–58).

## Notes for reuse
- Sentences reflect legal practice as well as harm and can change over time. An imitator should check normality, account for underreporting, and avoid implying that a percentile is an absolute safety probability.
