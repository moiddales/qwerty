# 2023B-13694: Untitled (life-cycle emissions and costs of five bus types, Vancouver roadmap)

## At a glance
The team built every model "bottom-up" from a single bus: five bus types (diesel, CNG, hybrid, battery-electric, trolley-electric) are each run through four life stages (production, operation, maintenance, disposal) for both emissions (kgCO2eq) and city-borne cost (USD). Fleet-level results for Vancouver are obtained by multiplying per-bus results by fleet counts and separating recurring "usage" effects from one-time "instantaneous" effects of buying and retiring buses. The 10-year roadmap is found by exhaustively enumerating yearly replacement schedules (depth-first search in batches) under a yearly budget that carries over, and scoring each schedule with a normalized cost-plus-emissions index. Headline results: Vancouver breaks even on emissions after about one year of full e-bus use and then saves about 57 million kgCO2eq per year (p. 12); with 50% funding the electric fleet is cheaper than renewing the old one (p. 17); and sensitivity analysis leads to the recommendation to electrify "as quickly as finances allow" (pp. 1, 23).

## How they read the problem
- They reframed all four requirements as one comparison of non-electric and electric buses, so they decided to "model each type of bus separately, calculating the ecological impact and financial cost of a single bus over its lifespan" and then scale by fleet composition (p. 4). This one design decision is what lets the same machinery serve Tasks 1–3 and three cities.
- They kept the real mix of a fleet: five bus categories, each represented by a specific 40-foot model (Volvo B8RLE, Orion VII, Volvo B5LH, BYD K9, New Flyer Xcelsior trolley) to control for size (p. 4). Vancouver was chosen because it publishes fleet composition and grid-mix data and already runs all five types (p. 4).
- Ecological impact was scoped to 100-year global warming potential in kgCO2eq. Air-quality pollutants were explicitly set aside because they are "generally correlated to carbon emissions and more difficult to quantify under a single standard" (p. 5).
- The financial model counts only costs "directly incurred by the city" (p. 13), and the external funding is applied as a subsidy fraction S% on the purchase price of electric and trolley buses (p. 13).
- Fleet transition logic: all non-electric buses become BEVs, trolley count stays fixed. The result is 1,170 BEVs + 262 trolleys (p. 10). Trolley infrastructure expansion is excluded as too city-specific (p. 10).

## Models and methods
**Life-cycle emissions model (Task 1).** Total lifetime emissions = production + operation + maintenance + disposal (p. 5).
- *Production:* material weights per bus from Zhao et al. (2021), scaled by curb weight for the CNG and trolley models, multiplied by per-kg GWP from GREET 2022. Lithium-ion batteries are counted per kWh rather than per kg (p. 6).
- *Operation:* well-to-wheel factors (3.49 kgCO2eq/L diesel, 3.17 kgCO2eq/kg CNG) and a grid-mix-weighted electricity factor, multiplied by energy use per km and lifetime km (pp. 6–7).
- *Maintenance:* replacement tires (4 per 50,000 km) and batteries (12-year life) over a 15-year bus life (20 years for trolleys) (p. 8).
- *Disposal:* modeled as a recycling credit, weight × R% × (recycled − virgin GWP), with R% = 50% (pp. 8–9).

Rationale is stated throughout. For example, battery emissions are scaled by kWh because "battery capacity and weight are not scalable across models" (p. 6).

**Fleet application.** Yearly "usage emissions" (operation + maintenance divided by lifespan) are separated from "instantaneous emissions" (production of new BEVs plus recycling credit for retired buses). Net change after T years = C_inst + ΔC_usage·T (pp. 11–12). A waterfall chart shows each bus type's contribution (p. 12).

**Financial model (Task 2).** It mirrors the four stages but uses market prices instead of a material breakdown.
- *Purchase:* the purchase price of BEVs and trolleys is multiplied by (1 − S%).
- *Operation:* local fuel prices; BEVs are charged at nighttime electricity rates, following the Shenzhen precedent; yearly delivery charges are added.
- *Maintenance:* tires, plus batteries at $111/kWh.
- *Other items:* 315 km of trolley overhead wire maintenance, about $12.1M/yr; scrap values recovered at disposal (pp. 13–15).

**Roadmap model (Task 3).**
- *Search:* a DFS generates every combination of yearly replacement counts in increments of z that sums to the non-electric fleet. Diesel is replaced first, then CNG, then hybrids (p. 19).
- *Yearly update:* each year applies the Task 1–2 models, EIA fuel-price projections and projected battery cost declines (p. 19).
- *Budget:* a yearly budget B carries unspent money forward, and any plan that overspends is eliminated (p. 19).
- *Scoring:* surviving plans are scored by I = weighted mean of the min-max-normalized 10-year cost and emissions, with w_C = w_F = 1 (pp. 18–19).

Stated rationale for the budget: "to prevent overspending and make transition plans more realistic" (p. 19).

## Data
Nearly all parameters are researched values with numbered citations: GREET 2022 for material and part emissions, the Canada Energy Regulator for BC grid mix, BC Hydro/FortisBC/Shell Canada for local energy prices, the FTA useful-life report for lifetimes and mileage, and manufacturer sheets for fuel efficiency (pp. 6–8, 14, 25). Where model-specific data were missing they scaled from a reference bus by curb weight or price, and they say which reference was used (pp. 6, 15). For other cities they swapped in local grid mixes and energy prices (Boston: ISO New England; São Paulo: GlobalPetrolPrices/Statista) and published fleet compositions (p. 21). The few genuinely assumed quantities are the recycling rate R%, the subsidy S%, batch size z and budget B, and these are exactly the ones they vary in sensitivity analysis.

## Assumptions
There are seven numbered assumptions, each a bold one-line statement followed by a short justification paragraph (pp. 3–4). Examples:
- Only five bus types are considered because they "make up the vast majority of all bus fleets around the world" (p. 3).
- Ridership is unaffected by the bus technology, citing the Shenzhen transition (p. 3).
- Fleet size stays constant: buses are replaced one-for-one (p. 4).
- The recycled share at disposal equals the recycled share in production (p. 4).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Recycling rate R%** (Task 1): disposal credits change linearly, but overall emissions and the ranking of bus types do not change (p. 10).
- **Subsidy S% from 0–50%** (Task 2): BEVs become the cheapest bus over their lifespan at about 30% funding, and trolleys at over 40%. The e-fleet beats renewal of the original fleet once 20% is funded, and at 10% it becomes cheaper after about 5 years (pp. 17–18).
- **Index weights w_C:w_F from 0:10 to 10:0** (Task 3): the optimal plan is identical for every ratio (p. 22).
- **Yearly budget from $50M to $100M** (Task 3): larger budgets bring the transition earlier, which supports the "as fast as budget allows" conclusion (pp. 22–23).

There is no external back-test. The strengths/weaknesses section names the remaining limitations: dependence on literature data, runtime of the exhaustive search, and logistics such as retiring the oldest buses first (p. 23).

## What makes it stand out
- **One per-bus engine reused everywhere.** The per-bus, per-stage formulation (p. 4) makes the fleet results, the financial model, the roadmap and the two extra cities all straightforward reapplications. The modularity itself is claimed as a strength (p. 23).
- **Usage vs instantaneous split.** Separating the one-time emissions and costs of a transition from recurring ones (pp. 11–12, 16–17) gives a clean break-even story ("break-even" after the first year, p. 12). It also makes the fair comparison explicit: renewing the old fleet would cost about $654M anyway (p. 17).
- **City-specific physics where it matters.** Weighting by Vancouver's hydro-heavy grid mix explains why BEVs are about thirty times cleaner than diesel in operation (p. 8). Swapping in a gas-heavy grid for Boston shows the model responds to local conditions (p. 21).
- **Honest, surprising intermediate results.** The paper reports that BEVs have the highest production and maintenance emissions because of batteries (pp. 6, 8) before showing that operation dominates. This lends credibility.
- **Roadmap as constrained search, not a heuristic schedule.** Every feasible schedule under a carry-over budget is enumerated and ranked (p. 19). The spaghetti plots of candidate plans (p. 20) make the search visible.
- **Sensitivity aimed at the assumed inputs.** Each sensitivity test targets one of the few non-researched parameters (R%, S%, weights, B), and each ends with a decision-relevant sentence (pp. 10, 18, 22–23).
- **Three contrasting cities.** Vancouver (mixed fleet, clean grid), Boston (gas grid) and São Paulo (14,502 diesel buses, scaled to z = 1000 and B = $1B) show the model's range (pp. 21–22).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** seven prose paragraphs. Context, then the bottom-up framework (five types × four stages), then city choice, then ecological finding ("nearly ten times less emissions"), then financial finding, then the DFS roadmap and index, then the extension cities and the budget-sensitivity conclusion. Few numbers; the emphasis is on the method chain and conclusions (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** Introduction → Assumptions and Justifications → Setting Up (model overview, representative buses) → Ecological Modelling (Req. 1) → Financial Modelling (Req. 2) → Roadmap Modelling (Req. 3) → Strengths and Weaknesses → Conclusion → Letter (Req. 4) → References.
- **Devices:**
  - Section headings tagged with the requirement number.
  - A repeated sub-structure for each model: Variables table → Evaluation Factors → Model Construction (a–e by life stage) → Application → Sensitivity.
  - Small per-bus-type result tables after each stage.
  - Stacked bar charts and waterfall charts showing each bus type's contribution (pp. 9, 12, 16).
  - Line plots of 10-year projections and of the sensitivity results (pp. 12, 20–22).
- **Letter:** addressed to Vancouver transportation officials (p. 24). It explains the two-part model in plain language and gives the yearly replacement table under stated conditions ($50M/yr, batches of 75). It then translates results into two totals: "just under $500 million" and "just slightly over 300 million kilograms of CO2 equivalent emissions" over ten years. It also gives a general rule: transition as early as the budget allows.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "Our team constructed our model from the bottom-up, first considering the basic ecological and financial impacts of individual buses." (p. 1)
  - "The city of Vancouver would “break-even” with emissions after the first year of full e-bus usage, and it would save approximately 57 million kgCO2eq every year afterward." (p. 12)
  - "It is important to note, however, that operating the original, non-electrified fleet would still eventually require the purchase of new buses to replace old ones" (p. 17)

## Notes for reuse
- The yearly budget B ($50M for Vancouver and Boston, $1B for São Paulo) and batch size z are set without a cited source (pp. 20–21), and they drive the shape of the recommended schedule. The paper does test B (p. 22); anyone imitating the approach should likewise anchor or vary such budget figures.
