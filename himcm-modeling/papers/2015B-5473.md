# 2015B-5473: Fear, Cost, and Regional Safety Ratings

## At a glance
The team rates My City two ways: a fear model weights crime categories using victimization research, while a cost model weights counts by estimated social cost. They then partition the city into 22 police regions and combine crime probability with fear weights to produce local danger ratings. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They argue that raw counts do not equal perceived safety. The paper first cleans and groups the two-week reports, then builds city-level indices, compares the approaches, and finally maps within-city variation. The mayoral letter turns the rankings into targeted advice. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Crime categories are normalized per 1,000 people, assigned fear or cost weights, and aggregated. Regional scores multiply the probability of each category by its fear weight. Exploratory tables examine domestic status, arrests, beat distributions and time patterns (pp. 7–38).

## Data
The paper uses My City crime spreadsheet, victimization survey and crime-cost references. The primary dataset is the two-week My City police report. Teams group offense descriptions and combine it with survey fear weights, crime-cost references, FBI comparison distributions, sentencing data or published city rankings. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes the two-week reports are a usable sample, crime categories can be grouped consistently, and severity can be represented by fear, cost, sentence or a calibrated response. Population or comparison-city denominators are treated as exposure proxies. Each paper flags underreporting and external-data limits (pp. 4–12). Its central choice is fear-weighted crime index, supported by crime-cost index; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The two indices are compared for rank stability and sensitivity to weights (pp. 24–40). Regional results are checked against the overall city score; strengths and weaknesses acknowledge survey-transfer and two-week sampling issues.

## What makes it stand out
Using two normative lenses is a strong answer to the ambiguous word “safe.” The regional model preserves the city-level logic while giving the mayor a location-specific intervention list.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The paper has a contents page, exploratory data section, separate model chapters, a strength/weakness review and a mayoral letter (pp. 3, 7–43).

## Notes for reuse
- Two weeks of reports and external fear weights cannot support a definitive long-term safety label. An imitator should report uncertainty, distinguish reported incidence from true incidence, and avoid ranking beats without population exposure.
