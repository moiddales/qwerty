# 2022B-12911: Global Warming and Carbon Dioxide Levels

## At a glance
The team uses time-series methods to forecast CO2 and temperature and then models their temporal relationship. They identify March 2003, rather than March 2004, as the largest ten-year average increase. ARI(8,2) performs best and forecasts 500 ppm as early as 2048; all models reach at least 500 ppm by 2056. ARIMA(3,1,3) forecasts a 1.5°C change in 2052. Pearson correlation is 0.96, and VAR(5) with Granger tests finds strong CO2-to-temperature influence. Its 2050 forecast is 512.85 ppm and 2.01°C (pp. 1, 11–23).

## How they read the problem
They begin with the 2004 claim, then treat CO2 and temperature as time series rather than only static regressions. CO2 is forecast with Holt, ARI, IMA and ARIMA alternatives; temperature change is forecast with ARIMA(3,1,3). A Pearson correlation is followed by VAR modeling and Granger tests, so the final forecast uses lagged histories of both variables (pp. 1, 7–23).

## Models and methods
The CO2 section compares trend and differenced time-series models using model-statistic tables. ARI(8,2) is chosen because it has the best reported performance. The temperature model uses ARIMA(3,1,3) on annual changes. VAR(5) then carries five lags of both CO2 and temperature into a joint forecast. Granger tests are used to distinguish predictive direction in the sample, with strong CO2-to-temperature evidence and weaker reverse evidence (pp. 9–23).

## Data
CO2 values come from NOAA and global temperature changes from NASA GISS, with the monthly/annual aggregation documented in the paper. Sensitivity experiments add CH4, N2O and SF6 and change the sampling frequency, allowing the team to test whether the joint forecast depends on one narrow data construction (pp. 13–22).

## Assumptions
The short historical series is assumed sufficient to fit autoregressive models and to represent future lag structure. VAR uses CO2 and temperature as the principal endogenous variables; other forcings enter only in sensitivity. The forecast intervals are interpreted as useful through 2100 despite acknowledged small-data limitations (pp. 6, 20–23).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
Model statistics compare Holt, ARI, IMA and ARIMA, and the selected ARI remains stable when data size and month are changed (pp. 11–15). The VAR sensitivity section changes data size, frequency and added greenhouse gases; the paper reports narrow 95% intervals and little ranking change under these checks (pp. 20–22).

## What makes it stand out
This is the most explicit temporal analysis among the 2022B cards. The team separates univariate forecasting from the joint VAR model and uses Granger tests to address direction without claiming that a correlation alone proves causality. The reported 2050 pair—512.85 ppm and 2.01°C—gives the policy conclusion a concrete scale.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary is quantitative and direct. Tables compare every candidate model; the paper then uses a compact VAR workflow and sensitivity subsections. The conclusion connects the threshold forecasts to an energy-sector recommendation without burying the uncertainty discussion (pp. 1, 11–23).

The CO2 model comparison includes a useful robustness detail: the selected ARI(8,2) remains the most accurate when the amount of data is reduced and when annual March averages are replaced with another month (pp. 13–15). The VAR section similarly tests monthly versus annual sampling and adds CH4, N2O and SF6; the reported ranking and forecast remain broadly stable (pp. 20–22). The authors also show 95% confidence bands for the VAR forecast, while acknowledging that a small sample and omitted forcings limit how literally the bands should be read (pp. 20–23).

The paper also distinguishes forecast accuracy from causal interpretation. The ARI model is selected for CO2 forecasting, while VAR and Granger analysis are introduced only after the univariate forecasts. The authors report that the reverse temperature-to-CO2 Granger relationship is weaker, then qualify the result because the sample is small and other climate drivers are omitted (pp. 18–23).

The final VAR result is deliberately paired with caveats. Narrow confidence bands are shown through 2100, but the authors still identify small sample size and omitted variables as concerns. That combination of a clear numerical answer and an explicit uncertainty boundary is a useful pattern for long-range environmental forecasting (pp. 20–23).

The model tables are organized around a practical decision: whether a stated safety threshold will be crossed before 2050. Reporting both the earliest ARI 500-ppm date and the VAR 2050 pair gives the reader a near-term warning and a joint-system estimate, while the sensitivity pages show why those dates should be treated as model outputs rather than calendar certainties (pp. 11–23).

The paper also gives a useful model-selection habit: it reports the preferred ARI model and its alternatives before moving to the joint VAR forecast, so the reader can distinguish a univariate best fit from a multivariate policy result (pp. 11–20).

## Notes for reuse
- Long-horizon extrapolation is highly sensitive to functional form and short training histories; report a model range and back-test before presenting one date as certain.
- Correlation or Granger direction supports temporal association, not a complete causal climate model; omitted forcings remain material.
