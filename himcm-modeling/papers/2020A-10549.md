# 2020A-10549: Untitled (summer job recommender using AHP, entropy weights and k-means)

## At a glance
The team builds a "summer job evaluation system" that recommends a *cluster* of suitable jobs rather than a single best job. A user's pairwise comparisons feed an AHP hierarchy (5 criteria, 10 sub-criteria); these subjective weights are blended with objective entropy weights computed from a 22-job database, the blended weights score each job, and k-means (k = 3, chosen by an SSE elbow) on the scores returns the top cluster, after filtering out jobs that fail an age requirement. Ten fictional students with distinct priorities are run through the pipeline. A final "optimized model" automatically repairs an inconsistent AHP comparison matrix so a student does not have to re-enter preferences (an example matrix goes from CR = 0.1407 to CR = 0.025055, pp. 19–20).

## How they read the problem
- They frame the task as building a recommendation system that weights variables "based on individual abilities, preferences, and situations" (p. 4), with COVID-19 as context that makes the choice harder and makes virtual work and infection risk relevant (pp. 4, 8).
- They split variables into **deterministic** ones that gate eligibility (age requirement) and **probabilistic** ones that are traded off (income, comfort/type of job, company characteristics, risks, skills) (p. 7). This is their direct reading of the statement's "deterministic or probabilistic" language. Note that "probabilistic" here means "not predetermined/traded off", not random variables.
- They restate the task as four questions, one of which is their own addition: use the entropy method "to offset the subjectivity problem" of AHP (p. 4).
- They reframe "best job" as a *set*: "Oftentimes, we want more than the one single best job. Rather, we want a cluster of possible options." (p. 14).
- They anticipate that a real teenage user will give inconsistent pairwise judgements, and treat that as a modeling problem in its own right (pp. 6, 18).

## Models and methods
- **AHP.** Five criteria and ten sub-criteria in a three-level hierarchy (Figure 3, p. 9). The standard Saaty 1–9 scale and a random-index table are used; CI and CR are computed in MATLAB, and CR < 0.1 is used to "verify that our fictional character is realistic" (p. 11). Rationale: AHP "can display complicated selection factors in simple concepts of hierarchy, which can be accepted easily by a decision-maker" (pp. 5–6).
- **Entropy weight method.** Applied to the 22-job × 10-index matrix after min–max normalization, with indices split into increasing (e.g. salary) and decreasing (e.g. work time) types (p. 12). The resulting weights (Table 3, p. 12) are the same for every user. Rationale: to bring in "the degree of dispersion of the evaluation index" for a "more objective decision" (p. 11).
- **Combination.** They choose θ1 + θ2 = 1 to minimize Σ(θ1·w_ahp − θ2·w_entr)² by least squares. The resulting ratio B gives mixing proportions u1 = 1/(1+B), u2 = B/(1+B), and the final weight is w_f = u1·w_ahp + u2·w_entr (eq. 5.1–5.2, p. 13). The job score is s_i = Σ w_j N_ij over normalized indices (eq. 6.1, p. 13).
- **k-means.** One-dimensional clustering of the 22 job scores. k is chosen from an SSE-vs-k plot, where the largest change in gradient is at k = 2→3 (pp. 14–15). The cluster with the highest centre is returned, then age-ineligible jobs are removed (p. 15). Rationale: simplicity, scalability, speed of convergence (p. 14). The MATLAB code is in Appendix C (p. 25).
- **Optimized (consistency-repair) model.** Each column of the normalized comparison matrix is compared with the normalized eigenvector using the cosine of the angle between them, which gives a "consistency factor" and a "deviation" per column. Reciprocal elements in the columns with the largest deviation are adjusted, using the best column as reference, and the procedure repeats until CR < 0.1 (pp. 18–19). It is demonstrated on a small example (pp. 19–20).

The pipeline is drawn as a flowchart (Figure 1, p. 6): job list → entropy weights, user input → AHP weights → combined weights → scores → k-means.

## Data
- A 22-job table (Appendix B, p. 24) gives number of staff, ability index, relevance, hourly rate, work time, distance, indoor/outdoor, sedentary, 1–5 risk level, COVID score, age requirement, an ability-requirement description, and a source column (Indeed, Investopedia, ZipRecruiter, Payscale and others).
- Wages and hours come from job sites (p. 7). The ordinal 1–5 scores (risk, ability, relevance, COVID) are the team's own ratings. Risk anchors are described verbally: 1 for sedentary/indoor/virtual, 3 for outdoor or first-aid jobs (p. 8).
- The personas' comparison matrices are invented and checked for consistency (Appendix A, pp. 21–24).

## Assumptions
There are four bolded assumptions, each followed by a justification paragraph (pp. 4–5). Examples:
- "All jobs of the same kind have the same salary, work time, and company size" (p. 5). Within-type variation is acknowledged and set aside.
- High school students are paid the online average wage, because "there is limited data on salaries paid specifically for high school students" (p. 5).
- Students have "perfect knowledge" of their summer plans, which lets them plan around recreation (p. 5).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Consistency checks (CR < 0.1) on all persona matrices (p. 11).
- Face validity: Christopher's recommended cluster (data analyst, research assistant, bank internship) matches his stated preference for large companies and skills, which they argue "validates our model" (p. 16).
- Cross-persona pattern analysis: high-ability jobs recur, and tutor, online translator and freelance writer appear repeatedly (pp. 17–18).
- Demonstration that the repair algorithm brings an inconsistent matrix into tolerance (pp. 19–20).
- No formal parameter sensitivity analysis is reported.

## What makes it stand out
- **A shortlist as output.** Clustering the scores and returning the top cluster (p. 15) fits how students actually choose: several viable options rather than one answer.
- **Designing for a real, fallible user.** The consistency-repair algorithm (pp. 18–20) addresses a practical failure of AHP in a consumer setting. It is an original extension, motivated by usability rather than by mathematics alone.
- **Hard filters separate from soft trade-offs.** The age requirement acts as a gate while the other factors are weighted (pp. 7, 15). This makes the eligibility logic clear.
- **Clear visual architecture.** A hierarchy diagram (p. 9), a model flowchart (p. 6), a variables table (p. 5), a rose diagram of a persona's weights (p. 10), an SSE elbow plot and a cluster scatter-plot (p. 15) let a reader follow the pipeline without the equations.
- **One worked persona before the batch.** Christopher is taken through every step (pp. 10–16) before the other nine are summarized in one table (p. 17). This keeps the methods section concrete.
- **Presentation piece placed up front.** The app-plus-article page sits immediately after the summary sheet (p. 2), so the requirement 4 deliverable is the first thing a judge sees after the summary.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** five paragraphs: context (COVID makes job search harder) → variables (22 jobs, five categories) → method chain (AHP → entropy → combined weights → k-means) → personas → optimized model, then keywords (p. 1). It is mostly qualitative and quotes no numerical results.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction/restatement → Assumptions & variables → Models overview → Basic model (Q1–2) → Testing (Q3) → Optimized model → Strengths/weaknesses → References → Appendices.
- **Devices:** section headings tagged with the question they answer ("(Questions 1 and 2)", p. 7); a hierarchy diagram with Goal/Criteria/Sub-criteria/Result bands (p. 9); a notation table (p. 5); one-line persona profiles with ages in parentheses (pp. 16–17); a strengths/weaknesses list that candidly notes company size is treated as always "increasing" (p. 20).
- **Article/app:** aimed at high schoolers, with a conversational, upbeat tone ("What are you waiting for? Come and find yourself a job today!", p. 2). Three annotated phone screens show the profile inputs (personality test, skills, goals, company, comfort), the recommendation list, and a peer-comment feature. It uses no numbers, only a framing statistic ("teen summer job has declined in the US").
- **Phrasing:**
  - "Oftentimes, we want more than the one single best job. Rather, we want a cluster of possible options." (p. 14)
  - "Given that our target audience are high school students, it is unrealistic to expect them to compute their comparison matrix and input it completely accurately." (p. 18)
  - "Certain variables in jobs predetermine whether high school students may participate in the job." (p. 7)

## Notes for reuse
- The entropy weights come from the job table and are identical for every user. Blending them in pulls all personas toward the same jobs, and the authors observe "a huge similarity between these ten fictional characters" (p. 17). If you copy the AHP + entropy blend for a *personal* recommender, check how much the user-independent component drowns out stated preferences.
- The 1–5 risk, ability, relevance and COVID scores in Appendix B are team-assigned, with only brief verbal anchors (p. 8). If you reuse this design, define a rubric for these ratings.
