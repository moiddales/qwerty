# 2022B-12465: Solution for Problem B of HiMCM 2022

## At a glance
The team treated the problem as a sequence of careful empirical time-series exercises. They checked the data before modeling: they tested for trend, autocorrelation and periodicity first, and only then chose model forms. For CO2 they fitted linear, quadratic and exponential curves and picked the quadratic using forward-chaining 10-fold cross-validation (MAE 0.681 ppm). That model reaches 497 ppm in 2050 and 685 ppm only in 2097. For temperature they proposed a hybrid "HW+Bootstrap" model: Holt-Winters smoothing without a seasonal term, plus block-bootstrapped residuals. It puts the 1.25/1.50/2 °C thresholds at 2032, 2042 and 2063, each with an interval. For the CO2–temperature link they regressed temperature on ln(CO2) with ARIMA errors. They then compared the two temperature models in six train/test back-testing scenarios.

## How they read the problem
- They open the restatement by correcting the problem text (pp. 3–4). Data Set 1 contains annual means, not March means. The quoted 377.7 ppm is the 2004 annual mean, and the true March 2004 value is 379.06 ppm.
- They regrouped the requirements into three questions (pp. 4): Q1 is CO2 (1a–1d), Q2 is temperature alone (2a–2b) and Q3 is the relationship (3a–3b). The official 2c ("how far into the future is your model reliable") becomes their 3b, an evaluation of forecast ability.
- A "Problem analysis" section (pp. 4–5) gives a method plan for each sub-question with a reason. For 1a they prefer a statistical trend test over "a threshold approach, where the 2004 level is directly compared to any previous levels". For 1d, because there are only 63 points, they want cross-validation. For Q2 they first test for periodicity. For Q3 a physics argument justifies log(CO2) as the trend driver.
- They scoped all projections explicitly as a business-as-usual scenario (Assumption 1, p. 5).

## Models and methods
- **Modified Mann-Kendall test** (pp. 6–7). A non-parametric monotonic-trend test whose variance is corrected for autocorrelation (Hamed and Rao). The stated rationale is that the running averages are "highly autocorrelated" (p. 12), so the standard test's independence assumption fails. They used the R package `modifiedmk`.
- **CO2 curve fits** (pp. 7–8, 12). Time was rebased to years since 1959 "to remove the potential effect of using the year as the x-value" (p. 5). The quadratic and exponential forms were tried because points at both ends lie above the linear trend (pp. 4–5).
- **Forward-chaining cross-validation** (p. 8). The data are split into 10 consecutive blocks. The model trains on blocks 1..k and tests on block k+1, and the scores are averaged. This respects temporal order, and the stated reason is the very limited sample.
- **HW+Bootstrap** (pp. 8–9, 13–14). The first step was diagnostic. STL found no seasonal component, the periodogram was very weak (largest ≈ 0.050) and the ACF showed strong autocorrelation. Regression plus ARIMA was rejected for this task because of "ARIMA's well-known poorer performance for long term forecasts" (p. 9). Exponential smoothing was preferred as "more explainable". The fitted values were α = 0.283 and β = 0.173. Residuals are resampled with a moving block bootstrap and averaged over 100 bagging runs, which also yields 10–90% bands. Adding the bootstrapped residuals raised r from 0.947 to 0.971 and cut RMSE from 0.107 to 0.081 °C.
- **Regression with ARIMA errors** (pp. 10, 15–17). The rationale is physical: temperature change is roughly proportional to radiative forcing, which grows with ln(CO2) (citing Manabe and Wetherald). The trend comes from ln(CO2), and the residual fluctuations are modeled by ARIMA(6,1,0), with orders chosen from the ACF and PACF. Future CO2 comes from the quadratic model, which links Q1 into the Q2/Q3 pipeline (p. 17).
- **Back-testing scenarios** (pp. 10–11, 18–19). There are six train/test configurations (Table 1): the training window is 1959–1979 or 1980–2000, the test window is 1980–2000 or 2001–2021, and each is run with both models. Pairing them lets the team compare the effect of forecast distance and of model choice.

## Data
They used only the two provided series. They checked the CO2 series against its source link and corrected the description of the data (pp. 3–4). The base period is stated as roughly 14 °C (p. 4). No external series were fitted, and outside sources serve only as background and physical justification.

## Assumptions
There are three numbered assumptions, each followed by a paragraph headed "Justification" (pp. 5–6):
- Historical statistical trends continue, i.e. a business-as-usual scenario. This is justified by analogy to IPCC scenario practice, together with a commitment to avoid overfitting and to validate on held-out data.
- CO2 drives the long-term temperature trend, while year-to-year fluctuations behave as an autoregressive process. This is justified by radiative-forcing physics and the strong correlation in the data.
- Mauna Loa CO2 represents the global atmosphere. This is justified by the site's altitude (3400 m) and its calibration.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Forward-chaining cross-validation with three metrics for the CO2 models (Table 2, p. 13).
- Residual diagnostics: the HW residuals are near-normal with no seasonality (p. 13). After ARIMA, the residual ACF lies within the confidence bounds and is white noise (pp. 15–16).
- Six back-testing scenarios (Table 4, p. 19). The results surprised the team: both models did better on the farther test window 2001–2021 than on 1980–2000. They concluded they "can hardly infer" whether skill degrades with forecast distance (p. 18). Trend+ARIMA was steadier across scenarios (RMSE ≈ 0.08–0.10 °C). HW+Bootstrap reached RMSE 0.234 °C in S5, which they explain by its dependence on its own history (p. 18).
- Comparison of the two models' projections to 2100 (Fig. 10, p. 20): similar rates, with Trend+ARIMA 0.1–0.2 °C lower and losing its fluctuations as ARIMA decays.

## What makes it stand out
- **Diagnose before modeling.** Stationarity, autocorrelation and periodicity are tested before a form is chosen (pp. 8–9), and the paper presents this order as a strength (p. 20). Every model choice in the paper has a stated, data-based reason.
- **Validation suited to time series.** Forward-chaining CV (p. 8) and train/test scenarios (pp. 10–11) avoid the leakage of random k-fold CV, and both are explained clearly enough to copy.
- **A physically interpretable relationship.** Rewriting the fit as ΔT = 3.776 ln(cc/324.594) (p. 15) turns a regression into a statement about radiative forcing, with an interpretable reference concentration.
- **Uncertainty given with the answers.** Threshold years come with intervals (p. 14), and the figures show 10–90% bootstrap bands (Figs. 4–5).
- **An honest answer to "how far is it reliable?".** They report evidence (better in the first 10–20 years), show where it contradicts their expectation, and say: "It is difficult and irresponsible to determine how long into the future our models will lose its reliability, given the very limited information available" (p. 18).
- **Reading the source data critically.** Catching that the data are annual means, not March values (pp. 3–4), shows command of the material from the first page.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** four paragraphs on one page: context with the IPCC figure, then CO2 (method, best model, 2050 and 685-ppm results), then temperature (novel model, r = 0.971, threshold years with intervals), then relationship and reliability. Nearly every claim carries a number.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, restatement) → Problem analysis → Assumptions → Methodology (per question) → Results and analyses (per question) → Strengths and Limitations → Conclusions → Article → References.
- **Devices:** the methodology and results sections are separated, both keyed to question labels such as "(Question 1a)". Equations are numbered. There is a scenario table for the validation design (Table 1) and a results table with the same rows (Table 4). Captions are long enough that the figures can be read on their own (e.g. Fig. 5, Fig. 9). The Limitations section names specific failure modes (missed turning points, short records, underestimated uncertainty).
- **Article:** headline "Act! Drastic temperature rise in response to massive carbon dioxide emissions" (p. 22). Numbers are turned into plain comparisons ("increased by 30% compared to 60 years ago", "twice as high at the end of this century"). One dual-axis chart shows observed and projected temperature with CO2. The rest is a graded list of actions, from talking to friends up to international cooperation.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "Compared to a threshold approach, where the 2004 level is directly compared to any previous levels, this approach has an advantage in solving the problem from a statistical point of view." (p. 4)
  - "We speculate CO2 changes may play an important role in affecting the change trend of temperature, rather than the detailed fluctuations…" (p. 10)
  - "However, to our surprise, both two models performed better when predicting the temperature anomalies in the distant period of 2001~2021 (S3 and S4) than that in the near period of 1980-2000 (S1 and S2)." (p. 18)

## Notes for reuse
- For 1a, the modified Mann-Kendall test on running averages shows that CO2 levels rise monotonically (pp. 11–12). It does not directly compare the 10-year increase ending in 2004 with earlier 10-year increases. Teams that computed those increments found 2003 marginally larger (see 2022B-12911, 2022B-13010). Anyone borrowing the test should make sure it answers the question actually asked.
