# 2024A-15504: Probabilistic Olympic sport inclusion model

## At a glance
This scanned paper frames selection as binary classification. A logistic model combines a base IOC-value score from nine subcriteria with an inertia term for past inclusion and a fitted bias correction. It reproduces patterns such as continuous presence, additions and removals, then predicts Australian football, Zwift cycling and netball as leading Brisbane candidates, with Zwift cycling a future possibility. Popularity and relevance/innovation are the most influential factors (pp. 1, 10–20).

## How they read the problem
The scanned paper frames inclusion as a binary classification problem. It first constructs nine subcriteria and a base IOC-value score, then adds two terms that historical data can identify: inertia from prior inclusion and a bias correction. The fitted classifier is tested on historical patterns before being applied to seven Australian-relevant candidates for Brisbane and to future candidates (pp. 1, 7–20).

## Models and methods
The logistic model maps a sport's base IOC-value score, prior Olympic presence and bias correction to an inclusion probability. The inertia term captures the fact that established sports have a different starting position from new candidates; the bias term lets the fitted curve match historical inclusion frequencies. This gives a direct probability interpretation while retaining interpretable subcriteria (pp. 10–16).

## Data
Nine subcriteria are collected from historical Olympic records, web sources and literature. The paper estimates missing values for gap years and future candidates, and works at discipline level because event-specific data are unavailable (pp. 7–12).

## Assumptions
Historical inclusion patterns are informative about future selection, and the three logistic components capture the dominant persistence and value effects. Estimated future values for Australian candidates are treated as scenario inputs. The discipline-level score is assumed sufficient for a first recommendation, with event-level extension left for better data (pp. 6–12, 20).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The key validation is reproduction of historical patterns: continuous presence, newly added sports and removed sports are classified by the fitted model (pp. 13–16). Sensitivity identifies popularity and relevance/innovation as the strongest factors. The paper reports data limitations openly, especially for future and event-level values (pp. 18–20).

## What makes it stand out
This is the only assigned Olympic paper that learns persistence and bias terms from historical inclusion rather than relying entirely on expert weights. Framing selection as a probability makes the output easy to compare and update as new Games occur.

The three-part probability structure is especially reusable: a new sport can score well on current values while still carrying a lower prior probability because it lacks Olympic history. Separating these effects prevents the inertia of established sports from being mistaken for popularity.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
Because the source is scanned, the paper relies on a compact summary, a numbered methodology, model-testing figures and candidate tables. The conclusion explains both the Brisbane prediction and the path to event-level refinement (pp. 1–2, 15–20).

The paper's historical test distinguishes three patterns that a single score would confound: sports that remain continuously included, sports newly added to the program, and sports that are removed. The fitted inertia and bias terms let the logistic curve reproduce those patterns while the base score reflects current IOC values (pp. 12–16). For Brisbane, the seven-candidate forecast favors Australian football, Zwift cycling and netball; Zwift's youth appeal also explains why the paper flags it as a longer-term candidate. The sensitivity section identifies popularity and relevance/innovation as the factors whose perturbation changes inclusion probability most (pp. 17–20).

The paper also states a useful boundary: it operates at discipline level because event-level historical data are incomplete. Extending the model to individual events is proposed only if event-specific popularity, inclusion history and IOC-value data are collected, which is a disciplined way to describe the next modeling step (pp. 20–21).

The inertia term is a particularly clear modeling choice. It acknowledges that the Olympic program has memory, while the base score and bias term allow the model to recognize change. This is more informative than silently adding historical inclusion as one more weighted indicator because the fitted terms have distinct interpretations (pp. 12–16).

The paper reports that the model accurately reproduces continuous presence, newly added and removed sports in its historical test. That categorical check is appropriate for a logistic classifier and is more faithful to the task than evaluating it only with a continuous ranking metric (pp. 13–16).

The approach is also updateable: once another Olympic cycle is observed, the inertia and bias terms can be refit while retaining the nine-factor base score. That makes the model suitable for repeated decision rounds, subject to better data (pp. 12–20).

The distinction between current value and historical inertia is the main reason its probability output is more interpretable than an undifferentiated weighted score (pp. 12–16).

## Notes for reuse
- Composite rankings inherit the choice of indicators, proxies and weights; show them and run perturbation tests before treating a rank as definitive.
- Forecasts and cost savings depend on long-horizon assumptions about prices, technology and policy; give scenario ranges.
