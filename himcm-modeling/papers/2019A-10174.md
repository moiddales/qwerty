# 2019A-10174: Time-dependent public charging model

## At a glance
Team 10174 models public charging as a time-dependent interaction between visitors, devices, outlets and electricity prices. A NetLogo agent-based model lets visitors enter destinations, choose seats, carry phones/laptops, consume battery and charge when an outlet is available. Destination attendance is represented by distributions that differ for airports, schools, libraries, rail stations, parks and cafés. Public outlet density (POD) controls access, while electricity price and destination counts change with year. A separate EV calculation uses vehicle growth, battery and public-charging shares. The result is a ten-year cost forecast with concrete density thresholds: for example, three outlets per 576 square feet in airports and three per 144 square feet in cafés give every visitor access under the tested assumptions (pp. 1–2, 14–20).

## How they read the problem
The team asks how the cost of “free” electricity changes over time, then decomposes that question into electricity price, attendance, destination capacity, outlet density and device behavior (pp. 1–3). They choose six destination types because each has a distinct visitor pattern: airports have bimodal peaks, schools have a morning arrival pulse, and cafés vary through the day (pp. 9–12). Instead of assigning one average load to a destination, they simulate individual visitors and devices. This makes availability and outlet competition emerge from the model rather than being imposed as a fixed utilization rate.

## Models and methods
A general cost equation multiplies energy consumption by commercial electricity price and scales a representative destination to the national number of destinations (pp. 4–5). Electricity price is extrapolated from historical commercial rates (Appendix A, pp. 27–28). Destination counts are forecast from tables for schools, rail stations, airports, libraries and other places (Appendix B, pp. 28–31).

The core model is agent-based. Visitors are created according to a destination’s time-varying arrival density, assigned a stay duration, seat coordinates and device list, then move through charging states. Devices have battery levels, discharge rates and charging times. A visitor can charge only when a seat/outlet is available; the simulation records device energy and access. POD scenarios range from low to high, with outlet placement tied to destination area and seat capacity (pp. 5–13, 47–52).

EV costs are estimated separately from EV counts, annual mileage, energy per mile, battery and the assumed public fraction. The authors compare their 2018 public-energy estimate (0.618 TWh) with a 0.57 TWh benchmark obtained from total EV electricity and a 20% public share (p. 17).

## Data
The paper draws U.S. electricity prices, destination counts, airport terminal capacities, Amtrak station sizes and representative attendance. LAX supplies airport peak information; tabled capacities support seat/outlet scaling (pp. 10–12, 27–46). Device wattage, charging rates, battery ranges and EV assumptions are listed in the model and appendices. NetLogo code and Python EV code are included, making the simulation reproducible (pp. 47–53).

## Assumptions
Visitors have random stay durations in destination-specific ranges; charging is linear in battery percentage; many destination types have negligible EV charging; and a fixed fraction of visitors carry each device class (pp. 7–13). Outlet access is represented through seats and POD, and destination attendance is normalized over a day. Electricity prices and counts change with year, while device efficiency can be changed through charging/drain parameters (pp. 4–5, 18–20).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The simulation is repeated 100 times for each year/POD combination, and mean daily consumption is plotted (p. 14). The EV estimate is checked against a known 2018 public-consumption value (p. 17). Density sensitivity shows that rail stations and libraries change little because nearly all visitors already have access, while airports and cafés have clear thresholds (pp. 15, 20). The paper also tests low, low-mid, high-mid and high POD over ten years, exposing both price growth and infrastructure effects.

## What makes it stand out
- Individual agents connect outlet density to actual access and charging energy.
- Destination-specific arrival distributions carry qualitative differences into quantitative results.
- The model is time-aware: electricity prices, visitor counts, device efficiency and EV adoption can all change.
- Repeated runs provide a simple Monte Carlo robustness check, while the 2018 EV comparison gives an external anchor.
- NetLogo and Python appendices make the computational model inspectable rather than a hidden calculation.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary and newspaper page explain the question in ordinary language before naming the agent model (pp. 1–2). A contents page and methodology definitions establish the six destinations and variables early (pp. 3–6). Figures 1–7 progress from flowchart and visitor distributions to device and EV results; the conclusion returns to practical levers such as POD and hardware efficiency (pp. 10–20). Large appendices preserve capacity tables and source code (pp. 27–53), a useful separation when a simulation needs detail without interrupting the main story.

## Notes for reuse
- Report the number of replications and random-seed policy when using an agent simulation; this paper reports 100 runs but leaves some implementation details in code.
- Calibrate arrival and stay distributions locally; averages can hide peak access failures (pp. 9–12).
