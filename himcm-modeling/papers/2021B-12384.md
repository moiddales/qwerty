# 2021B-12384: Untitled

## At a glance
This paper is the strongest in the set on **domain-correct measurement and evaluation**. Part 1b explains how the Bureau's table would actually be produced: echo-sounding plus GPS bathymetry, pixel-count surface areas, and the standard stacked-frustum lake-volume formula from a fisheries survey manual. Part 1a is condensed into a published lake water-balance equation. The water-level work is statistical. Mann-Kendall tests find decreasing trends in levels and in within-year variability. Drought is defined by year-over-year percentage change falling below a baseline taken from a reference cycle (1952–1959) for at least 9 months, which dates the current drought to May 2020–September 2021. Both forecasts use the same idea: transform the series into a cumulative sum of year-over-year percent changes, fit a line plus a sinusoid to it, and back-transform. Recent-drought data give 1040.122 / 994.032 / 840.207 ft for 2025 / 2030 / 2050, and 2005–2020 data give 1074.482 / 1037.302 / 973.278 ft. Part 3 is a federal plan plus a dashboard of effectiveness metrics taken from research on recycled-water programs.

## How they read the problem
- Each of the three sections opens with its own "Question Restatement" and its own assumptions block (pp. 4, 9, 17). The paper therefore reads as three self-contained modules.
- Part 1b is read as a methodology question ("we showcase the information and methodology required to verify the data", p. 4). The answer is a realistic survey pipeline rather than a fit to the four Table 1 points (pp. 5–7).
- Drought is explicitly scoped to **hydrological drought**, because "the only droughts that could be adequately described with water elevation data are hydrological droughts" (Assumption 4, p. 10).
- The exploratory analysis justifies focusing on the monthly dataset: the annual high-low range mirrors the within-year standard deviation of monthly levels (p. 10).
- The plan is scoped to Nevada, Arizona and California. Mexico is excluded explicitly on the grounds of international-politics complexity (p. 17).

## Models and methods
- **Measurement pipeline (1b).** Assumptions 1–3 (p. 4) set up the geometry: the lake is a stack of frustums, contours have similar shapes, and echo-sounding plus GPS is accurate. Surface area comes from counting pixels inside each contour on a bathymetric map and multiplying by the map scale (pp. 5–6). Volume is the sum of frustums between successive contours (eqs. 2–3, pp. 6–7), citing Taube's lake-volume manual.
- **Water-balance equation (1a).** The paper adapts the MLake formulation: dV/dt = Pol − Eol + R + D + Qin − Qout − Qgwd + Qgwr, with a symbol table (pp. 8–9). Relationships are drawn from its structure, for example that evaporation and precipitation drive many other terms (p. 9). The equation is used as a framework, not calibrated.
- **Trend tests (2a).** Mann-Kendall tests on annual standard deviation and on levels (p. 11).
- **Drought criterion (2a).** The baseline is the 50th-percentile monthly change over 1952–1959, taken as a reference dry/wet cycle (Assumption 2, p. 9). Change is measured year over year "to eliminate the stochastic existence of outlier water elevations". The 9-month minimum length is "based on historical conditions of the length of a dry season" (p. 12).
- **Forecast model (2b).** A plain linear fit to raw levels left structured residuals, so the data are transformed into N(t) = Σ C(i), where C is the YoY percent change (eqs. 6–7). On the transformed series, a line leaves sinusoidal residuals (Fig 5), so the final model is line + sine (eq. 8, Fig 6). It is inverted month by month (eqs. 9–10) and averaged into annual values (eq. 11) (pp. 13–15).
- **Evaluation metrics (3b).** Taken from Arias (2011), grouped into quality and quantity, breadth of application, and customer and public support, each with a formula or test statement (pp. 22–24).

## Data
Only the two COMAP datasets are used quantitatively. The measurement part relies on authoritative method descriptions (NOAA, USGS, Nebraska Game & Parks, Michigan DNR). The plan relies on EPA, DOE and survey sources. Data are not cleaned except for the window selection. The team notes that the monthly dataset is richer than the annual high/low set (p. 10).

## Assumptions
Assumptions are given **per section**, each followed by a "Justification" (pp. 4, 9–10, 17–18). Examples:
- The lake as stacked frustums follows from how bathymetric contours are collected.
- Volume and water level are interchangeable for discussing hydrological factors, "with the only notable difference between the two being the unit discussed".
- The years 1952–1959 represent a baseline dry-then-wet hydrological cycle, because it shows a stark decrease followed by a stark increase within 5–10 years.
- The plan will pass lawmaking barriers if it is feasible, supported by a cited survey on public views of water recycling.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Formal significance tests (Mann-Kendall, with p-values) support the descriptive claims (p. 11).
- Residual analysis drives the model specification (Figs 5–6, p. 14), and the combined model is overlaid on the transformed data.
- The starting elevation of the current drought is placed in the historical distribution (1.73 SD below median, bottom 4%) (p. 13).
- There is no numerical sensitivity analysis or hold-out test. Instead, a strengths/weaknesses discussion states when the model fails: with an incomplete wet/dry cycle in the training window it "tends to overestimate the loss" (pp. 16–17).

## What makes it stand out
- **Realistic measurement science for 1b.** Bathymetric surveying, map-based area and stacked frustums is how lake capacity tables are actually built. The explanation is clear enough for a non-specialist (pp. 5–7).
- **A literature water-balance model.** Using a published lake-dynamics formulation as a framework lends credibility, and its symbol table doubles as the factor list (pp. 8–9).
- **Statistical evidence for patterns.** Mann-Kendall p-values back the claim that levels and variability are declining (p. 11). Few teams used hypothesis tests here.
- **A drought definition with a reference baseline.** Calibrating "dry" against a chosen historical cycle, and filtering out short anomalies with a YoY change and a 9-month minimum, gives a reproducible rule (p. 12).
- **Transformation chosen from the residuals.** The model's form comes from inspecting residuals, and each transform and its inverse is written out (pp. 13–15).
- **Impact metrics drawn from real practice.** The evaluation dashboard (quality standards, portfolio share, growth, coverage, satisfaction, voter support) answers "how would you measure the impact" concretely and credibly (pp. 22–24).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** four paragraphs: context, the volume and factor work, the modeling idea, and the plan and metrics. It explains the methods clearly but does not quote the forecast numbers (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** 1 Lake Mead Water Dynamics → 2 Modelling of Water Levels → 3 Wastewater Recycling Plan → 4 Plan Analysis → 5 Conclusion → 6 References. Each section has its own restatement and assumptions.
- **Devices:** an echo-sounding diagram and sample bathymetric map, a symbol table for the balance equation, panel figures (A)/(B)/(C) for EDA and forecasts with the prediction value in the caption, a resource-flow tree (Fig 9), and metric "test statements" with formulas.
- **Letter/memo/article:** a full-bleed magazine page (p. 2) with photo header "A Recycling Plan to Save Lake Mead Before It Drys Up". It has two short boxes, "The Recent Trend in Lake Mead" (Lake Mead "could lose up to 200 feet in water elevation" by 2050) and "Our Goal in Water Reclamation", then an "Our Vision" list and a "Planning" section. It translates the model into one memorable number and a clear goal.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "The only droughts that could be adequately described with water elevation data are hydrological droughts." (p. 10)
  - "The inciting elevation is 1.73 standard deviations below the 50th percentile which makes it the bottom 4% of inciting drought elevations." (p. 13)
  - "Our model tends to overestimate the loss in water elevations for future timeframes if it's given an incomplete picture of the lake's hydrological cycle." (p. 16)

## Notes for reuse
- Model 1 is fitted to about 17 months (May 2020–Sep 2021) and extrapolated about 30 years to 840 ft by 2050. The authors themselves warn that a window without a full wet/dry cycle overestimates loss (pp. 16–17). Treat such long extrapolations as scenarios.
- The plan is not sized against the forecast shortfall, and no volumes or costs are computed. Whether recycling could cover "all or part" of the shortfall is argued qualitatively. The summary says the balance equation was "used" for the level model, but the forecasts rely only on the elevation series.
