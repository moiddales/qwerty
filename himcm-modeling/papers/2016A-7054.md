# 2016A-7054: Wave-Packets and Three-Lane Triathlon Course

## At a glance
The team uses eight gender-and-speed divisions, a three-lane course representation, and 55-person wave-packets. Congestion falls 72% with lanes, 29% when bike and run roads close separately, and 27% when packets are ordered by speed. A four-minute packet gap is used in the schedule, with explicit discussion of the road-closure trade-off. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They define congestion from passing and local crowding, then optimize a weighted metric that also values road closure and finishers. The model evaluates course design and wave schedule separately before combining them. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
NetLogo agents have probabilistic segment times and move in fast, main and slow lanes. Wave-packets contain 25% fast, 50% medium and 25% slow athletes; a composite objective scores congestion, closure and completion. Scenarios vary distances and packet gaps (pp. 4–35).

## Data
The paper uses HiMCM triathlon dataset and synthetic agent speeds. The source is the prior-triathlon spreadsheet of age, gender, category, segment and transition times. Teams derive quartiles, clusters or fitted distributions and generate simulated entrants and schedules. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes prior-race times represent this year’s entrants, segment speeds can be summarized by groups or distributions, and wave starts do not change individual ability. Congestion is represented by passing, density or speed overlap; road closure is a hard limit or an explicitly weighted term (pp. 4–10). Its central choice is NetLogo agent-based model, supported by quartile speed divisions; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The base no-lane model is compared with lane, separated-road and packet variants. Sensitivity experiments change packet composition, gap and race distances; results are reported as percentage changes and schedule tables (pp. 24–40).

## What makes it stand out
The paper gives the organizer knobs to turn—lanes, packet composition, road separation and gap—and quantifies each. Its explicit composite objective makes trade-offs visible.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
An attractive sponsor/Mayor letter is followed by model diagrams, NetLogo screenshots and a race schedule. Headline percentages appear in the summary and are traceable to scenario tables (pp. 1–4, 20–38).

## Notes for reuse
- The composite weights are policy choices and the 7-hour schedule conflicts with a strict 5.5-hour road limit unless interpreted carefully. An imitator should resolve that constraint explicitly and report units for each objective.
