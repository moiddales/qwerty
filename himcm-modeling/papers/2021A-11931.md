# 2021A-11931: Shedding Light on a Battery of Batteries

## At a glance
Instead of scoring batteries against each other, the team scores each battery by how far it falls short of the homeowner's requirements. They build a "disparity" for each factor (capacity, peak power, budget, lifetime, maintenance; later dimension and safety). A battery that meets a requirement gets zero; one that falls short gets a penalty that grows non-linearly. The battery pack with the smallest weighted total wins. Demand is built bottom-up from an appliance questionnaire (power, schedule, necessary or optional, value), and the number of batteries in the pack is optimized. For the sample home and budget, two lead-acid packs (Yangtze Solar 6-GFM-150, then CSPower GEL) rank first, ahead of GenZ LFP (pp. 1, 7–19).

## How they read the problem
- Turns Requirement 1a into a structured **questionnaire**: which appliances, necessary or optional, periodic (with fixed hours) or random (with average daily hours), power, the appliance's value on a 1–9 scale, and the desired days of autonomy (p. 7).
- Defines "best" as **least mismatch with this household's expectations** rather than "best specifications", which makes the model personal by construction (pp. 1, 7).
- Separates a base model for the 1600 sq ft home (Requirement 1) from an extended **Battery Ranking Model (BRM)** that adds dimension, safety and transport/installation cost (Requirement 2) (pp. 13–15).
- Uses a much larger battery list than the problem gives: 16 real products of 5 chemistries (NMC, LFP, LTO, SLA, GEL) (p. 15).

## Models and methods
- **Capacity requirement with graceful degradation.** Desired capacity D_C = [a·E_dt + (k − a)·E_dn] / [u·ρ·(1 − f)], where k is the number of autonomy days, a is the number of days all appliances can run, u is depth of discharge, ρ is efficiency and f is capacity fade (Eq. 4, p. 8). If the pack cannot cover k full days, the penalty depends on how much appliance value is lost on the remaining "necessary-only" days. If even necessary loads fail before k − 0.5 days, the disparity is infinite (Eqs. 6–8, pp. 8–9).
- **Peak power** is the maximum over the day of the scheduled loads plus all random loads (worst case) (Eq. 11, p. 10).
- **Penalty shapes.** Shortfalls use a squared relative gap, scaled by a rate constant r₁ = 25, calibrated so that a 20% shortfall gives disparity 1. Overruns of budget and size use a cubic-type form with r₂ = 5.84, calibrated so that a 10% overrun gives disparity 1 (Eqs. 13, 16, 19–20, pp. 10–12). Calibrating the penalty constants against a stated tolerance is a clear, reusable idea.
- **Pack size.** For each battery, n from 1 to n_max is enumerated and the n with the smallest weighted capacity + power + budget (+ dimension) disparity is chosen (Eqs. 15, 17, 27; Tables 11, 14, pp. 10–11, 17–19).
- **Aggregation.** The user rates each factor 1–9; the ratings are normalized and summed as w′·d (Eqs. 21–22, p. 12).
- **Safety** is a value per chemistry (NMC 1 … GEL 3) turned into a disparity (Table 5, Eq. 29, p. 15).

## Data
- Specifications for 16 batteries from manufacturer and review sites, with a table of capacity, depth of discharge, power, type, lifetime, fade, cost, size and efficiency (Table 6, p. 15).
- Missing values are filled with stated rules: lead-acid efficiency 70%; lithium efficiency presumed 100%; 40% fade at end of life; 50% usable capacity for lead-acid; 10 A average current for lead-acid power (pp. 15–16).
- The appliance list and homeowner requirements (budget $12,000, 3 days of autonomy, 1 m³ space, $1,000 extra costs) are set "based on common sense" (Tables 7–8, p. 16).

## Assumptions
Five assumptions, each followed by a justification paragraph (p. 5). Examples:
- Batteries are indoors at 25 °C, which is where most capacities are rated, so location does not affect performance (Assumptions 3–4).
- Inverter and charge-controller losses are negligible compared with other losses (Assumption 5).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Base model vs BRM** comparison: the extra factors narrow the gap between the top two because the GEL battery is smaller and safer (p. 19).
- **Sensitivity analysis** in two parts: ±10% error in each user input, and in each weight, measured as the mean percentage change in overall disparity across batteries (Eq. 36, Figures 1–2, pp. 19–20). The team concludes the model is insensitive to most inputs and to the weights.
- **Screening check:** a rough manual elimination before running the model reaches the same shortlist, which supports the result (pp. 16–17).

## What makes it stand out
- **Requirement-gap formulation.** Scoring each battery by its shortfall from a household's needs makes generalization automatic: change the inputs and the ranking changes (pp. 7, 12).
- **Necessary vs optional loads and appliance value.** These give the capacity model a realistic fallback ("run only essentials on the last days") instead of a pass/fail threshold (pp. 8–9).
- **Battery pack size is a decision variable.** Many teams compared single units; optimizing n lets cheap low-capacity batteries compete fairly (pp. 10–11, 17–18).
- **Calibrated penalty constants** with a one-line justification for each make the non-linear scoring transparent (pp. 11–12).
- **Worked example end-to-end** (Tesla Powerwall 2 traced through every equation, pp. 17–19) lets a reader verify the method.
- **Concrete-battery analysis uses the source paper's data**: energy density falling across discharge cycles (7.10 → 6.11 Wh/m²) leads to an "emergency storage only" recommendation (p. 22).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** a titled narrative of five paragraphs covering motivation, the two-stage modeling plan (the named BRM), how the evaluation was done (16 batteries, top three, sensitivity), the concrete battery verdict, and the article. Key words line at the end. It is light on final numbers (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, restatement, assumptions) → Base model → Extended model (BRM) → Data → Application and analysis (candidates, substitution, evaluation, sensitivity) → Concrete battery → Conclusion (strengths/weaknesses) → Article → References (pp. 2–3).
- **Devices:** variable tables for each model (Tables 1, 4); a list of tables (p. 3); "Factors consideration" subsections that justify each factor before modeling it; side-by-side small tables of disparity by n and final rankings (pp. 18–19).
- **Article:** a headline, a byline, two captioned photos, and a reader-facing explanation of what inputs the model asks for (p. 24).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "The higher the disparity, the less suitability of the battery for the household." (p. 7)
  - "Instead of using concrete batteries on a daily basis, energy should be stored in them for long terms and used when the energy in stored in conventional batteries are depleted under rare extreme conditions." (p. 22)

## Notes for reuse
- Lithium efficiencies that were not found were set to 100% (p. 15), which favors lithium packs in the capacity calculation. If you fill gaps this way, use a realistic default (lithium round-trip efficiency is typically about 90–95%) or run the ranking under both values.
- Equations (17) and (27) are written as arg max but are used as minimization (pp. 11, 14, 18). Write the optimization direction explicitly.
