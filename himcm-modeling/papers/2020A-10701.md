# 2020A-10701: Untitled ("Job Finder" summer job recommender)

## At a glance
The team builds five constructed indices for each job: salary, time, knowledge, environment and intensity. Their distinctive feature is that the indices are computed from *both* job data and the student's own situation. Net salary subtracts commuting and meal costs and adds expected overtime pay. The time index trades off recreation time left in a 12-hour day against conflicts with the student's schedule. AHP judgment matrices supply the student's weights, TOPSIS ranks the 31 jobs, and hierarchical clustering then groups similar jobs so that the recommendation takes the best job *from each cluster* for diversity. The model is illustrated in detail on a knowledge-inclined student (top 3: cybersecurity teaching assistant, entry-level behavior tech, chemistry lab assistant), applied to ten "inclination" personas, and stress-tested by switching that student to a time-inclined profile. The paper opens with a poster-style cover page and ends with a multi-screen app design.

## How they read the problem
- They note that existing summer-job "evaluation forms" "only ask people some questions without forming a suggestion about their choices". The model's purpose is to turn answers into a ranked recommendation (p. 4).
- They interpret recreation time concretely: work time plus recreational time is fixed at 12 hours per day, and commuting counts as work time (Assumption 5, p. 6). The time index therefore directly measures the recreation the student keeps.
- Probabilistic factors are handled through expected values. Overtime happens with probability ρ_over, so expected overtime pay and expected overtime hours enter salary and time (Assumption 6, eqs. 6.1, 6.5, pp. 6, 8). This is a direct response to the statement's "deterministic or probabilistic" wording.
- They plan for a "global" model fitting students around the world, and mention that an online survey "will be conducted" to make personas representative (p. 4). The paper does not report that survey's results.
- A one-page "Work Structure" flowchart maps the whole paper before the assumptions (p. 5).

## Models and methods
- **Index construction (the core modeling).**
  - *Salary* is what the student actually takes home: base pay plus expected overtime minus transport and meal costs. A job that provides meals lowers n_m, and a remote job zeroes transport (pp. 7–8).
  - *Time* rewards remaining recreation, weighted by γ, and penalizes conflicts with the student's existing schedule, weighted by μ, including the possible conflict when overtime runs past the end of the shift (T_margin) (p. 8).
  - *Knowledge* is λ = Σ w_i δ_i over academic knowledge, experience and social skills, with δ rated 1–5 on a "judgment table". Weights come from the student's AHP sub-matrix. A 0/1 "conformity variable" α switches off academic knowledge that is irrelevant to the student's career. λ is multiplied by work hours (pp. 8–9).
  - *Environment* is a weighted sum over working condition, interpersonal environment and location (p. 9).
  - *Intensity* is a graded labor-type category (p. 9).
- **Normalization and TOPSIS.** Vector normalization, weighted matrix, ideal and anti-ideal points, L2 distances, closeness score s_iw ∈ [0, 1] (pp. 9–11). Rationale: TOPSIS "can make full use of the information of the original data", and it "has no strict limitation on the data distribution and sample content" (p. 10).
- **AHP.** Saaty 1–9 scale, eigenvector weights, CI/CR with an RI table (pp. 11–13). A hierarchy diagram is given (p. 12).
- **Systematic (hierarchical) clustering.** Euclidean distances on the job indices, agglomerative merging and a dendrogram. The tree is cut at height 25 to give 8 groups, and the highest-TOPSIS job in each group is recommended first (pp. 13–14, 16). Rationale: "to avoid recommending highly similar job[s], enhancing the quality of our recommendation" (p. 1).

## Data
- Appendix A, "Fictional Job Data", lists 31 positions, many named after real employers (e.g. waiter at Compass Group, sales associate at EXPRESS, chemistry lab assistant). Columns are daily salary, start/end times, overtime probability ρ, overtime hours T, S, meals provided and work-from-home flag (pp. 25–27). The source of these figures is not stated.
- Knowledge and environment ratings (1–5) are qualitative judgments using the judgment table (p. 9). Persona judgment matrices are constructed by the team to express each inclination (pp. 15, 17).

## Assumptions
There are six bullet pairs, "Assumption n" followed by "Explanation n" (pp. 5–6). Examples:
- **No experience requirement is modeled**, because asking students to list all their experience "reduces the usability of our model" and students can judge this themselves (p. 5).
- **Work time plus recreation time is 12 hours per day**, "proper for most students" after excluding daily routines (p. 6).
- **Uncertain salary is replaced by its expected value** (p. 6).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Explaining outliers.** For the worked example, the authors check why job 12 ranks low: it has 24-hour shifts, the lowest hourly pay and medium knowledge gain. They conclude the ranking is consistent with the student's weights (p. 16). An index-profile bar chart of the recommended jobs shows that every pick is strong on knowledge, or compensates with time (pp. 16–17).
- **Cross-persona analysis.** Job 23 (cybersecurity teaching assistant) is often the top job. The authors trace this to the job's own data: 0% overtime probability, high hourly pay, short hours and high knowledge gain (pp. 19–20).
- **Sensitivity.** The same student is given a time-first judgment matrix (weights [0.209, 0.480, 0.120, 0.071, 0.120]). The top 3 becomes cybersecurity teaching, sanitation worker and waiter at Compass Group, all with no more than 4 hours of work. The recommended set (4, 8, 13, 23, 24, 26, 27, 30) is "significantly different" from the knowledge-inclined set (pp. 20–21).

## What makes it stand out
- **Indices that depend on the person as well as the job.** Net salary after commuting and meals, and schedule-conflict time, make the same job score differently for different students. This answers the statement's "based on their own situation" literally (pp. 7–8).
- **Recreation time budget.** The fixed 12-hour day turns "time for recreation" into a measurable quantity in the model rather than a vague preference (pp. 6, 8).
- **Probabilistic overtime handled with expected values.** This is a simple, explainable way to include a probabilistic factor (pp. 7–8).
- **Clustering for diversity.** Taking the best job per cluster prevents a shortlist of near-duplicates (pp. 13–14, 16). This is a reasoned design choice about what a useful recommendation looks like.
- **Diagnostic reading of results.** The authors explain *why* jobs rank high or low by going back to the index values (pp. 16, 19–20). This makes the results credible rather than black-box.
- **Strong visual identity.** A cover poster ("Unlock your Fabulous Summer Job Choice"), a work-structure flowchart, colour bar charts and dendrograms, and polished phone mock-ups (pp. 2, 5, 16, 21–24).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** context → method stack (AHP + TOPSIS + cluster analysis) → the five indices, with the time index explained → personas and worked example → claimed novelty → app (p. 1). It is qualitative, with no numerical results; keywords at the end.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction → Problem restatement & analysis → Our work (flowchart) → Assumptions & justification → Variables → Index extraction (one subsection per index) → Evaluation model (TOPSIS, AHP) → Cluster analysis → Results → Sensitivity → User experience → Strengths & weaknesses → References → Appendix.
- **Devices:** a full-page cover image placed before the contents (p. 2); a "Structure of Our Work" flowchart with an annotation for each step (p. 5); a variables table (p. 7); one subsection per index with its own formula; a single worked persona followed by a summary table for all ten; an index-profile chart for the recommended jobs (pp. 17, 21).
- **App:** consumer-facing. Each screen is described by function (search by tags, ranking that updates when personal information changes, Q&A forum, news feed). Privacy is mentioned: students may withhold school and gender "but this will result in a less targeted list" (p. 22). The weaknesses section is candid about the chat-box survey being inconvenient (p. 24).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "There are indeed some summer job evaluation forms provided by several institutions. However, these forms cannot provide a clear report or evaluation to people, since they only ask people some questions without forming a suggestion about their choices." (p. 4)
  - "Since the student's weighting for salary is also high, it's reasonable that the medium knowledge index can't compensate for the low salary and time index." (p. 16)

## Notes for reuse
- The job table is labelled "Fictional Job Data" but uses real-looking postings, and its source is not stated (pp. 25–27). If you reuse this design, document where each attribute (pay, overtime probability, meals) comes from.
- A few jobs dominate on several indices at once, so the same cybersecurity teaching assistant positions top most personas (pp. 19–20). With weighted ranking methods, check whether one job dominates the others before claiming the model is personalized. The per-cluster recommendation partly mitigates this.
