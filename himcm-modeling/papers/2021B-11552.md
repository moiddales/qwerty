# 2021B-11552: Tackling the Drought

## At a glance
The team treats lake volume as a bookkeeping identity V = V0 + I − O − L and builds a sub-model for each term, mostly as measurement procedures (river cross-section integrals, a scaled flow experiment, the Penman/Linacre evaporation formulas). Its most distinctive move is in Part 2: drought is defined twice, once by a simple below-average threshold and once by a **Bollinger Bands** rule borrowed from stock trading, and the Bollinger hyperparameters are then put through a sensitivity analysis. Two time-series models answer the forecasting task: a short-term ARMA on the last three years predicts a flat ~1082 ft (1082.40, 1082.37, 1082.31 ft for 2025/2030/2050), and a long-term ARIMA on 2005–2021 predicts a steady decline (1050.84, 1029.09, 942.10 ft). Part 3 lists five wastewater sources, policy levers for local leaders, and a recovery-rate index RR = WR/WD.

## How they read the problem
- The team restates each part as "Problem 1/2/3" with its sub-steps (pp. 4–5) and gives a one-paragraph "Our work" roadmap (p. 5).
- In Part 1a they read the three named flows as an equation and break each one into measurable physical quantities: inflow I = IR + IP with IR = Σ(S·v)·t over the Colorado and three tributaries (p. 7). Part 1b is read as "how would you survey it". They answer with a calculus procedure and a data-driven regression check (pp. 12–14).
- In Part 2 they separate a drought's *level* from its *direction*. The threshold criterion looks at the absolute water level, and the Bollinger criterion looks at the "gradient" (p. 15). This lets them argue that drought "does not completely depend on the absolute level of water level" (p. 16).
- The data before 1939 are excluded as reservoir filling (p. 15).
- For Model 1 ("most recent drought period") they use only the last three years of data, 2018–2021 (p. 17), not the 2002-onward drought that their own threshold criterion finds.

## Models and methods
- **Water balance and flow sub-models (Part 1a).** Inflow uses S = ∫(f_up − f_down)dx from surveyed surface and bed contours, and the mean velocity comes from averaging point samples (pp. 8–9). A FLUENT meshing example and a dimensional-analysis argument (Π = ΔP·A²·Q⁻²·ρ) show how a scaled model could estimate real flow (pp. 9–10). Release outflow is S_R·v_R·t_R (p. 10). Loss L = A·E·t, where E comes from the simplified Linacre/Penman lake-evaporation formula, which needs only elevation, latitude and temperature data (pp. 11–12). The rationale given is that this formula is simpler than full Penman and accurate to ~0.3 mm/day on annual means (p. 12). Nothing in this part is calibrated with data; it describes procedures.
- **Volume verification (Part 1b).** The cuboid formula V = A(E − H0) is extended to a sum over N slender columns, which the team links to a double integral (pp. 12–13). They cite the 1935 BOR mapping project as the real data route (p. 13). Then a two-parameter OLS fit of V on A·E to the four Table 1 points, with closed-form estimators, and a 3-D multiple regression (p. 14).
- **Drought criteria (Part 2a).** Criterion 1 marks a drought when the level falls below the long-run mean "and failed to recover back to average level soon enough" (p. 16). Criterion 2 marks a drought when the moving average breaks through the lower Bollinger band (MA − β·std) "with a high slope". The ±1σ choice is motivated by the normal-distribution coverage ("Pauta criterion", 68.26%) (pp. 16–17). Window = 4 years (48 months) and β = 1.
- **Forecasts (Part 2b).** Both are Box–Jenkins workflows: stationarity check (ADF), ACF/PACF plots, order chosen by AIC/BIC, residual diagnostics (Ljung-Box), then forecast (pp. 17–20). The workflow is shown with regression-output tables and fitted-versus-original plots.
- **Plan metric (Part 3).** RR = WR/WD is defined as the share of demand met by reclaimed water (p. 22).

## Data
They use only the two COMAP elevation files and Table 1, plus formulas and constants from the literature. No external hydrological time series are brought in. Data before 1939 are dropped for the pattern analysis, and the series is shown both de-meaned and first-differenced (p. 15). Evaporation and flow inputs are described as things to measure, not estimated values.

## Assumptions
There is a short numbered list with a justification sentence for each (p. 5). Examples:
- Volume changes only through inflow, outflow and loss, "according to the given information description".
- Annual precipitation inflow is constant, because "the total annual precipitation is not much different" at a yearly scale.
- Direct consumption from the lake is constant, because its uncertainty is large.
- The historical level data are accurate and the dry-period trend since 2000 continues.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Sensitivity of the drought detector** (Section 7, pp. 22–23). With β fixed at 1, the window is set to 12, 24, 48 and 96 months (Fig 7.1, four panels). With the window fixed at 48, β is set to 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 (Fig 7.2). They explain what goes wrong at each extreme: small windows cause frequent crossings, and large windows miss "relatively micro rise and drop". They conclude that window = 48 and β = 1 are reasonable.
- Time-series diagnostics (ADF, ACF/PACF, AIC/BIC, Ljung-Box and a residual histogram) for the ARMA model (pp. 18–19).
- Model comparison: STTS is called "unreliable" for decades-ahead use, LTTS is used as a check (p. 18), and LTTS is preferred because it uses more history (p. 20).
- Strengths/weaknesses section (p. 24). It names the constant-precipitation and constant-consumption assumptions as "too strong".

## What makes it stand out
- **A borrowed tool that fits the job.** Bollinger Bands from finance are used as an adaptive drought detector based on moving average ± k·σ. They work on the *trend* of the level, not one fixed elevation, which answers "define your criteria" in an original, data-driven way (pp. 16–17).
- **Two criteria side by side.** Giving a simple threshold rule and a more sensitive band rule shows how much the drought periods depend on the definition. The team says the band method is "more sensitive than Criterion 1" (p. 17).
- **Sensitivity on the definition itself.** Most teams test model parameters. This one tests the parameters of its drought detector, with paired 2×2 figure grids (p. 23), which turns a subjective choice into a justified one.
- **The two forecasts are contrasted plainly.** A flat ~1082 ft against a fall to ~942 ft by 2050 is reported with a short explanation of why the longer window is more credible (pp. 19–20).
- **Part 1b is answered in two ways.** There is a calculus survey procedure and a regression check against Table 1 (pp. 12–14).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** four paragraphs, one per part plus sensitivity. Methods are named (IOL model, OLS, Bollinger Bands, STTS/LTTS, RR) and the forecasts are stated qualitatively ("remain above 1080 feet", "gradually decline"). It ends with keywords. It is light on numbers.
- **Paper structure:** News Report → Introduction (background, restatement, our work) → Assumptions → Problem 1 → Problem 2 → Problem 3 → Sensitivity Analysis → Strengths and Weaknesses → References.
- **Devices:** a model-pipeline figure for the volume decomposition (Fig 4.2), clear sub-model headings (Inflow/Outflow/Loss Model), numbered Box–Jenkins steps, regression-output screenshots, shaded drought periods on the full time series (Fig 5.3), and 2×2 sensitivity panels.
- **Letter/memo/article:** the one-page news report comes first in the paper (p. 3). It opens with a 1984/2021 satellite-photo comparison, walks through the investigation in plain words, gives the long-run warning (level "lower than 950 feet"), and ends with a call to everyone.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "We come up with threshold value strategy and Bollinger Bands strategy for the drought periods prediction, which focus on the absolute value and the gradient of the water level respectively." (p. 15)
  - "When we extract dry periods according this idea, we should filter out micro changes in water levels, which may cause the dry period to be too fragmented and lose continuity." (p. 16)
  - "We are able to quantify the impact of our plan using wastewater recycling as a percentage of water demand." (p. 22)

## Notes for reuse
- Model 1 is fitted only to 2018–2021, while the team's own threshold criterion dates the current drought from 2002 (Table 5.1, p. 16). The near-flat Model 1 forecast follows from that window choice. If you imitate this, tie the forecasting window explicitly to your Part 2a definition.
- Part 3 defines RR but never sets the recycled volume against the shortfall implied by the forecasts. So the question of whether recycling could cover "all or part" of the shortfall is answered qualitatively.
