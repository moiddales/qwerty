# 2021B-11576: The Recognition, Prediction and Countermeasure for the Drought in Lake Mead

## At a glance
This paper is the most quantitative of the 2021B set in Parts 1a and 3. Part 1a builds a **numerical annual water budget** for Lake Mead: inflow ≈ 9.0 maf, outflow ≈ −9.6 maf, evaporation ≈ −0.6 maf, a net −1.2 maf, which the team translates into "a rate of 12 ft per year" (p. 11). Drought is defined by a **policy-based threshold of 1115 ft**, taken from the Bureau of Reclamation's Interim Guidelines. Five drought periods are then characterized by duration, average level and fluctuation (p. 14). Two forecasts follow. A plain ARIMA on the 2013–2021 drought gives 1049.82 / 1024.57 / 927.77 ft for 2025/2030/2050. A Trend-Seasonal-ARIMA on 2005-onward data gives 1055.50 / 1035.59 / 1006.42 ft. The wastewater plan is an **integer program** that chooses how many secondary, advanced and no-discharge treatment facilities to build at minimum cost. The answer is 2, 8 and 9 new facilities for about $602.8 million, spread over 2021–2030.

## How they read the problem
- They restate the requirements as three bullets (p. 4) and give an "Our Approach" overview (p. 5). A "Model Overview" bullet list (pp. 6–7) and flowchart (Fig 1, p. 7) show the five components (Water Budget Balance, Calculation Relationship, Drought Recognition, Waterlevel Forecast, Wastewater Recycling Plan) and how they feed each other.
- Each major section opens with an "Analysis" subsection that lists the "features need to be clarified" before modeling (pp. 7, 12, 18). Every part therefore starts by breaking the question into explicit sub-questions.
- They read Part 1a as a budgeting exercise. They collect actual magnitudes for the flows from Bureau of Reclamation material and the literature, instead of only naming factors (pp. 9–11).
- Drought is tied to *water-supply consequences*. Below 1115 ft, they argue, the lake "is incapable of supporting" the ~7.5 maf delivered each year (p. 13).
- They interpret the plan as an infrastructure-investment decision for local leaders under a budget. Assumption 3 explicitly scopes the plan to large treatment facilities, not household reuse (p. 5).

## Models and methods
- **Water budget (1a).** The yearly balance V(t) = V(t−1) + V_input − V_output − V_loss (p. 9) is filled with magnitudes from cited sources. Inflow is gauged at Diamond Creek (Barnett & Pierce), and evaporation of ~0.6 maf (≈7% of inflow) comes from Bureau of Reclamation data (p. 10). A correlation matrix of four budget factors computed in MATLAB (Fig 4, p. 11) is read physically: bank storage "tends to be saturated under sufficient inflow" and makes up for inflow when inflow is low (p. 10). Table 2 sums these to −1.2 maf per year (p. 11).
- **Elevation–area–volume (1b).** Area is assumed quadratic in elevation, so the volume integral is cubic. The area coefficients are fitted first, then the base elevation L0 is fitted to the volumes (eqs. 4–6, pp. 11–12). The rationale is that the integral turns the irregular-shape problem into finding A(L). The MATLAB code in Appendix B.1 shows the polyfit/fmincon steps (p. 23).
- **Drought recognition (2a).** The threshold is justified by a supply argument (p. 13) and illustrated by a drought-degree figure (Fig 6). The indices are duration Nᵢ, mean level and standard deviation within each period (eqs. 7–8, p. 14).
- **Forecasts (2b).** Model 1 is ARIMA on the latest drought only (MATLAB `arima`, code p. 23). Model 2 decomposes Lₜ = Tₜ + Sₜ + Iₜ (eqs. 11–13, p. 16). The trend is a 12-month moving average, the seasonal terms are means of the season's months, and ARIMA models the irregular residual. The rationale is that Model 2's longer series needs trend and seasonality handled explicitly (p. 16).
- **Facility plan (3b).** Decision variables are the counts of three facility types. Capacity and cost per facility come from Hernández-Chover et al. (Table 6, p. 19). The required 2030 capacity is obtained by scaling 2018 capacity by the ratio of drawdowns below 1219 ft (eq. 15, p. 20). The 2030 level comes from Model 1. Minimum-count constraints keep the existing facilities. The result is spread evenly over a decade "to alleviate the financial pressure" (p. 20).

## Data
This is the most externally sourced paper in the set. Sources include Bureau of Reclamation budget figures and the 2007 Interim Guidelines, USGS/academic water-budget papers, a 217-plant study of wastewater-facility cost and capacity, Nevada facility counts for 2004/2012/2018, and an EPA estimate ($3.08 billion, 55.9% for advanced treatment) (pp. 10, 19). Assumption 5 states that all reviewed data are accurate (p. 6). The attachment data feed the drought and forecast parts.

## Assumptions
There are five general assumptions, each with a "↪ Justification" line (pp. 5–6). Examples:
- Sedimentation and microbial effects are negligible relative to the lake's volume.
- Water level is predictable because the series is autocorrelated. This explicitly justifies the time-series route.
- The plan relies on large treatment facilities, because household-level reuse is technically impractical.
- Current treatment technology will not be overtaken by breakthroughs, because breakthroughs "are often unpredictable and irregular".

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Model fit plots for both forecasts (Figs 8–9, pp. 15, 17). The code computes an R²-style fit for Model 1 (p. 23).
- The 1b fit is shown against the four Table 1 points (Fig 5, p. 12).
- One-factor sensitivity (Section 6.4, p. 21): expected wastewater volume is varied ±40% in 10% steps, and the counts of each facility type and the total budget are plotted (Fig 12, four panels). The authors read the monotone response as evidence of "rationality and robustness".
- An "Advantages / Possible Improvements" section (p. 22).

## What makes it stand out
- **The budget turns the factors into numbers.** Part 1a ends with a four-line budget table and a 12 ft/yr implication (p. 11), not just a factor list. The same number reappears in the drought discussion (p. 15) and in the news article.
- **The drought threshold is anchored in policy.** Using the Bureau's operating guideline (1115 ft) and a delivery argument gives the criterion real-world meaning (p. 13).
- **Drought periods are profiled.** Table 3 compares duration, average level and volatility for all five periods (p. 14), which directly answers "how does the most recent drought compare".
- **Model 2 differs from Model 1 in method, not only in data window.** The trend + seasonal + ARIMA decomposition handles intra-annual cycles, and the two models' monthly forecasts are tabulated side by side (pp. 16–17).
- **The plan is solved as an optimization with a schedule.** The integer program gives a concrete facility mix and cost. Phasing the cost over 2021–2030 (Fig 11, p. 21) addresses the decision-maker's budget constraint the problem mentions.
- **The modeling chain is visible.** The Model 1 forecast for 2030 drives the plan's required capacity (eq. 15), so Parts 2 and 3 are explicitly linked (p. 20).

  - "The results show that the recent drought has a relatively higher duration fluctuation level even though its average elevation remains the same compared to other drought periods." (p. 15)

## Notes for reuse
- The required 2030 treatment capacity (eq. 15, p. 20) scales 2018 capacity by the ratio of drawdowns below 1219 ft. This proportionality is not justified, yet the whole facility count and budget depend on it. The claimed recovery of "7.9 ft every year" (p. 20) is also not derived in the text.
- The facility cost and capacity figures come from a general study of 217 wastewater plants and are applied directly to Nevada (Table 6, p. 19). The cost row in eq. 14 differs in scale from Table 6 and eq. 16. Check units and transferability before reusing such figures.
