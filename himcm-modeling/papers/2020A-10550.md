# 2020A-10550: Solution for Problem A of HiMCM 2020

## At a glance
The team treats the best summer job as a multiple-criteria decision problem where the criteria influence each other. They solve it with the Analytic Network Process (ANP) rather than AHP. Ten real UK student jobs (from studentjob.co.uk) are evaluated on 15 criteria in four clusters: Salary, Benefit, Work conditions and Suitability. Some criteria depend only on the job; others (commuting and the four "match" criteria) depend jointly on the job and the user's profile. Each user's 0–100 questionnaire answers set the cluster weights. The limiting supermatrix then gives a priority for *every* job, so users receive a full ranking. Ten Monte Carlo–generated students get visibly different rankings (Table 9, p. 17). A sensitivity section shows how changing the user's weights or the between-cluster weights flips the top job (pp. 17–19).

## How they read the problem
- They define "best" explicitly: a job from which the student "can have most gains (financial, intellectual, and mental, etc.), most likes (mostly match his/her preferences), and has great opportunity to own it" (p. 4). This definition is restated and justified as Assumption 1 (p. 5). Including the chance of *getting* the job is an unusual and realistic addition, and it motivates the "skill match" and "experience match" criteria (p. 8).
- They identify the problem type directly: "The best job selection problem in nature is a multiple criteria decision making (MCMD) problems" (p. 4). They argue that the decision factors "might interrelated with each other", which motivates a network model rather than a hierarchy (pp. 4, 7).
- They separate *job properties* (scraped data) from *user situation* (form: location, skills, interests, personality, expected salary, recreation hours) and *user preferences* (questionnaire). Each enters a different part of the supermatrix (pp. 5–6, 10).
- They model a realistic product pipeline: scrape jobs → filter out obviously unsuitable ones (salary far below expectation, missing required skills) → ANP solver (pp. 5–7).

## Models and methods
- **ANP network.** A goal node, four criteria clusters and an alternatives (jobs) cluster. Arcs carry dependence and feedback, including jobs ↔ criteria and within/between criteria clusters (Figure 2, p. 9). Rationale: ANP "uses a network structure to consider the dependence between models and the interaction and feedback between evaluation criteria", which AHP and decision trees do not (p. 7).
- **Supermatrix construction.** 21 sub-matrices W_mn are identified (Table 2, p. 11). They are classified by what information they need (p. 10):
  - job–criteria blocks (from job data);
  - user × job blocks W56/W65 (suitability);
  - inter-criteria blocks, which are "determined by our experience".

  Each column is built from a Saaty 1–9 pairwise comparison and its principal eigenvector. Where a criterion is quantitative (salary, commuting cost), they use the ratios of the actual values instead of judgements (p. 11). A worked example builds the "interest match" column for Student A from his profile (Tables 3–4, pp. 12–13).
- **Consistency.** CR = CI/RI with Saaty's RI table; the threshold is CR ≤ 0.10 (p. 13).
- **Cluster weights.** A cluster-weights matrix (Table 6, p. 14) weights the unweighted supermatrix so that columns sum to 1. Its last column, w1–w4, comes from the user's 0–100 importance scores for payment, skills improvement, work conditions and interest/skill match (p. 7). Student A's weights are 0.2/0.4/0.2/0.2 (Table 7, p. 16).
- **Limiting matrix.** The weighted supermatrix is raised to powers until the columns converge (80–100 iterations). Job priorities are then read from the alternatives block and normalized (pp. 15–16).
- **Efficiency design.** Job-related weights are stratified by job *type* (e.g. camp counselor vs store cashier) and by individual job details. Only user-related blocks are recomputed for a new user, and only job-detail blocks when the job list changes (p. 11). This shows the team thinking about how the model would run as an app.

## Data
- Ten real postings from studentjob.co.uk, structured into a table: location, hourly rate, weekly salary, leadership, friendship, technical skills, communication skills, environment, weekly hours, flexibility, safety, personality and experience requirements (Appendix A, p. 23). Scraping tools and other sites (summerjobs.com, indeed.com, goodwall.io, 58.com) are described as the general data route (p. 6).
- Each workplace is a separate job record, so commuting distance can be computed (p. 6). In the experiment, commuting costs were "directly derived" for simplicity rather than computed with GIS (p. 15).
- The full unweighted and limiting supermatrices are given as appendices (pp. 24–25).

## Assumptions
There are three assumptions, each with a labelled "Justification" paragraph (p. 5):
- the definition of "best job" (above);
- search costs are ignored and jobs of a type (e.g. all camp counselors) are treated as the same, while "In theory our model supports very detailed profiling of jobs and users";
- randomly generated persons can stand in for real users, because real personal data was not available.

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Face validity per persona.** For each of the 10 persons, the top-ranked jobs are compared with the profile, using red/green colour coding in Table 9 (p. 17). The authors state that the best jobs "agree well with the users' situation/preferences".
- **User-weight sensitivity.** Student B's cluster weights are swapped from salary-focused (w1 = 0.5, w2 = 0.1) to benefit-focused (w1 = 0.1, w2 = 0.5). The top job moves from Athletic Fitness Model (0.142) to Student Brand Ambassador (0.129) (Table 10, p. 18).
- **Structural-weight sensitivity.** For Student C, the influence of Suitability on Benefit is raised (column weights changed from 0.2/0.1/0.3/0.4 to 0.1/0.1/0.6/0.2). The best job changes from Camp Counselor to Athletic Fitness Model (pp. 18–19).
- **Discrimination test.** P2 and P10 share the same personality and goal and differ only in fitness vs communication skill. They receive different best jobs (Athletic Model vs Telephone Survey Advisor), which shows the model responds to the user-situation blocks (p. 19).

## What makes it stand out
- **Method matched to a stated structural feature.** The team argues that criteria interact and that jobs feed back to criteria, so they choose ANP (p. 7). The colour-coded network (p. 9) shows which criteria are job-only and which are user-dependent.
- **"Chance of getting the job" as part of best.** Skill and experience match turn the problem from preference ranking into two-sided matching (pp. 4, 8).
- **Real job postings.** Using actual listings rather than generic job types gives concrete wages, hours and requirements (p. 23).
- **Reproducible persona generation.** Personas are drawn by Monte Carlo from defined option sets (e.g. MBTI 16 types, low/medium/high skills), and inconsistent draws are explicitly fixed (p. 15).
- **Sensitivity that probes model structure as well as inputs.** The paper separates the user-controlled weights from the modeller's between-cluster weights and shows that both can change the decision. It recommends keeping the inter-criteria nodes fixed (pp. 17–19).
- **Product-minded presentation.** The app design covers the cloud database, GPS location for commuting, a wizard for profile input, a 0–100 importance questionnaire that maps directly to w1–w4, and a ranked output list (pp. 19–20).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** context → method and problem type (ANP for an MCDM problem) → 15 criteria in four aspects, each defined in a sentence → validation with ten jobs and ten persons → ranked output → app (p. 1). It is qualitative and gives no numerical results.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction & restatement → Problem analysis (per question) → Assumptions → Methodology (workflow, data, ANP steps) → Results → Sensitivity analysis → Presentation → Strengths & limitations → Conclusion → References → Appendices (supermatrices).
- **Devices:** a "Problem analysis" section that plans the answer to each question before any modeling (p. 4); a methodological flowchart (p. 6); a network diagram with colour meaning (p. 9); a worked example of one sub-matrix column with its comparison matrix and eigenvector (pp. 12–13); a heat-map table of priorities across all personas (p. 17); a before/after sensitivity table (p. 18).
- **App presentation:** described screen by screen with a caption mapping a)–e). The questionnaire wording shown on p. 7 is the exact user-facing text, which links the math to the interface.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "We mean a best job for high school students as a job offering great gains, matching students' preferences, able to be completed by high school students, and of course, having great chance for the student to own it." (p. 3)
  - "When jobs are updated, we only need to recalculate the sub-matrices related to individual job details; when new user uses our model, we only need to recalculate sub-matrices related to user." (p. 11)
  - "Making change on those nodes will also impact the results but we recommend keeping them unchanged throughout the process." (p. 19)

## Notes for reuse
- The between-cluster weights (Table 6) and the inter-criteria sub-matrices are "determined by our experience" (p. 10). The paper's own sensitivity test shows they can change the best job (pp. 18–19). If you adopt ANP, state how such structural weights are set.
- The personas' questionnaire answers and comparison matrices were filled in by the team from the generated profiles ("we filled out questionnaire", p. 15). The model's agreement with profiles is therefore partly built in by construction, and is a plausibility check rather than independent validation.
