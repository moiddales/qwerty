# 2019A-9844: Increasing Charging Electricity Demands and Reducing the Cost in Public Places

## At a glance
The team first estimates electricity consumed by public charging for EVs and mobile phones, then predicts the next five years with Bass diffusion. It converts ownership, battery capacity, charging frequency, efficiency loss and the fraction of charging done in public into annual energy. The forecast gives about 3.2 million pure EVs and 283.81 million smartphones in 2020, with 1.69×10^9 kWh of combined modeled power consumption (p. 9). A cost model adds equipment investment, construction, maintenance, electricity, land and the user’s waiting time. Four worked scenarios—New York café, JFK airport, King's Plaza mall and Stanford University—show how utilization and scale change the result. Their practical recommendations vary by setting: control queue time, adjust outlet density, use solar generation and let commercial sites recover costs through customer benefits (pp. 1–2, 13–20).

## How they read the problem
They interpret demand as energy actually delivered in public, rather than all electricity used by devices. The paper chooses the United States as its analysis area and separates large equipment (EVs) from small equipment (phones), because their batteries, charging powers and infrastructure differ (pp. 2–4). A schematic scope figure lays out the pipeline: collect ownership and battery data, calculate public energy, forecast, then cost representative sites (p. 2). The four scenarios are selected to cover compact commercial, high-volume transport, large retail and non-profit education contexts (pp. 13–18).

## Models and methods
For EVs, annual energy is built from number of vehicles, battery capacity, charging frequency and efficiency/loss factors; only a stated portion of charging occurs at public stations. For phones, ownership, battery capacity, charging frequency and public-use share are treated analogously. The resulting series is an energy demand rather than a sales count, which permits a direct electricity-cost calculation (pp. 3–8).

The Bass model captures adoption with innovation and imitation terms. Separate fits are made for pure EV ownership and mobile-phone data, and the fitted curves extrapolate demand through the next five years (pp. 8–10). The paper reports a 2020 pure-EV prediction of about 3.2 million, close to a cited external estimate of 3.3 million (p. 9).

The cost model has two parallel branches. Monetary costs include purchase/installation and construction, maintenance, electricity and floor area. Queue-time cost monetizes waiting as the value of a user’s time; for EVs it is tied to demand and available stations, while phone charging cost includes socket utilization and power. The formulas are then populated with site-specific hours, rates and counts (pp. 10–17).

## Data
EV ownership and battery data, BEV market share, phone ownership, battery capacities and charging behavior are collected for 2011–2018 (pp. 6–9). Equipment prices and electricity rates are cited. Site examples include New York and Los Angeles observations, airport and shopping-mall scales, and a school population. Some values are explicit assumptions; the paper lists them beside the scenario calculations and in the appendices (pp. 13–18, 22–28).

## Assumptions
The analysis area is the United States; iPhone capacity represents mobile phones; charging efficiency and public charging shares are treated as stable over the forecast; and each scenario has a representative operating time and utilization. Queue-time cost assumes users value time and that limited stations create competition (pp. 3–12). The authors also assume equipment and electricity costs can be added linearly. Sensitivity tables vary uncertain parameters rather than hiding them (pp. 20–23).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The EV forecast is checked against an external 2020 estimate (p. 9). Historical fitted curves and five-year forecasts are plotted for both device classes (pp. 8–10). Four scenarios test transfer across different scales and utilization levels; Figure 8 compares their total charging costs (pp. 13–18). A sensitivity analysis perturbs model parameters and reports which factors most affect cost (pp. 20–23). The article and conclusion make the same recommendations at a nontechnical level, providing a communication check on the technical result.

## What makes it stand out
- Energy is derived from physical use (battery, frequency, losses and public share) before any dollars are applied.
- Bass diffusion is a natural way to represent adoption that accelerates and then saturates, rather than extrapolating one straight line.
- Queue-time cost acknowledges that a charging station consumes users’ time and space as well as electricity.
- The four scenarios make the model actionable: an airport’s 24-hour utilization is not treated like a café’s, and a school has different revenue options.
- Sensitivity tables identify cost drivers and support targeted initiatives.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The summary sheet follows the model’s causal order—data, energy, forecast, cost, scenarios, initiatives—and the newspaper page translates the result into anxiety about a dead phone or EV (pp. 1, 21). A schematic overview on p. 2 and a variables/assumptions section on pp. 3–4 orient the reader before equations. Scenario pages repeat a clear recipe: state inputs, substitute into the formula, show a chart, then interpret. MATLAB code and sensitivity output are preserved in the appendices (pp. 22–28), while the main text remains readable.

## Notes for reuse
- Document battery, utilization and public-share assumptions with units; these dominate energy results.
- Treat queue-time as a separate user cost and show how changing station count trades capital cost against waiting.
