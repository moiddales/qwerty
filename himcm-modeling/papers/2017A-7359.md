# 2017A-7359: Drone Clusters as Sky Light Displays

## At a glance
The team set the show at a 2018 New Year celebration and built three displays: a rotating Ferris wheel, a "Western dragon" whose wings beat in 2D and 3D, and a New Year countdown clock that turns into the characters "2018". The key idea is to sort each display by its type of motion. Rigid motion (wheel, clock) is handled with closed-form parametric coordinates rotating about a fixed point. Non-rigid motion (dragon) uses a multi-link "skeleton" driven by rotation matrices, with "skin attachment" drones that follow it. Changes between images use the team's own "dense scatter diffusion algorithm", built on a law-of-cosines argument plus staggered departure times. The engineering plan calls for 420 drones plus 10 spares, a 430 m² launch field, a 10-minute show and about $12,600 in drone rental. The memo recommends going ahead.

## How they read the problem
- Before any modelling, a "Problem Analysis" section (pp. 5–7) breaks the task into five sub-problems: fixed-point analysis, flight-path analysis, three-dimensional trajectory analysis, flight-collision safety, and "other issues" (size, pixel density under budget, number of drones, area, site).
- The central reframing is to call the Ferris wheel and clock **rigid-body motion of regular objects** and the dragon **non-rigid motion of irregular objects** (p. 6). Rigid motion turns path planning into "circular motion around the fixed point". Non-rigid motion needs a skeleton (link) model.
- Each display gets a "fixed point" (the wheel hub, the clock centre, the centre of the dragon's body) that anchors the coordinates and the motion (pp. 5–6).
- Collision avoidance is treated as a design problem for every phase. The wheel is layered by altitude, the dragon enforces a minimum safety distance, and image switching uses the diffusion algorithm (pp. 6–7).
- Light colour and wind are left out (assumption 2, p. 7). Drones are treated as points (assumption 4).

## Models and methods
- **Parametric geometry of the wheel (pp. 10–12).** The origin is the wheel centre. Stand legs are y = ±x tan β with evenly spaced points. Spoke points are (jd cos iγ, jd sin iγ). Gravity-type cabins hang from the rim at (x_i, y_i − r), and observation-type cabins sit on the extended spoke at radius R + r. The team compared three real Ferris-wheel types and dropped the spokeless one as rare (p. 9). This is a good example of using real-world structure to decide geometry.
- **Rotation model (pp. 13–14).** Adding θ to every angular argument turns the static equations into trajectories. The paper then states each type of point's path, such as "a circle with the center … and radius R".
- **Enhancements (pp. 14–17).** Curved spokes come from composed 2D rotation matrices (B + C − CB). The inner rims expand and contract periodically, driven by the fractional part of T1·θ/2π. Stand drones circulate using a mod function over the perimeter index. All are written as functions of the wheel angle θ, so they stay in sync with the rotation.
- **Launch scheduling (p. 13; pp. 27–29).** Batches arrive at T_i = (i−1)t + (2R+H+h)/v. Each drone takes off at T_ij = T_i − D_ij/v. Flight time uses a trapezoidal velocity profile, t = (aS + v²)/(av) when S > v²/a (eq. 36), with a = 1 m/s² and v = 5 m/s.
- **Dragon skeleton (pp. 18–20).** Each limb is a chain of links rotated in sequence: A1′ − A0 = Z1(A1 − A0), A2′ − A1′ = Z2Z1(A2 − A1), and so on. Outline drones in region i follow the same product of rotations (eq. 31). 3D flapping embeds the plane as yoz and applies a rotation about the z axis (eq. 32). Rationale: a "multi-link system" (refs [4]–[6]) can express non-rigid motion. The team claims the method generalises to "any non-rigid graphic" (p. 21).
- **Dense scatter diffusion algorithm (pp. 21–24).** If two drones leave fixed points along rays from a common point C at the same speed, the law of cosines gives c²_{n+1} − c²_n = 2v(1 − cos C)(a + b + v + 2nv) ≥ 0, so their separation never shrinks (eq. 33). The dense clock image is treated as one such point. Drones are matched greedily: the source point farthest from the target set goes first, with rows and columns deleted from the distance matrix. This was sped up from O(N³) to about O(N^{3/2}) by matching only the outermost points. Each drone's departure is delayed by (E(k,5) − E(1,5))/v so that all arrive at the same time. A fix synchronises departures when a later point must travel farther. The same idea, run in reverse or chained through a dense intermediate, covers any 2D or 3D pair of patterns (p. 24).
- **Pipeline.** Image to boundary scatter, then static coordinates, then launch schedule, then animation equations, then a dense-intermediate transition to the next image. Everything was simulated in MATLAB (code on pp. 34–41).

## Data
- Drone specifications come from Intel's Shooting Star page (ref. [1], p. 25): 384 × 384 × 93 mm, maximum climb 5 m/s, horizontal 18 m/s, 27-minute flight time, 120 m ceiling. The team chose to cap speed at 5 m/s and set a minimum safety space of 1 m × 1 m × 0.5 m.
- Image data are the team's own drawings (dragon) and designs (clock, "2018"), processed in MATLAB.
- Cost uses "the unit price of 30 dollars/drone" (p. 26) with no source given. The fireworks comparison in the memo (≈ $28,500) also has no source.
- Display sizes, spacing and timings are design choices recorded in parameter tables.

## Assumptions
Section 2.1 (p. 7) lists four short assumptions: identical good-quality drones, no wind, constant acceleration then deceleration, and drones as points. More of the working assumptions are built into the **parameter tables** (Tables 2–5, pp. 26–30). Each table has a "S.T." column giving the constraint each value must satisfy, for example "n7 = 76 ≤ 2π·R1 ≈ 188", "R2 = 28 m ≤ R1 − 1 = 29" and "H′ < 80 − 2R1 − H". This makes the design choices checkable against the 1 m spacing and the 80 m performance box. Another example is the choice of a 15-minute show cap against the 27-minute battery: "for safety reason, we set the duration of the performance to be no more than 15 minutes" (p. 26).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Validation is by MATLAB simulation of every phase: launch frames, wheel rotation frames, dragon flapping in 2D and 3D, and clock-to-"2018" diffusion frames (Figs. 9, 11, 13, 20, 23, 26–29).
- The non-collision property of diffusion is argued analytically (eq. 33). The anti-collision fix is reported to make collisions much less frequent in the simulated trajectories (p. 24).
- Timing is checked against a lower bound. Table 6 gives a "Limit time" (minimum feasible, 210 s in total) next to a "Suggested time" (600 s in total) for each phase (p. 31).
- There is no formal parameter sensitivity analysis. Weaknesses (p. 32) note that biological effects such as tail wagging and non-rigid deformation of the skin were not modelled.

## What makes it stand out
- **Motion-type taxonomy as the organising idea (pp. 5–7).** Splitting displays into rigid and non-rigid motion gives each display a fitting model and a clear storyline.
- **Real mechanical detail (pp. 9–12).** Modelling gravity-type and observation-type cabins, and the stand, gives drone-level coordinates that look like a real Ferris wheel. These are not generic circles.
- **Skeleton-plus-skin animation (pp. 18–21).** Borrowed from character animation, this lets a complex outline move naturally with only a few controlled rotation angles.
- **A provable transition idea (pp. 22–24).** The law-of-cosines lemma gives a short, checkable reason why diverging from a dense cluster is safe. Delayed departures turn it into a usable schedule, and complexity is discussed.
- **Constraint columns in parameter tables (pp. 26–30).** Each number comes with the inequality it must satisfy, which shows the design was checked and not just picked.
- **Limit-versus-suggested schedule (p. 31).** Separating the physically minimal time from the recommended time is an honest way to present duration.
- **Visual memo (pp. 3–4).** Four figure strips show the whole show at a glance before any numbers.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** one page. It opens with context (Intel's show, the mayor's request), gives the show concept and drone count, lists the modelling components (fixed points, paths, 3D trajectories, anti-collision switching), and ends with the deliverables of the simulation (number of drones, area, launch path, interval, budget, site). There are few numbers, and it ends with keywords.
- **Paper structure:** Summary → Contents → Memo → Introduction/Restatement/Problem Analysis → Assumptions & Variables → Image preprocessing → Mathematical Models (wheel, dragon, clock) → Project design & simulation → Strengths/Weaknesses → Further Work → References → MATLAB appendix.
- **Devices:** a problem-analysis section ahead of the models; variable lists grouped by display; step-numbered algorithms; parameter tables with constraint ("S.T.") columns; frame-strip simulation figures; a schedule table with limit and suggested times.
- **Letter/memo:** addressed to "Mr. Mayor", in four headed parts (Plan and Design; Reasons for Using Drones; Plan for the Site; Cost and Budget). It argues on art and technology, environmental benefit compared with fireworks gases, ease of use and safety. Numbers are given in everyday terms (drone size in mm, take-off area in m², dollar totals).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "the flight trajectory problem of UAV is transformed into the trajectory equation problem of three kinds of points making circular motion around the fixed point." (p. 6)
  - "if the two UAVs start from fixed points and diffuse at a uniform linear speed, the distance between them will be farther away or remains constant. Therefore, collisions will never occur." (p. 22)
  - "All you have to do is to provide an image, then we can finish off by designing, planning of trajectory, and determining number of drones." (p. 4)

## Notes for reuse
- The no-collision guarantee in eq. 33 holds only under the approximation that the dense source image is a single point. For the real matching the paper itself says only that its fix "significantly reduces the occurrence frequency of collisions" (p. 24). Imitators should present it as a heuristic backed by simulation, not as a proof.
- The headline drone count differs across sections: 528 in the summary sheet (p. 1), 420 + 10 spares in the memo and body (pp. 3, 26). The $30-per-drone rental price behind the budget has no source.
