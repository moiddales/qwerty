# 2018A-8700: From Rollercoaster's Indicators to "RollerOutstander"

## At a glance
The team built an entropy-weighted index from six objective indicators chosen by a feasibility screen and grey relational analysis, after filling heavily missing columns with a back-propagation (BP) neural network (pp. 1, 8–16). Their Top 10 is headed by Kingda Ka, Top Thrill Dragster and Superman: Escape from Krypton (Fig. 8, p. 17). Single-indicator perturbation tests give the tolerance range of each indicator before the ranking changes (pp. 18–19). The comparison with two online lists goes beyond counting overlaps: radar charts of mean characteristics, a world map and a discussion of US-centred bias explain *why* the lists differ (pp. 19–22). Half the paper is a full product design for the app "RollerOutstander": fuzzy-evaluation filtering, an item-based collaborative filtering plus tag-based recommender with evaluation metrics, and a six-screen prototype (pp. 22–35).

## How they read the problem
- A short literature review sorts existing rankings into "entirely quantitative ones" (RCDB record lists) and "the ones based on the subjective judgments" (Ranker) (p. 6), which positions their model as a multi-indicator objective alternative.
- They go column by column through the spreadsheet saying what each field is for: location fields kept only for the app, Status dropped, year treated as "more advanced technology," inversion yes/no merged into the count (pp. 7–8).
- Candidate indicators include ones not in the data (sales volume, price, favourable comments, ride limit), rejected in a reason table as hard to obtain or quantify (Tables 1–2, pp. 12–13): a clear statement of what "objective" means for them.
- They separate the objective ranking (Task 2) from the app's ranking, where subjective factors are deliberately readmitted through fuzzy evaluation (p. 24).

## Models and methods
- **Preprocessing.** Min-max normalization (p. 8). Z-score outliers beyond ±3 are deleted and re-imputed (example: Smiler's 14 inversions, Z = 4.52, p. 8). Minimal indicators converted to maximal by translation (p. 8). Small gaps filled with means; large gaps (drop, duration, G-force, vertical angle) with a three-layer BP network trained on the other specifications, with momentum ("correction matrix") updates (pp. 9–11). Rationale: mean substitution "does not apply" to large-scale missingness, and the BP network was chosen "after comparing several different methods" (p. 9). Error falls to 3.48% after more than 100,000 iterations (Fig. 3, p. 11), and train/test predictions are overlaid (Fig. 4, p. 11).
- **Construction and type.** A 3D scatter of height–speed–length shows steel's advantage, so wood and steel are treated separately (Fig. 5, pp. 12–13). Type enters as a multiplier T = [1.00, 1.15, 1.10, 1.10, 1.20] for sit down, inverted, stand up, suspended, flying (p. 13).
- **Grey relational analysis** (resolution coefficient ρ = 0.5) produces a correlation heatmap used to drop redundant indicators; the text names speed, length and drop as rejected (p. 15). The final system in Fig. 7 (p. 15) is inversions, G-force, vertical angle, length, height and year.
- **Entropy weight method** gives the indicator weights d_j; AHP was considered but rejected because it "refers to the experts' opinion and reduces the objectivity" (p. 16). Score Q_i = Σ d_j z_ij T_i (p. 16). A PCA section reports that four components explain 90.576% of variance (Table 4, p. 17).
- **App algorithms.** A fuzzy comprehensive evaluation (factor set, comment set, weight vector, membership matrix, B = A∘R) combines objective scores with subjective factors such as surrounding facilities (pp. 24–25). The recommender combines item-based collaborative filtering (similarity penalized for item popularity, K ≈ 20 nearest items, normalized similarities for diversity) and tag-based recommendation (log-popularity penalties, cosine similarity between tags to handle sparsity, tag cleaning rules) (pp. 28–32). ItemCF is chosen because the number of coasters is small and stable, it reaches long-tail items, and it can start recommending from a user's first action (pp. 28–29).

## Data
COMAP data only, for the model; online lists for comparison. Missing data are handled in two tiers (mean vs neural network) by how much is missing (p. 8). The code appendix (pp. 39–45) shows the BP network trained on normalized year, height, speed, length and inversions, with separate wood/steel files. Tools are listed explicitly: Python for calculation, Excel and Python for graphs, Word, Xmind Zen (p. 36).

## Assumptions
A short bulleted list of three general assumptions (p. 7): the data are reliable; design parameters are reasonable (no design defects); and rider psychology about height or rollover is ignored, so every manoeuvre is within tolerance. Further working assumptions appear where needed, e.g., newer coasters reflect more advanced technology (p. 7) and the typical user is a young thrill-seeker (p. 23).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Imputation check:** iteration-error curve and train/test overlay for the drop network (pp. 11–12).
- **Sensitivity:** each indicator is scaled by (1 + r), with r stepped by ±0.01, until the ranking first changes; thresholds are reported per indicator (Table 5, p. 19), and the original and perturbed score curves are compared (Fig. 10). They conclude the ranking "will not overly affect the overall ranking because of an indicator" (p. 19).
- **External comparison:** a positional table against a University of Buffalo student ranking and ranker.com (Table 6, p. 20); mean characteristics of each Top 10 (Table 7) and radar charts (Fig. 11, p. 21); a world map of coaster counts (Fig. 12). Explanations offered: US-based rankers, older lists keeping once-advanced coasters, and the visual impact of wooden structures (pp. 21–22).
- **Recommender evaluation design:** A/B testing and recall, precision, coverage and diversity are defined (pp. 27–28), though not computed.

## What makes it stand out
- **Explaining disagreement with profiles, not anecdotes** (pp. 20–21): comparing the mean height, age, inversions and so on of each Top 10 shows *what kind* of coaster each list favours, for example that their list contains no inverting coasters while the online ones do.
- **Bias analysis of the comparison lists themselves** (pp. 21–22): the geographic and age arguments turn "our list differs" into a substantive point about subjective rankings.
- **Explicit indicator screening** (pp. 12–15): a candidate list, a rejection table with reasons, and a redundancy screen make the indicator choice auditable.
- **Threshold-style sensitivity** (pp. 18–19): reporting how far each indicator can move before the ranking changes is easier to interpret than an arbitrary ±10% run.
- **A product-grade app design** (pp. 22–35): user portrait, pain points, algorithm choice justified by the domain (few, long-lived items; infrequent use), evaluation metrics, prototype screens and a concrete user story (Allen in Valencia, California, p. 34).
- **Strong visual identity**: a full-page app poster (p. 3) and a designed news release with photo and styled Top 10 table (p. 2).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** a title, a one-paragraph overview, then one paragraph per problem ("In problem one… In problem 2… In problem 3…") naming methods and one number (3% imputation error) (p. 1); it ends with a keyword line.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, literature review, our work) → Variables → Assumptions → Data preprocessing → Comprehensive evaluation model → Ranking interpretation (results, sensitivity, comparison, conclusion) → App demand analysis → Filtering system → Recommendation system → User experience → Advantages/disadvantages → References, tools → Appendix (data, rankings, PCA, code).
- **Devices:** "Our Work" bullet roadmap (p. 6); reason table for rejected indicators; correlation heatmap; mind-map of the final indicator system (Fig. 7); bar chart of the Top 10 with scores (Fig. 8); sensitivity flowchart (Fig. 9); radar charts; local notation tables before each algorithm (pp. 28–30); phone-frame mock-ups with captions (p. 33).
- **News release:** dated, with a product headline, a photo, and the Top 10 in a coloured table; sensory opening ("Heartbeat, screaming, trembling, free of gravity…", p. 2); no technical terms.
- **Phrasing:** "Merely the accuracy of prediction is not sufficient for a good RS… a qualified RS should provide information that user's not familiar but likely to try" (p. 27); "The tendency to put the roller coasters with the special figure (highest, longest, etc.) on the top makes this occur." (p. 20).

## Notes for reuse
- Smiler's 14 inversions is a real record value, yet it was deleted as a Z-score outlier and re-imputed (p. 8). Deleting genuine extremes removes exactly the coasters a "best of" ranking should notice, so check outliers against sources before removing them.
- The type multiplier vector [1.00, 1.15, 1.10, 1.10, 1.20] multiplies every final score but has no stated source (p. 13). Also, the screening text rejects length while Fig. 7 keeps it (p. 15), so keep the stated screening result consistent with the indicator set actually used.
