# 2018A-8744: Untitled (Thrill, Discomfort and Comprehensive Index; app "Roller Ranker")

## At a glance
The team argued that a good coaster maximizes thrill but should be penalized for discomfort. They built an additive Thrill Index (height, speed, G-force, inversion frequency, type score) and subtracted a Discomfort Index made of two logistic curves calibrated to published human tolerance thresholds for G-force and inversion frequency (pp. 10–18). All variables were Box-Cox transformed and mapped to percentiles in [0, 1] (pp. 9–10), and the type of coaster was scored with a Borda count over online enthusiast polls (pp. 12–14). Default weights for the seven terms came from an AHP analysis of an online pairwise-preference survey (pp. 25–27). The resulting Top 10 is led by Steel Dragon 2000, Fury 325 and Millennium Force (p. 27). The app "Roller Ranker" offers three tiers (default, basic thrill-vs-comfort slider, advanced AHP), worked through with a fictional user, Katie (pp. 18–25).

## How they read the problem
- The restatement stresses that the model must use "the most important factors that contribute to the experience of riding roller coasters, and these should all be present in the given dataset" (p. 4).
- The central interpretive move is to treat "best" as net experience: "A roller coaster may be extremely thrilling to ride, but if people experience discomfort on the trip, the overall experience of the ride can be seriously degraded" (p. 14). This stops the ranking from simply rewarding the most extreme specifications.
- Correlated variables are not double-counted: drop is excluded in favour of height (r = 0.95; drop is 53% missing) (pp. 10–11), and inversion count is replaced by inversion *frequency* (inversions per second of ride) to separate compact, intense layouts from long ones (p. 12).
- They identified three user groups (beginners, experienced riders, enthusiasts) and matched an app tier to each (Table 8.1, p. 19).

## Models and methods
- **Correlation and imputation.** A Pearson heatmap (Fig. 3.1, p. 7) identifies pairs such as height–drop (0.95) and speed–drop (0.97). Height, speed and length (<2% missing) get mean imputation; drop, duration, G-force and vertical angle (25–73% missing, Table 3.3, p. 8) get least-squares regression imputation from their most correlated partner (height for drop, length for duration and G-force, G-force for vertical angle) (pp. 8–9).
- **Standardization.** Because the distributions are skewed (Fig. 4.1), each variable is Box-Cox transformed (λ values reported, Fig. 4.2, p. 9) and then mapped through the normal CDF to a percentile in [0, 1] (pp. 9–10). Rationale: values "will have the same range," which lets indices be added.
- **Thrill Index** TI = h_max + s_max + g_max + ω + b_T, built one term at a time, with a physical or experiential justification for each (weightlessness, speed, vertical acceleration, inversions, design type) (pp. 10–13).
- **Type score by extended Borda count.** 46 forum rankings of coaster types (Reddit, CoasterForce) are tallied with 7…1 points and scaled by the winner's 264 points: Inverted 1, Flying 0.704, Wing 0.674, Sit Down 0.591, Floorless/Suspended 0.587, Stand 0.24 (Table 5.1, p. 14). Rationale: "Arbitrary numbering is possible, but it would reduce the objectivity of our index" (p. 13).
- **Discomfort Index** DI = 1/(1+e^{45(0.88−g)}) + 1/(1+e^{30(0.85−ω)}). The G-force curve is pinned at 50% discomfort at 5G and about 99% at 6G after transforming those thresholds into the standardized scale; the inversion curve uses a reported 0.133 inversions per second dizziness threshold (pp. 14–16), visualized as a 3D surface (Fig. 6.3, p. 17).
- **Comprehensive Index** ℂ = TI − DI (Eq. 7.3, p. 18), designed to "penalize roller coasters that either gives excess emphasis to the experience of thrill or neglects the discomfort of the rider."
- **AHP layer.** Slider values 1–9 are converted to Saaty-scale entries (x′ = 2x − 9, or 1/(11 − 2x)), a 7×7 reciprocal matrix is built, consistency is checked (λ_max, CI, RI = 1.32, CR < 0.1), and weights are column-normalized row means (pp. 21–25). The same procedure applied to the survey matrix gives the default weights (pp. 25–27).

## Data
COMAP data plus three kinds of external input: forum polls for type popularity (p. 13), literature values for G tolerance and rotation-induced dizziness (refs. 6–7, p. 30), and the team's own online survey, whose 21 question-by-question response distributions are printed in Appendix D (62 responses each, pp. 39–49). Descriptive statistics (Table 3.2, p. 6) and missing-value percentages (Table 3.3, p. 8) are reported before modelling.

## Assumptions
Five bulleted assumption–justification pairs (pp. 4–5), including that imputed data approximate real data ("without the full data, valid comparison… cannot be made"), that people may feel discomfort after a ride, and that discomfort starts near 5G and 0.133 inversions per second, "selected based on existing studies which can be found in the references." Variables, matrices, vectors and indices each get a typographic convention (p. 5).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- Comparison with ranker.com (described as a large voluntary community, possibly affected by undercoverage bias) and a single enthusiast's blog (described as expert but very subjective), chosen because "their ranking concept differs from our model's greatly" (p. 28). Common coasters are colour-matched across columns (Table 10.2, p. 28).
- AHP consistency checking is demonstrated on a consistent and a slightly inconsistent matrix (CR = 0.0014) (pp. 23–24).
- No sensitivity analysis of the weights or logistic thresholds is reported. The Evaluation section discusses limitations instead, including Borda count's failure of the independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives criterion (Arrow's theorem) and the study-derived discomfort curves (p. 29).

## What makes it stand out
- **Benefit-minus-cost framing** (pp. 14–18): a nonlinear penalty term gives the ranking a defensible reason not to reward ever-larger G-forces, a point most rankings miss.
- **Thresholds anchored in literature** (pp. 14–16): the logistic curves are calibrated to named tolerance levels instead of arbitrary steepness.
- **Engineered features** (p. 12): inversion frequency is a simple derived variable that carries more meaning than a raw count.
- **Objective treatment of a categorical variable** (pp. 13–14): Borda aggregation of real polls, with its voting-theory properties and limits discussed.
- **Careful scaling** (pp. 9–10): Box-Cox plus percentile mapping handles skew and puts all terms on a common [0, 1] scale.
- **Tiered app tied to user segments** (pp. 19–25): each tier uses a progressively richer version of the same formula, and the AHP consistency check is shown with numbers.

(p. 1); "Certainly, it is undesired that our ranking recommends roller coasters that simply have the highest drop, fastest speed, or the greatest number of inversions, while people feel horrible after riding it" (p. 17).

## Notes for reuse
- The printed weights in Eq. 9.5 (p. 27) differ from the weights in the code, which also adds Length to the Thrill Index (pp. 33–34), so the reported Top 10 cannot be traced to the stated formula. In the same spirit, the basic-tier formula ℂ = α·DI − β·TI (p. 20) reverses the signs of ℂ = TI − DI. Keep text, formulas and code in agreement.
- G-force is 72.67% missing and is regression-imputed from length (r = −0.61) (p. 8), yet it drives both a thrill term and the steep discomfort penalty. With no sensitivity test on the logistic thresholds or weights, it is unknown how much of the ranking depends on imputed G-forces.
