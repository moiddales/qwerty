# 2020A-11135: Multiple Criteria-Based Job Recommendation Model

## At a glance
The team builds a four-step recommender:
1. Filter out infeasible jobs with a decision tree (prerequisites, work-from-home or not).
2. Turn each job feature into a 0–1 "input index" tailored to the student.
3. Weight the indices with AHP, including a mechanism that tells the user *which* comparison is inconsistent.
4. Divide the AHP benefit score by normalized weekly hours in a benefit/cost step, giving a final job index.

The model has two applications. **A1** recommends job *categories* from a pool of 44 using national data. **A2** ranks *specific* local postings the student enters (e.g. CVS cashier vs math tutor). The key indexing idea is that wage and flexibility are "more is better", but social interaction and physical activity have a personal optimum. The student gives a preferred range, which is turned into a normal curve, so jobs nearest the centre score highest. Ten colourful personas are tested in both applications. The model is compared with a simple lexicographic baseline, and AHP sensitivity curves find the weight at which the top job changes.

## How they read the problem
- They identify conflicting factors as the core difficulty: "some may want to earn more money but still have concerns with the required physicality of the job" (p. 3).
- **Two levels of decision.** A student may first want to know what *kind* of job suits them, and then which actual listing to take. Some features belong to job categories (social interaction, physical activity) and others to specific employers (work culture, interest). This split produces the two applications (pp. 3, 5).
- **Recreation time and wellbeing as cost.** Hours worked are treated as the cost of a job, and money costs are folded into *net* weekly wage, i.e. wage minus transport and work expenses (pp. 5, 8–9). Assumption 5 states that, all else equal, students prefer fewer hours and shorter commutes (p. 4).
- **Context.** Assumption 1 is that a COVID-19 vaccine will be distributed by summer 2021, so in-person jobs are in scope. It is justified by the Pfizer/Moderna announcements (p. 3). Assumption 4 is that weekly hours stay within child labor limits (p. 4).
- **Non-monotone preferences.** Assumption 6 says preference for social interaction and physical activity is bell-shaped. It is justified by the Yerkes–Dodson arousal–performance curve (p. 4).

## Models and methods
- **Decision tree filter.** Binary questions (work from home? has skill X?) remove jobs before scoring (Figure 4, p. 9). In A1, jobs below the student's minimum acceptable wage or flexibility are also removed (pp. 10–11).
- **Wage and flexibility indices (A1).** After the wage cutoff the distribution is skewed (histogram, p. 10), so a Box-Cox transformation makes it approximately Gaussian. Z-scores are then min–max scaled. Flexibility uses a defined 1–9 rubric (fixed hours … no set schedule) and the same transformation (p. 11).
- **Preference-range index.** The student gives a preferred range on a 1–9 rubric (e.g. social interaction 1 = entirely individual work, 9 = heavy customer interaction). μ is the range midpoint and σ = range/(2·2.576) ≈ 0.194·range, so the range covers 99% of the curve. The index is (max|z| − |z_i|)/(max|z| − min|z|), so it is highest at the student's ideal level (pp. 11–12). Rationale: asking for a range rather than one value captures "the student's tolerance of deviations from his/her optimal amount" (p. 11).
- **Flexibility formula (A2).** f = (1 + share of hours out of office)(1 + shifts/3)(1 + (1 − workdays/5)). Each term is 1 for a standard 9–5 job and at most 2. The form is multiplicative "to reduce the comparative value of super extreme jobs": a job that is well-rounded beats one that excels in a single factor (pp. 13–14).
- **Location (A2)** is reversed min–max on commute time (p. 14). **Culture and interest (A2)** have no available data, so they come from the student's own pairwise AHP comparison of the candidate jobs (p. 14).
- **AHP.** 1–9 scale; weights from column-normalization and row averages; CI/CR < 10% (pp. 7–8). For inconsistent input, the expected comparison is p_i/p_j. The deviation ε_ij = a_ij·p_j/p_i identifies the single most inconsistent comparison, which the user is asked to re-enter (p. 8). Rationale: "people are likely to be cardinally inconsistent because they cannot estimate precisely measurement values" (p. 6).
- **Benefit/cost.** Hours are normalized by the maximum, and J_i = ω_i / h̄_i (pp. 8–9).

## Data
- A1 uses a pool of 44 high-school-accessible job categories with national-average data. Wages are median entry-level hourly wages "accounting for bonuses like tips for service workers" (pp. 9–10, 16). The reference list includes Indeed, Zippia and Monster job lists.
- Flexibility, social interaction and physical activity are rated by the team on explicit 1–9 rubrics with verbal anchors (p. 11).
- A2 uses five constructed postings (CVS cashier, math tutor, babysitter, McDonald's prep cook, Walmart janitor) with hours out of office, total hours, shifts and workdays. Net weekly wage and commute are set per persona, because they depend on where the person lives (p. 19).

## Assumptions
There are six numbered assumptions, each with a labelled justification (pp. 3–4). Notable examples:
- **Entry-level wages:** "Most high schoolers do not have related work experience. Therefore they will be considered as entry-level workers" (p. 3).
- **Wage is the only benefit:** no stock, health or retirement benefits, because summer jobs are too short (p. 3).
- **Bell-shaped preference** for social interaction and physical activity, grounded in a cited psychology result (p. 4).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Persona plausibility.** Each of the ten results is discussed against the biography. The paper also points out when inputs are too strict: MacKenzie and Olivia are left with only tutor, and the authors "recommend that she be less demanding in her preferences" (p. 17). They note surprising but explainable outcomes, such as antisocial Jacob getting math tutor because pay, flexibility and low hours "overrun that premise" (p. 20).
- **Baseline comparison.** A lexicographic model sorts by the most important factor and breaks ties with the next. For Ava, its top 7 overlaps the AHP model's top 7 in only one job (swim instructor). The authors argue that the AHP model captures *how much* one factor is preferred, while the baseline captures only the order (pp. 18–19).
- **AHP sensitivity.** Following Goepel's method, one weight at a time is varied for Arthur while the ratios of the other weights are held fixed, and final scores are plotted against that weight. Tutor stays on top for any wage, flexibility or physical-activity weight. For social interaction, the top job changes to lawn care only if the weight rises to 0.498, which they call the "Absolute-Top factor" (pp. 21–22).

## What makes it stand out
- **Correct treatment of "ideal-point" criteria.** Most entries treat every criterion as maximize or minimize. Here the student's preferred range becomes a Gaussian ideal, which suits factors like social interaction and physical activity where more is not always better (pp. 11–12, 22–23).
- **Category vs specific-job applications** mirror a real search process, and each uses only the features that make sense at that level (pp. 5, 9–15).
- **Actionable inconsistency handling.** Instead of rejecting an inconsistent matrix, the model tells the user which comparison to reconsider (p. 8).
- **Baseline comparison.** Showing that a naive method gives very different answers justifies the added complexity with evidence (pp. 18–19).
- **Sensitivity framed for the user.** The "Absolute-Top factor" asks which weight change would alter the job the student actually sees first (p. 21). The app then offers the same idea interactively through weight sliders (p. 24).
- **Every constant explained.** The flexibility formula's constants (1, 3 shifts, 5 workdays) are each justified, so that a standard 9–5 job scores 1 per term (pp. 13–14).
- **Memorable personas.** Vivid biographies make the qualitative check of results engaging and easy to follow (pp. 15–16).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** context with a statistic ("as many as 34.6% of high schoolers pursue job opportunities") → nine features → four-step pipeline with each method named → two applications and ten personas → baseline comparison result → claimed strengths (p. 1). It is dense with method names and qualitative about results.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction → Assumptions & factors (inputs) → Model (overview; AHP; inconsistency; cost–benefit; A1; A2) → Results for 10 people (bios, preferences, A1 results, baseline comparison, A2 results) → Model analysis (sensitivity, strengths, weaknesses) → App design → Conclusion → References.
- **Devices:** an inputs/outputs diagram (p. 4); a four-box model-structure flowchart with a "reevaluate inconsistent inputs" loop (p. 6); a decision-tree figure (p. 9); before/after histograms for Box-Cox (p. 10); rubric tables for the 1–9 scales (p. 11); per-person result tables with index values in parentheses (pp. 17, 20); line plots of score vs weight with the current weight marked (pp. 21–22); a side-by-side baseline ranking table (p. 18).
- **App:** justified by the statistic that 95% of teens have smartphones, and because "since our model needs user input, some of the other methods, like newspapers, are just not practical" (p. 23). Design iteration is mentioned: "We originally used a table but we realized that it was going to be hard to read, so we changed it to be a graph" (p. 24).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "We asked for the range as opposed to the singular optimal level in order to account for the student's tolerance of deviations from his/her optimal amount." (p. 11)
  - "In other words, a job that is well-rounded with regards to the flexibility factors will be rated higher than a job that excels in one factor but is poor in the others." (p. 13)
  - "Our model takes into consideration the absolute differences in preferences while this simple model only takes into consideration the relative differences." (p. 19)

## Notes for reuse
- The final index divides by normalized weekly hours for every student, so shorter-hour jobs are always favoured. This follows from Assumption 5 (p. 4), but a student who *wants* more hours, for example to earn more, is not represented. If you copy the benefit/cost step, make hours a user preference rather than a fixed cost.
