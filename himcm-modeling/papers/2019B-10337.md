# 2019B-10337: Modeling the impacts of an SSPET ban

## At a glance
Team 10337 models a single-serving PET (SSPET) water-bottle ban through changes in beverage volumes. Their key device is a locational base rate: national beverage shares are adjusted with city census and airport survey inputs so the model reflects where people actually drink. For each beverage, they calculate environmental, economic and health impacts per liter, then multiply by the change in liters after the ban. Tap water, large bottles, soda, coffee/tea and alcohol are included, making substitution explicit. The same accounting is applied to Concord, San Francisco and San Francisco Airport, with recommended public fountains and reusable-bottle support (pp. 2–20).

## How they read the problem
The paper separates four tasks: estimate pre-ban consumption, estimate post-ban substitution, convert substitutions to impacts, and recommend infrastructure. It distinguishes city and airport locations because an airport has captive travelers, security constraints and a different beverage mix (pp. 2–4, 16–18). Instead of claiming a single universal percentage, the team supplies survey questions for residents and airport passengers to measure local fluid shares (pp. 6–9). This makes the model operational: a city can replace assumed rates with survey results.

## Models and methods
The baseline model starts with a national beverage consumption vector. Census and survey variables modify it into a locational base rate: for example, the population, age or setting can change shares of tap, large-bottle water, soda, coffee/tea and alcohol. The paper gives a questionnaire that asks respondents to allocate their fluid intake among categories and, for airports, to report how they would obtain water after a ban (pp. 6–9).

For each beverage, the team computes energy, waste and financial effects per liter. The energy appendix adds water collection/treatment, PET production and transport; soda includes additional sugar-production energy (pp. 21–24). Waste calculations account for bottle mass and larger containers. The main model stores three components in an impact vector—environmental, economic and health—then multiplies the vector by the volume change after the ban. Summing across beverages yields a Ban Overall Impact (pp. 10–19).

Airport revenue includes reusable bottles purchased and other changes in sales. The airport model sets SSPET water to zero in the post-ban mix and reallocates volume to alternatives, while acknowledging that travelers may have less room or time for large containers (pp. 16–18).

## Data
Inputs come from census data, national fluid-intake studies, beverage prices, airport passenger counts and lifecycle energy/waste sources. The paper’s tables derive variables from city census data and list energy requirements for water treatment and transport (pp. 8–13, 21–24). Survey forms are presented as a practical data-collection plan rather than claimed observations (pp. 6–9).

## Assumptions
The team assumes product impacts have negligible effect on an individual’s immediate purchase decision, fluid volume is conserved, and trash/recycling collection costs remain unchanged (pp. 4–5). It assumes 16 waking “fluid drinking” hours, fixed average beverage prices and that SSPET bottles have a representative 0.5-L size (pp. 5–6, 13). In airports, large bottles are considered inconvenient to carry; local survey data can replace that assumption (p. 17).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The model compares national and locational base rates and presents sample calculations for the U.S., Concord, San Francisco and the airport (pp. 11–19). A UC Berkeley beverage-tax result is used to adjust substitution toward water and away from sugar-sweetened drinks (p. 14). Results are shown as vectors and tables, so the contribution of each beverage can be inspected. The limitations section identifies omitted alternatives, fixed fluid volumes and uncertain data rather than hiding them (p. 22).

## What makes it stand out
- Locational base rates make data collection part of the model and prevent national averages from being treated as local truth.
- The impact-vector representation keeps environment, economy and health separate until the final aggregation.
- Lifecycle energy and waste include production and transport, not only disposal.
- City and airport cases use the same structure but different behavior and capacity assumptions.
- Survey forms, derivation tables and appendices make the framework implementable by a municipality.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The paper introduces variables and assumptions before calculation, then presents each model part with tables and explanatory prose (pp. 2–10). The newspaper article on p. 20 turns an abstract ban into a public-health and waste story. Impact-vector tables (pp. 15–19) make a complicated score auditable, while the energy appendix gives enough detail to reproduce the major environmental terms (pp. 21–24). A dedicated limitations section tells the reader where local measurement is needed.

## Notes for reuse
- Survey the target location before applying national beverage shares; airport passengers and city residents may substitute differently (pp. 6–9).
- Keep units consistent when combining per-liter impacts with volume changes; lifecycle terms should be documented in an appendix (pp. 21–24).
