# 2015B-5967: A Flexible Crime Rating

## At a glance
The paper builds a single crime rating that combines counts, people affected, severity, arrest efficiency, residential proximity and spatial distribution. My City’s score is benchmarked against Minneapolis, Detroit, Seattle and Los Angeles, with explicit warnings when comparison data are theoretical. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They prioritize a score usable for both My City and cities with less detailed data. The base model uses only broadly available fields; optional factors improve the local analysis without making cross-city comparisons impossible. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Crime counts are normalized by population and multiplied by severity and victimization weights. An arrest/proximity adjustment creates a flexible extended score. Benchmark cities are placed on the same scale where data permit; missing fields are reported rather than silently imputed (pp. 4–25).

## Data
The paper uses My City spreadsheet, FBI/municipal comparisons and severity references. The primary dataset is the two-week My City police report. Teams group offense descriptions and combine it with survey fear weights, crime-cost references, FBI comparison distributions, sentencing data or published city rankings. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes the two-week reports are a usable sample, crime categories can be grouped consistently, and severity can be represented by fear, cost, sentence or a calibrated response. Population or comparison-city denominators are treated as exposure proxies. Each paper flags underreporting and external-data limits (pp. 4–12). Its central choice is crime rating composite, supported by severity and victimization weights; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The team compares scores under the base and extended models and tests alternative reference cities (pp. 24–31). It reports limitations when external figures are projections and checks that the ordering is qualitatively plausible.

## What makes it stand out
The distinction between a portable base score and a richer local score is valuable. The paper explains why a lower score means safer and gives concrete benchmark anchors for a mayor.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
The mayoral letter appears early, then the report defines variables, computes the score and documents benchmarks. Tables and a final model review keep the composite interpretable (pp. 1–3, 20–33).

## Notes for reuse
- Benchmark scores are only as good as the external data. An imitator should publish the weight table and avoid comparing a detailed local score to a sparse city score without labeling the difference.
