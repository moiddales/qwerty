# 2019B-9548: The Bottle Outbreak

## At a glance
The team treats the ban as a shock to the beverage market and argues that every other impact follows from how the market reorganizes. Their core model borrows the competitive Lotka-Volterra equations from ecology: drink categories are "species" competing for a fixed pool of consumers. They modify the equations so no drink category ever disappears entirely, then extend them from two to three to five categories. The resulting before/after market shares feed four groups of impact formulas: environmental, social, government budget and individual. Applied to Concord, San Francisco and SFO airport, the model shows lower plastic pollution and lower abatement costs, set against higher fountain-installation costs and lower tax revenue. Sugar intake rises most in San Francisco. Their conclusion is that the ban is "a doable ban" (p. 1), with different trade-offs in each setting.

## How they read the problem
- **The market is the root cause.** The summary and restatement say the ban "directly impacts" the drink market, and that the market shift is "the fundamental cause" of environmental, social, governmental and individual changes (pp. 1, 5). This gives the paper a two-stage pipeline: first market dynamics, then the impacts.
- **Equilibrium framing.** The task becomes predicting "the change in a previously balanced market after a major competitor exits it and determine its final state of equilibrium" (p. 6). The paper then compares the two stable states.
- **One model for all settings.** Towns, cities and airports differ only in parameter values: "By changing the values of variables, the model can simulate airports, towns and cities" (p. 6).
- **Four impact branches.** Impacts are grouped as environment (plastic pollution, energy), social (tax, jobs), government budget (pollution abatement, tap-water facilities) and individual (cost, health/sugar, emergency supplies) (pp. 10–15). The IBWA concerns (more sugar, emergency need for bottled water) are each given their own term.
- **Scoping by data availability.** The team explicitly drops job losses (bottling is highly automated, so the impact is negligible) and individual and emergency costs (no profit-rate or cost data) from the numerical applications. They say the formulas stand ready "if the data is given to us" (p. 18).

## Models and methods
**Competitive Lotka-Volterra market model (pp. 7–11).** Drink categories are treated as species and their sales shares as populations. The stated rationale is the analogy: companies compete for a fixed set of consumers, a "limited resource" (p. 7). The paper then modifies the model in stages:
1. **Two competitors** with the standard equations. They explain the stability points and that coexistence requires σ1, σ2 < 1 (p. 8).
2. **Lower bound.** They add a factor (x − L) so that a category's share never drops below a minimum L. Their justification is that beverages "cannot be entirely 'wiped out' over a short period of time" (pp. 1, 8).
3. **Three competitors.** The categories are PET bottled water, packaged drinks and "others" (mainly tap water), with the constraint x1 + x2 + x3 = 1 (pp. 8–9). The ban is represented by setting bottled water's growth rate to −0.1 so that its share declines smoothly. A shorter delay between announcement and enforcement brings the new equilibrium sooner (Figs. 2–4, p. 9).
4. **Five competitors** (p. 10): bottled water under 1 L, bottled water of 1 L or more, other beverages, glass-bottled water and tap water. The model is integrated numerically ("make a calculation every small period of time", p. 10). The run has a "first stable period", then a "turning period" after the ban sets r1 < 0, then a "second stable period" once x1 reaches zero and is dropped from the system (p. 11, Fig. 5).

The σ coefficients and growth rates are described as "deducted" (estimated by the team). In reality they "can be obtained by surveys" (p. 9). No fitting to data is reported.

**Impact equations (pp. 11–15).** Each impact is a short formula driven by the change in sales of each drink category:
- plastic mass not recycled: M = Σ(1 − R)·p_i·m_i
- carbon footprint: (p_i·(x_i − R·E_i))/k
- tax change: R = Σ p_i·q_i − p0·m
- job loss: N = p0/w
- abatement budget: W = Σ Δx_i·s_i, later scaled by a location factor a
- fountain installation cost: ⌈Δx3/β⌉·Price
- extra individual cost: ΔC1 = n·p·(C′ − C0)
- sugar burden: Q = max(γ·Σ Δx_i·(l_i − S), 0)
- emergency-supply cost, including a new-production-line term q

## Data
Background statistics (about $100 per person per year spent on bottled water, a 19% beverage-market share, 67.3% single-serve, up to 70% not recycled) frame the introduction (p. 5). Model inputs are mostly assumed, and the text says so openly:
- Concord's initial market mix is "assumed" to be 6:2:2, with total need 12,000 and 95% recycling, because "the government didn't public the details" (p. 19).
- Per-capita bottle consumption is set at 0.1 per day (Concord) and 0.15 per day (SF), with 500 mL bottles (p. 16).
- Location factors are chosen by reasoning about local conditions: a = 1 / 0.5 / 0.1 and γ = 1 / 0.1 / 0.5 for Concord / SF / SFO (pp. 17–18).
- The article itself tells readers that the researchers "have to make proper assumptions according to reality" because the necessary data were not available (p. 4).

## Assumptions
A notation table (Table 1, p. 6) is followed by four assumption–justification pairs (pp. 6–7). Representative examples:
- **Total demand is constant in the short run.** Water is a daily necessity and "a perfectly inelastic good" (p. 6). This supports the share constraint Σx_i = 1.
- **Delay between declaration and execution.** Markets need time to adapt. This motivates the smooth decline and the "turning period" (p. 7).
- **Linear rather than exponential growth.** Sales under competition do not grow exponentially (p. 7).
- **Few categories.** Three (later five) categories keep the calculation manageable; more can be added "by inputting a different combination of data" (p. 7).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
There is no formal validation or sensitivity analysis. Variation appears as a scenario: the shortened-delay run (Fig. 4, p. 9) shows equilibrium arriving faster. Three location runs are compared side by side (Tables 2–4). The Strengths and Weaknesses section admits that coefficients may not be "the most fitting ones" without real data, and that factors such as advertising lie outside the competition analogy (pp. 22–23).

## What makes it stand out
- **A unifying causal mechanism.** All impacts come from one modeled quantity, the change in market shares. This makes the many impact formulas consistent and turns the city-versus-airport comparison into a change of parameters (pp. 5–6, 10–15).
- **A cross-domain analogy that is adapted rather than copied.** The team explains why plain Lotka-Volterra is unrealistic (competitive exclusion, exponential growth) and modifies it accordingly (lower bound L, linear growth assumption) (pp. 7–8). This shows judgment about the borrowed model.
- **Building up model complexity in stages.** Moving from 2 to 3 to 5 competitors lets readers follow the mechanics before the full system (pp. 7–11).
- **A clear dynamic story of the ban.** The first stable period, turning period and second stable period form a vocabulary that explains a time-series plot to non-specialists (pp. 10–11, Fig. 5).
- **Responding directly to the opponents.** Sugar intake and emergency supplies get their own equations, so the IBWA's "unintended consequences" are tested rather than ignored (pp. 14–15).
- **Location-specific recommendations tied to model outputs.** For example, Concord's fountain costs lead to a proposed small fountain fee and beverage tax, and SF's sugar spike leads to anti-sugar measures (pp. 20–21).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** Seven short paragraphs (p. 1): controversy → market as root cause → model progression (2/3/5 competitors, lower bounds, linear growth) → four impact branches → city findings → airport contrast (about 70% tap/glass share) → verdict. It is mainly about method and has few numbers.
- **Paper structure:** Summary → Contents → Newspaper Article → Introduction (background, restatement) → Assumptions (variables, assumptions/justifications) → Competition Model → Four Impacts → Model Application (city, airport) → Analysis and Suggestions (Concord, SF, SFO) → Strengths and Weaknesses → Bibliography.
- **Devices:** a notation table; three side-by-side market-share plots (before, after, shortened delay); for each location a pair of figures (market curve plus a horizontal "benefits and harms" percent-change bar chart) and a before/after table of six impact items (pp. 19–21). The article reuses the same six-impact percent-change chart for all three sites.
- **Article:** The headline is a question ("Would The Bottled Water Ban Backfire?", p. 4), with a one-sentence standfirst, short paragraphs aimed at different stakeholders (businesses, environmentalists) and a frank note about data limits. It is laid out in a boxed newspaper style.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "The dramatic shift in the market is the fundamental cause for changes at environmental, social, governmental and individual levels." (p. 1)
  - "In the actual market, a given kind of beverage cannot go "out of business" in a short period of time even if σ > 1." (p. 8)
  - "We dub it the "first stable period"." (p. 10)

## Notes for reuse
- The impact results in Tables 2–4 rest on assumed inputs: the initial 6:2:2 market mix, growth rate −0.1, and location factors a and γ chosen by argument rather than from data (pp. 9, 17–19). Use the numbers as an illustration of the pipeline, not as estimates.
- The town-size inputs differ from the problem statement: Concord's population is given as about 130,000 (p. 16), against roughly 19,000 in the official statement, and the summary describes the ban threshold as 500 mL (p. 1), against 1 L in the bylaw. Check these against the statement before reusing any per-capita figures.
