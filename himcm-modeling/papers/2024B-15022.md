# 2024B-15022: Untitled (Multi-factor Dynamic Prediction Model for HPC Carbon Emissions)

## At a glance
The team estimated global HPC energy indirectly, as cryptocurrency-mining energy plus the hyperscale share of data-centre energy. They then fitted a logarithmic link between that energy and global computing power, with a utilization factor. Carbon emissions come from a country-by-country energy-mix calculation for 20 HPC-hosting countries. The 2010–2023 series was condensed into one fitted time function, the "Multi-factor Dynamic Prediction Model" (MFD-HPC), with an HPC growth term, a renewable-share term and an energy-competition factor θ. This function projects 2,342,483,383.33 tCO2 in 2030 (5.21% of global emissions) (p. 15–16). The model is then extended with water use and e-waste, and four recommendations are written into it as modified terms and regulatory constraints (p. 20–23).

## How they read the problem
- **Defining "HPC energy" when no direct data exist.** Assumption 1 says HPC energy is data-centre energy for HPC tasks plus crypto mining. AI is not added separately because it runs on data-centre infrastructure (p. 4). Assumption 2 counts the "hyper-scale" data-centre category as HPC (p. 4). This turns an unmeasurable quantity into published IEA and CCAF series (Eq. 1, p. 5).
- **Scoping geography.** Twenty countries (US, China, Japan, Germany, … Finland) stand in for the global total. Each facility is assumed to use its host country's energy mix (Assumptions 3–4, p. 4).
- **Simplifying the mix to one driver.** Each mix is fossil + renewable + nuclear (Assumption 5). Because the nuclear share was "relatively stable", the time model tracks only the renewable share ρ (Assumption 7, p. 4; p. 11).
- **Treating "demand from other sectors" as a parameter.** Competition from other industries becomes a multiplicative factor θ, set to 1 from a reference on current energy surplus (p. 11).
- The restatement turns each requirement into an objective paragraph (p. 3–4). Requirements 2 and 3 share one section because the same function answers both (p. 2).

## Models and methods
- **Top-down energy decomposition (Eq. 1).** E_HPC = E_Crypto + k·E_DC, where k is the hyperscale share of data-centre energy taken from IEA data (Figs. 1–3, p. 5–6). The paper states the rationale: global HPC energy is "difficult" to quantify directly (p. 5).
- **Energy vs computing power (Eqs. 2–3).** The functional form E = a·ln(u·P) + b comes from a cited reference on energy-efficient HPC [4] (p. 6–7). Utilization u enters inside the logarithm. It is set to 60% before 2018 and 50% after, from [5], with the argument that rapid post-2018 growth created "computing power saturation" (p. 7). Two piecewise fits are shown with a transition segment (Fig. 5, p. 7). This is how the team handles the full-capacity versus average-utilization part of the requirement. The paper does not report a separate full-capacity energy figure; full load is discussed qualitatively (p. 8).
- **Carbon accounting (Eq. 4).** Per-country computing power (Fig. 6, p. 9) is converted to per-country energy (Fig. 7, p. 10) and multiplied by mix-weighted emission factors. The fossil factor is a weighted blend, 0.4×820 + 0.3×650 + 0.3×450 = 658 gCO2/kWh (p. 9). Country mixes come from EIA, e.g. US 83/8/9% fossil/renewable/nuclear in 2010 and 70/21/9% in 2023 (Table 1, p. 9).
- **MFD-HPC time function (Eqs. 5–6).** Each driver is first studied against time. r = 49.46% is fitted from TOP500 performance trends (Fig. 9) and checked against literature. ρ = 0.9192(t − 2010) + 26.45 (%) is a linear fit (Fig. 10). θ = 1 (p. 10–12). The composite function is then curve-fitted to the 2010–2023 emissions series (Fig. 11, p. 14). The resulting coefficients are m = 750293.04, n = 6567.91 and s = 21184010.47 (p. 12). The paper notes that m, n and s have "no specific physical meaning" (p. 12).
- **Environmental-impact transform (Eq. 7).** From reference [11], I = ω1(a ln C + b) + Σ g(ω_i y_i), with ω1 = 1 when only carbon is considered (p. 12; Fig. 12, p. 15). Later extended with water and e-waste terms (p. 20).
- **Policy extension (Eqs. 17–25).** The recommendations change specific terms. Efficiency becomes E′ = η·E. Recycling subtracts δ terms proportional to recycling rates ϕ. Mandatory assessments become upper-bound constraints, so the final model is an index subject to caps (p. 22–23). This step is formula-level only; no numerical run of the modified model is reported.

## Data
- External sources are named for each input: IEA (data-centre energy), CCAF (Bitcoin electricity), TOP500 (computing power, by country), EIA (country mixes), IEA (emission factors), and IEA/UNFCCC/IPCC/BP for global CO2 totals (40.5×10^9 t in 2023, 45×10^9 t expected in 2030) (p. 5–6, 9, 15–16, 25–26).
- Utilization rates (60%/50%) come from one cited study [5] (p. 7). θ = 1 is justified by citing Our World in Data on current energy surplus (p. 11).
- The 20-country scope (Assumption 3) gets around missing data for the rest of the world (p. 4).

## Assumptions
Seven numbered assumptions in prose, most with a one-clause reason (p. 4). Representative examples:
- Assumption 1 excludes a separate AI term because AI "rel[ies] on the infrastructure of data centers", which avoids double counting (p. 4).
- Assumption 4 assigns each facility its host country's mix and openly calls the true facility mix "difficult to quantify" (p. 4).
- Assumption 6 reaches ~100% renewables by 2090 and has renewables replace nuclear, citing nuclear's dependence on rare-mineral extraction (p. 4).
- Assumption 7 drops nuclear from the time dynamics because its share was "relatively stable" (p. 4).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **External check of Requirement 1.** The 2024 prediction (289.21 TWh at an estimated 8,200 PFlop/s) is compared with the IEA World Energy Outlook 2024 range of 240–340 TWh (p. 7).
- **Literature check of the growth rate.** The fitted r = 49.46% "aligns with the range provided in the relevant literature" (p. 10).
- **Analytic sensitivity.** Partial derivatives of Ct with respect to r, θ and ρ give the sign and size of each effect (Eqs. 8–10, p. 13–15).
- **Scenario sensitivity.** Growth-rate shocks of 40% and 60% after 2030 (Fig. 13, p. 13, 16) and renewable shares of 40/60/80% (Fig. 15, p. 17–18).
- Section 5.3 frames this as "controlling for variables" (p. 13). Strengths and weaknesses close the paper (p. 25).

## What makes it stand out
- **A defensible proxy for an unmeasured quantity.** Splitting HPC energy into crypto mining plus a data-centre share links the problem to well-documented IEA and CCAF series (p. 5–6). This is a reusable pattern whenever a sector's energy is not reported directly.
- **External cross-check of the baseline.** The 2024 estimate is placed inside the IEA's published range and the paper says so explicitly (p. 7). That gives the reader one anchor of credibility before the forecasts start.
- **Country-resolved energy mixes.** Emissions are computed country by country with each country's fossil/renewable/nuclear shares (p. 8–10). This answers "accounting for energy mixes" structurally rather than with one global factor, and it supports the later recommendation to site HPC in high-renewable regions such as the Nordic countries (p. 16).
- **Driver-by-driver construction of the forecast function.** Each factor (r, ρ, θ) is studied against time before being combined (p. 10–12). Partial derivatives then make the direction of each effect explicit (p. 13–15).
- **Clean reduction for the 100% renewable case.** Setting ρ = 1 removes the fossil term and leaves Ct = m(1+r)^(t−2010) + s (p. 18). This shows in one line why decarbonizing supply does not stop growth-driven emissions.
- **Recommendations translated into model terms.** Each policy maps to a parameter or constraint: η for efficiency, ϕ for recycling, and C ≤ C_MAX and similar caps for impact assessments (p. 22–23). This closes the loop the problem asks for in 5b.
- **Balanced 100% renewable discussion.** The paper organizes challenges as technical, economic, social/policy and resource-related, including that renewable hardware is itself not "entirely green" (p. 18–19).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** One page in seven paragraphs. Two paragraphs of context (including the UN report's limited treatment of HPC), then one paragraph per requirement in the form "The Nth requirement was … To address this, we …", and a closing line on recommendations. The only headline number is the 2030 projection and its 5.21% share (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, restatement) → Assumptions → Notations → Requirement 1 → Requirements 2 & 3 (model, time function, sensitivity, 2030 prediction) → Requirement 4 (renewables, extension) → Requirement 5 (recommendations, model incorporation, letter) → Strengths and Weaknesses → References.
- **Devices:** Requirement-numbered headings; a notation table (p. 5); paired side-by-side data figures (p. 6); explicit fitted equations printed next to fit plots (p. 7, 12); a small data table excerpting the country mixes (p. 9); a symbol glossary placed directly under the final model (p. 23).
- **Letter:** One page, signed by the team. It explains the method in lay terms (data centres plus "data encryption", fitted to rising computing power), gives the 2030 figure in tons and as a share of global emissions, and includes a year-by-year 2021–2030 emissions table in million tonnes. It closes by tying the request to the 17 SDGs (p. 24).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "This aligns with the IEA’s estimate in the World Energy Outlook 2024[6], which places global HPC energy consumption in the range of 240 to 340 TWh, thereby validating the accuracy and feasibility of the model." (p. 7)
  - "This indicates that increasing the proportion of renewable energy has a clear mitigating effect on carbon emissions, but the growing demand for HPC remains the primary driver of future emissions." (p. 18)
  - "Water usage directly depends on the energy consumption of HPC data centers, as cooling system water requirements are proportional to energy use." (p. 20)

## Notes for reuse
- The 49.46% annual growth rate was fitted to TOP500 computing performance (Fig. 9). It is applied as an exponential directly to emissions (Eq. 5), even though Requirement 1 found energy rising only logarithmically with computing power (Eq. 3). This growth term dominates the 2030 projection (about 2.34 Gt, roughly 15× the 2023 value). The fitted renewable-share coefficient (n = 6567.91 tCO2 per unit of 1 − ρ) is negligible next to it, so the energy-mix term barely moves the forecast. An imitator should apply a growth rate to the quantity it was measured on.
- The 2023 share is reported as 3.51% (160,521,583.33 t of 40.5×10^9 t, p. 15). That arithmetic gives about 0.4%, so the "nearly 2% increase" to 5.21% in 2030 (p. 16) understates the projected rise. Recompute shares before comparing them.
