# 2014B-5325: SIR/SIRD and Decision Tree for Disease Response

## At a glance
Three linked models provide a staged response. SIR gives a first forecast, SIRD adds deaths and vaccine protection, and a decision tree classifies risk and recommends treatment, transport, restriction or observation. Shared inputs allow the models to be updated as evidence arrives. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
The team separates prediction from policy. It first asks what happens without controls, then adds vaccination and mortality, and finally maps quantitative risk thresholds to actions. The added field information changes transmission assumptions and raises the recommended response level. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Difference equations track susceptible, infected, recovered and dead people; vaccine coverage and average efficacy move people out of susceptibility. A decision tree uses individual and community risk indicators to select measures. Scenarios vary vaccine supply and protection (pp. 5–16).

## Data
The paper uses prompt village counts, vaccine efficacy assumptions and disease sources. The input is the village case supplied by the prompt. Teams supplement it with Ebola or disease-transmission references, assumed transition probabilities, GDP/distance data, vaccine efficacy or sentencing-style resource proxies as appropriate. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes a closed or nearly closed village during one modeled run, homogeneous contacts unless age groups are introduced, and fixed or sampled transition probabilities. Latent infection, contagiousness, mortality and intervention effects are explicit states or parameters. The teams state that sparse field data make these assumptions provisional (pp. 5–9). Its central choice is SIR model, supported by SIRD with vaccination; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The three models are compared on identical initial conditions and intervention scenarios (pp. 15–21). Thresholds are shown in tables and the authors discuss strengths and limits of each component (pp. 21–23).

## What makes it stand out
The modular architecture is easy to reuse: forecast, quantify deaths, then choose an action. The decision tree makes scarce-resource recommendations explicit instead of burying them in a single objective.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
Executive summary, model-building subsections, a threshold tree, and a compact response table suit a public-health audience (pp. 1, 5–23).

## Notes for reuse
- Thresholds are policy choices and should be calibrated with public-health experts. The vaccination model assumes an average protection rate and does not model waning immunity or heterogeneous contacts.
