# 2020B-10839: Untitled ("The Stochastic Model")

## At a glance
The team treats the FRPCE problem as choosing a **start year for each of the 48 recovery projects**. Postponing a project lowers its benefit and its feasibility of success. They built a "Stochastic Model" (S. Model) that draws random start years for all projects and throws out any plan whose total time-decayed benefit is below a user-chosen share of the maximum possible benefit. The surviving plans are ranked by the product of a feasibility-adjusted total cost and the standard deviation of yearly spending. The recommended plan runs 34 years, keeps 89% of the maximum benefit, has an adjusted total cost of $107,135,000 and a yearly-cost standard deviation of $267,411 (pp. 1, 11–12, 18). The paper presents the model as a user-friendly tool: one function call produces the three best schedules as an Excel file (pp. 12, 17).

## How they read the problem
- They read "long-term and reliable" funding as a **smooth annual fundraising burden**. The standard deviation of yearly cost becomes an explicit objective, because a steady budget "would minimize the probability of failing to raise sufficient funds for a particular year with a high budget" (p. 4).
- Their four objectives are: fund all 48 projects, minimise total cost, minimise year-to-year fluctuation, and reach a target benefit (pp. 3, 6).
- They map the data columns onto factors (p. 3–4). Taxonomic uniqueness comes from its column. "Threatened level" and "ecological benefit" both come from the Benefit column, by assumption (Assumption 1, p. 5). Feasibility of success comes from its column and decays over time.
- They reframe "minimum fundraising" as an **expected cost that accounts for risk of failure**. Each project's cost is divided by its feasibility of success, which gives an estimated cost that "theoretically ensures the success of the project" (pp. 3, 10).
- Time is "the most important part" of the model, since start dates are the decision variable (p. 3).

## Models and methods
- **Time-dependent benefit.** Threatened level grows exponentially, T_i,t = T_i,0(1+r_t)^(t−1) (p. 7). Feasibility falls in proportion to that growth, f_i = f_i,0 · T_i,0/T_i,s_i (p. 8). Uniqueness grows as u_i = u_i,0(1+r_u)^(s_i−1) (p. 7). A project's score is bt_i = x_i,0 · f_i · (1 − r_b u_i)^(s_i−1), where x_i,0 is the Benefit column, and the plan's benefit is the sum over the 48 projects (p. 8). Exponential forms are chosen "for the convenience of this model" (Assumption 2, p. 6). The decay rates are user parameters with default values and no data source (pp. 7–8).
- **Plan generation.** Each project gets a random start year s_i ~ uniform(lower, upper). The bounds depend on project length, so long projects start early, "to accelerate the calculation" (p. 9). Projects cannot be paused once started (Assumption 3, p. 6).
- **Filtering and scoring.** A plan is kept only if its total benefit is at least exp_pct_b × the maximum benefit. The maximum is the unrealistic "all projects start in year 1" plan, used as a benchmark (p. 10). For each kept plan, they compute the adjusted total cost Σ sch_i,j / f_i and the SD of the *unadjusted* yearly costs. The SD uses real cash because "the original yearly cost is the actual budget for the fundraising process" (pp. 10–11). Plans are ranked by the product of the two.
- **Implementation.** One "process" call runs 100,000 random plans. A "rep" wrapper calls it 500 times and returns the three lowest-score plans (p. 11). The code is written in Python with Numba JIT compilation and exports the schedules to Excel (pp. 19–22). 3-D Plotly scatter plots compare pairs of projects over start year and endangerment coefficient (pp. 8–9).

## Data
They use only the COMAP spreadsheet. The code reads the 48 projects, rounds costs to thousands and replaces missing cells with 0 (p. 19). The authors note that ecological benefit would need "a more abundant data set", so the Benefit column stands in for both threatened level and ecological benefit (pp. 5, 8). The rates r_u, r_b and r_t are described as reflecting "the user's understanding of the imperiled species", and r_t could in principle be estimated from past population data (p. 7). The references are background reading on endangerment and conservation (p. 23).

## Assumptions
Seven assumptions are given as a numbered list, each followed by a "Justification" paragraph (pp. 5–6). Representative examples:
- *Projects cannot be paused once started* (Assumption 3), because a break could undermine the project (p. 6).
- *Benefit and feasibility are locked in when a project starts* (Assumption 4). This is why the adjusted cost uses the start-year feasibility f_i in every year (pp. 6, 10).
- *Success is only known at the end* (Assumption 5), so feasibility cannot be split into yearly components (p. 6).
- *Lower feasibility means higher expected cost* (Assumption 7). This motivates cost/feasibility as the quantity to budget (p. 6).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Sensitivity analysis** (Section 4, pp. 12–16) varies four inputs: the benefit benchmark (82–85%), r_u (0.006–0.024), r_b (1–4) and r_t (0.25–1.00). For each setting they run 10 million random plans and plot a histogram of the score over the plans that pass the filter (p. 13). They explain each change in shape. A stricter benchmark or faster decay leaves fewer feasible plans and a more "discrete" histogram. Higher r_t shifts the score range because feasibility falls and adjusted cost rises (pp. 13–16).
- **Benchmarking against random plans.** The optimised schedule's score is compared with the best scores from 10 million random schedules, and the authors say it is "less than a third" of those values (p. 12).
- A strengths/weaknesses section acknowledges two limits: re-funding after a failed project is not modelled, and each rate parameter takes one value for all species (p. 17).

## What makes it stand out
- **Budget smoothness as a measured objective.** Turning "reliable" funding into the SD of yearly cost, and choosing to measure it on real cash rather than risk-adjusted cost, is a clear and defensible reading of the problem (pp. 4, 11).
- **Risk-adjusted cost.** Dividing cost by feasibility of success links the probability data to money in one simple step that the memo can explain (pp. 7, 10).
- **Benchmark-then-rank design.** Normalising the benefit filter against the "everyone starts in year 1" ideal lets the Board state its ambition as a single percentage (pp. 10, 12). The sensitivity section then shows what changing that percentage does.
- **A decision-support tool, not just one answer.** Default parameters can be changed, the code exports the three best schedules to Excel with one command, and interactive 3-D plots are provided. The strengths section calls it "Easy to Use" (pp. 12, 16–17).
- **Full-schedule visual.** A project × year cost table with start years highlighted appears on the summary sheet, in the results and in the memo (pp. 1, 11, 18). The reader sees the whole plan at once.
- **Sensitivity presented as distributions.** Histograms of the score over all plans that pass the filter show the whole set of feasible plans, not a single changed number (pp. 13–16).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** a context paragraph and a restatement of the tasks, then the model and its four objectives with section pointers ("(Section 3.1)", "(Section 4)"). Next come the headline numbers (89% benefit, $107,135,000, $267,411 SD, 34 years), a miniature of the schedule table, and a recommendation (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** Introduction & factor analysis → Variables and Assumptions → The Stochastic Model (objectives, time-dependent benefit, algorithm, results) → Sensitivity Analysis → Strength and Weakness → Conclusion → Memo → Appendix (code, works cited).
- **Devices:** variable list (p. 5); assumption–justification pairs (pp. 5–6); a short table of start-year bounds by project length (p. 9); 2×2 panels of 3-D scatter plots (p. 9) and of histograms (pp. 13–16); a commented code listing with a note on how to run it in Jupyter (pp. 19–22).
- **Memo:** dated, with TO/FROM/SUBJECT lines. It shows the schedule chart, then three headline numbers, then what the Board can adjust (rates, benefit benchmark), and closes with an explicit caveat about failed projects (p. 18).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "The money required to fund a project is calculated by dividing the total cost of the project by its feasibility of success." (p. 7)
  - "Notice that devc takes from the original yearly costs instead of the adjusted one. This is because the original yearly cost is the actual budget for the fundraising process." (p. 11)
  - "Please be aware that the outcome of this model does not account for a certain level of risk in case of a failing project." (p. 18)

## Notes for reuse
- The decay rates (r_u, r_b, r_t) and the benefit benchmark are set by hand with no empirical source, and the recommended schedule depends on them. The default values also differ between places: the text in Section 3.3.5 gives r_t = 0.01 and exp_pct_b = 85, the result uses r_t = 1 and reports 89%, and the code default is 87 (pp. 11–12, 20). The run counts are also stated differently ("500 billion" on p. 1, "a billion" on p. 12, 100,000 × 500 in Section 3.3.5). An imitator should keep one parameter table and one run count used everywhere.
- The ranking score multiplies adjusted total cost by yearly SD. This product weights the two objectives in a fixed way, and that choice is not justified or varied. The $107M "adjusted total cost" is a risk-adjusted figure, not the cash actually raised.
