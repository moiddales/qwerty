# 2024B-16226: Let's Build a Green HPC Together!

## At a glance
This paper is a case-study and framework paper. It works with six named facilities: Tianhe-1, Sunway TaihuLight, the Shenzhen Cloud Computing Center, and data-centre clusters in Northern Virginia, Dallas and Silicon Valley. For each it defines full-load and average-utilization energy (p. 6–7). A layered "progressive model" follows. An energy-structure model splits HPC energy into six categories. A carbon-accounting model applies emission factors to each category. An Environmental Impact Index (EII) then combines climate, ecosystem and air-quality impacts (p. 7–11). Two scenarios to 2030 are compared: HPC growth with a fossil-dominated mix, and growth with renewables rising to 40%. The first gives "hundreds of millions of tons of CO2" in 2030; the second brings emissions down to the "million tons" range (p. 12–13). Extensions add renewable shares up to 100%, a water-consumption model with an extended EII, a policy simulation (60% renewable supply for HPC by 2030), and a sensitivity analysis (p. 14–21).

## How they read the problem
- **Broad view of "energy consumption".** The team counts more than electricity. HPC "energy consumption" has six categories: electricity, water resources, chemical fuel (backup generators), building energy, equipment manufacturing and waste treatment (Table 4, p. 8). Life-cycle and auxiliary emissions are therefore part of the model from Task II onward.
- **Explicit scope definitions for Task I.** Annual energy is defined over 365 days in MWh. "Two modes of operation" are defined as full load (the theoretical upper limit) and actual operation (average utilization) (p. 6).
- **Impact, not just emissions.** Requirement 2 asks for the "environmental impact of total carbon emissions", which the team reads as a separate layer. Carbon is translated into climate, ecosystem and air-quality impacts through the weighted EII (p. 10–11).
- **Policy as a scenario.** For Requirement 5b they assume the UN adopts their "promote renewable energy power supply" recommendation with a 60% target by 2030. The scenario is then re-run (p. 18).
- A short literature review (p. 3) and a five-point restatement (p. 4) open the paper. A flow chart, "HPC Environmental Impact Assessment System", maps Tasks I–V (Fig. 2, p. 4).

## Models and methods
- **Energy formulas (Eqs. 1–3).** These are simple and transparent. Utilization multiplies peak power, and facility totals are summed (p. 7). The rationale is in Assumption 1, which takes energy as linearly proportional to computational load (p. 4).
- **Energy structure model (Eqs. 4–5).** E_total is the sum of the six categories, with shares p_i that add to 1. Per-facility pie charts show the resulting shares; their legend mixes consumption types and sources (electricity, water, natural gas, fuel oil, solar, wind) (Fig. 5, p. 8).
- **Carbon accounting (Eqs. 6–12).** Electricity uses Σ grid share × emission factor. Water uses a treatment and transport factor (t CO2/m³). Fuel uses fuel-specific factors. Manufacturing emissions are spread over T_lifecycle. Waste uses a per-unit factor. The category emissions are summed to c_total (p. 9–10).
- **EII (Eqs. 13–16).** EII = w1·Impact_climate + w2·Impact_ecosystem + w3·Impact_airquality, where:
  - Climate = c_total × GWP.
  - Ecosystem = c_total·f_landuse + E_water·f_waterstress.
  - Air quality = c_total·f_PM2.5 + C_fuel·f_SO2.
  - Weights are adjustable "to focus on specific environmental objectives" (p. 10–11).
- **Forecast and scenarios (Eqs. 17–22).** Exponential compute growth is divided by an improving efficiency, combined with a future mix and emission factors, and passed through the EII (p. 11–12). Scenarios are simulated in MATLAB (code in Appendix, p. 24–25).
- **Extensions (Eqs. 23–28 and the water model).** Renewable ramps toward fixed targets, with emission reduction measured as a difference from baseline. The water model reuses the energy-mix shares, so water and carbon move together, and the extended EII normalizes both to baselines (p. 14–16).

## Data
- A data-source table lists each dataset with its URL (Table 2, p. 5). Sources include EIA state electricity data, the KPMG copy of the Statistical Review of World Energy 2023, and Chinese news and industry pages for the supercomputers' power use and local power composition.
- Emission factors for future mixes are stated in the text (p. 12). Water factors (50/10/1) are labelled "initial assumptions" (p. 15).
- The MATLAB appendix shows how the time series and structures were generated:
  - The 2015–2022 facility series are back-projected from the 2023 values using random growth factors between 1.2 and 1.5.
  - Facility energy structures and totals are produced with `rand`/`randi`.
  - Scenario runs use fixed initial consumptions per category and emission factors per category (p. 24–25).

## Assumptions
Four assumptions, each with a bold title and a justification paragraph (p. 4–5):
- Energy is linearly proportional to computational load, which "simplifies the analysis and provides a foundation for more complex, non-linear models" (p. 4).
- The energy mix is stable in the short term, giving a baseline for the scenarios (p. 4–5).
- Hardware and cooling efficiency are constant within the model's scope (p. 5). The forecast section later relaxes this with a 10–15%/yr efficiency gain (p. 12).
- Carbon emission factors depend directly on energy type (p. 5).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Scenario contrast as the main robustness device.** Fossil-dominant vs renewable-increase runs are reported for carbon (Figs. 8–10), water (Fig. 11) and EII (Fig. 12), each with a "reduction" bar chart of the difference (p. 13–17).
- **Sensitivity analysis section.** Growth rate, energy ratios and emission factors are named as the parameters to test. The figure varies the renewable share (30/40/50%) and shows emissions and the intensity index; higher renewable share gives slower growth and a lower index (Fig. 14, p. 21).
- **Policy before/after comparison** (Fig. 13, p. 18).
- Strengths and weaknesses follow, with weaknesses naming the linearity assumption and data limitations (p. 22). No comparison with external estimates is reported.

## What makes it stand out
- **A clear, layered architecture.** Structure → accounting → impact index is easy to follow and to extend. The flow chart (p. 4) and the "progressive model" wording in the summary (p. 1) present the paper as one system.
- **Broad, life-cycle view of HPC's footprint.** Backup fuel, building operations, equipment manufacturing amortized over service life, and waste treatment all sit beside electricity (Table 4, Eqs. 6–11, p. 8–9). This answers the "comprehensive model" wording and connects to several of the problem's listed impact areas.
- **An impact index that goes beyond tons of CO2.** The EII links carbon and water to climate, ecosystem and air-quality effects with interpretable factors (GWP, land use, water stress, PM2.5, SO2) (p. 10–11). It is later reused as a policy instrument through mandatory EII-based disclosure (p. 18).
- **Joint carbon–water reasoning.** Water factors are tied to the same energy mix, so a single policy lever moves both. The paper also flags hydropower-driven regional water stress as a counterpoint (p. 16).
- **Scenarios carried through every task.** The same two scenarios run through Tasks III–V and the letter, which makes the narrative consistent and easy to compare (p. 12–20).
- **Accessible presentation.** It has a title and a friendly summary, bolded key findings, task-numbered sections, a data-source table with URLs, and code in an appendix.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** Titled "Let's Build a Green HPC Together!" One purpose sentence in bold, then a "For Task I/II/III/IV" paragraph each, with model names and key findings in bold, a closing paragraph on recommendations and the letter, and keywords. Findings are mostly qualitative (directions of change rather than numbers) (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, literature review, restatement) → Assumptions and Justifications → Model Preparation (notations, data) → Task I–V Solutions → Sensitivity Analysis → Evaluation (strengths, weaknesses) → Conclusion → References → Appendix (code).
- **Devices:** Task-numbered headings; a flow chart of the whole system (p. 4); a notation table and a data-source table (p. 5); a category table (p. 8); small-multiple pie charts and bar panels for six facilities (p. 8, 10); paired "level" and "reduction vs baseline" charts for scenario comparison (p. 15–16); "Boundary:" paragraphs stating the 2030 bound after each scenario (p. 13).
- **Letter:** Written in first person ("I"), about 1.5 pages. It opens with respect for the committee and names the September 2024 report, then states the gap. It summarizes both scenarios in plain words (growth of 8–10%, "hundreds of millions of tons", 40% renewables) and ends with five bolded policy asks and a forward-looking close (p. 19–20).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "By adjusting the weights w1, w2, w3, it is possible to focus on specific environmental objectives (e.g., reducing climate warming, protecting ecosystems, or improving air quality)." (p. 10)
  - "The reductions gradually expand in the later stages, showing the long-term benefits of the energy transition." (p. 15)
  - "For example, when the proportion of renewable energy increases, the overall water consumption may show a decreasing trend, but if the main reliance is on hydropower, regional water stress may occur in some areas" (p. 16)
  - "However, I believe that the report fails to pay sufficient attention to the environmental impact of HPC, especially its potential contribution to global climate change." (p. 19)

## Notes for reuse
- The text says the 2015–2023 facility data were "collected" (p. 6). The appendix code, however, back-projects 2015–2022 from the 2023 values with random factors and generates the facility energy structures with random numbers (p. 24). Figs. 3, 5 and 6 are therefore partly synthetic. An imitator should label generated or illustrative data as such.
- The EII weights (w1–w3, α, β) and impact factors (f_landuse, f_waterstress, f_PM2.5, f_SO2) are never given values (p. 10–11, 16). In the code, the plotted "EII" is computed as emissions ÷ consumption and labelled "Energy Intensity Index" (p. 13, 21, 25). The plotted series is therefore not the weighted index defined in Eq. 13.
- Growth inputs vary between sections: 20–30%/yr in the growth model (p. 11) versus 8–10%/yr in the scenarios (p. 12). The 2030 bounds are only stated in words ("hundreds of millions of tons", "million tons") (p. 13). Reuse requires choosing and stating one set of inputs and giving numeric bounds.
