# 2023A-14632: Untitled (Seed Agent Model and Invasive Species Trade-Off Model)

## At a glance
For Part 1 the team built a **Seed Agent Model**. Each seed is an agent that disperses by a **Brownian-motion** random walk: the number of moves is Poisson with a wind-dependent mean and the step sizes are normal. Each agent draws an exponential **death date**, germinates after a normally distributed delay, and grows according to an index function of temperature, daylight and soil potassium/nitrogen. On reaching the puffball stage it releases 2000 new seeds. The model was calibrated with "hyperparameters" for a temperate site (Clay, NY), an arid site (Phoenix, AZ) and a tropical site (Florida Keys, FL). It shows cyclical dormancy–dispersal–blooming dynamics with region-specific blooming seasons, and the summary reports roughly 500–900 plants after one year. For Part 2 they reframed the impact factor as a **trade-off pair**: an ecological score (Lotka–Volterra effect on three native species plus a logistic "spreadability" rate) against an economic score in dollars (Gordon–Schaefer harvest profit + Sen-welfare social benefit − human health risk). On this basis dandelions and garlic mustard are classified invasive and English ivy non-invasive. A "Data-Score-Divide" framework generalizes the classification (p. 1, p. 22). The paper also won the NCTM award.

## How they read the problem
- They restated the tasks in numbered form (p. 4). Part 1 was scoped to a 100 m × 100 m square with the parent puffball at the corner (0, 0) and a simulated year from 1 January to 31 December (Assumptions 1, 7, 8, p. 4; p. 9).
- The problem's three climate words map directly to three real US places, one each for temperate, arid and tropical (p. 8).
- They introduced the term **hyperparameters** for regional inputs (temperature, light, wind, soil K and N, lifespans, initial seeds) "that are not directly passed into our model but still change the output" (p. 9). This makes the model portable to any site.
- In Part 2 they took up the statement's "Friend? Foe?" framing. They split impact into *ecological harm* and *economic benefit* and represent the impact factor as a coordinate pair rather than a single number. In their framing, a species is invasive unless economic benefit clearly outweighs ecological harm (p. 1, p. 13).
- They distinguished "foreign" from "invasive" species, which matches the glossary's warning that the terms are not interchangeable (p. 14, p. 22).

## Models and methods
**Dispersal.** Each seed's number of moves is N ~ Poisson(m(t)), where m(t) is a wind-strength hyperparameter. Each move adds Δx, Δy ~ Normal(0, σ²), and seeds leaving the plot are dropped (Eqs. 1–3, p. 5–6). The rationale is that Brownian motion is commonly used to model random particle movement such as seeds in open air (p. 5).

**Death dates.** They cite the Type III survivorship curve for dandelions, where most die early. Death dates are drawn from an exponential distribution, with mean lifespan λ_s = 4 days for seeds and λ_d = 15 days for plants, and redrawn after germination and after growth (Eq. 4, Fig. 2, p. 6; p. 11). The only interaction between agents is that two seeds landing in the same place eliminate one of them (p. 6). The code enforces this with a 1 m minimum spacing (p. 25).

**Growth.** Three indices score conditions on [−1, 1]. Temperature T_I is positive inside 50–77 °F (a quadratic peak) and falls along a logistic tail outside that range. Light L_I works the same way on 6–24 hours. Nutrition N_I uses Gaussian-shaped scores for K (40–80 ppm) and N (20–40 ppm) (Eqs. 5–7, Fig. 3, p. 7–8). Growth dG/dt = (T_I + L_I + N_I)/(3·50), so ideal conditions give the minimum 50-day cycle (Eq. 8, p. 8). An overview diagram connects regional characteristics to the agent process (Fig. 4, p. 8).

**Sensitivity method.** Each hyperparameter is randomly perturbed within ±10% in Monte Carlo runs. The RMSE of each run's population time series against the default run is computed, min–max scaled and shown on a radar chart (Eq. 9, Figs. 11–12, p. 12).

**Ecological score.** A generalized Lotka–Volterra competition system dxᵢ/dt = rᵢxᵢ(1 − Σⱼαᵢⱼxⱼ/Kᵢ) is solved numerically with SciPy, before and after adding the foreign species (Eqs. 10–11, p. 14–15). χ is the sum over three native species of the new/old population ratio after one year (Eq. 12). Spreadability is 100r, with r taken from logistic growth, N(t) = L/(1 + Ce^(−rt)). For dandelion, r = 0.04 comes from the Part 1 population curve, which has a carrying capacity of about 900 plants (p. 15, p. 18). Ecological score = 100r + χ (Eq. 14).

**Economic score.** Profit π comes from a Gordon–Schaefer-style analysis: the optimal effort is where linear marginal revenue meets constant marginal cost (MR = MC), and profit is total revenue minus total cost (Fig. 14, p. 16). Social benefit is W = (1 − G)ΣYᵢ (Sen's welfare function with the Gini coefficient G), and health risk R is built the same way (Eqs. 15–16, p. 16). Economic score = π + W − R (Eq. 17), and the impact score is the pair (Eq. 18, p. 17).

**Classification.** Scores are plotted on an ecological vs economic plane, and an evaluator draws a "line of division". The team says this step is inspired by support vector machines (DSD framework, Fig. 19, p. 22).

## Data
Regional climate inputs (temperature, sunshine hours, wind) were taken from NOAA and local climate sites, and soil K and N from soil surveys and extension publications for each region (p. 9–11). Defaults and sources are consolidated in a hyperparameter table (p. 11). Part 2 uses assumed starting populations (100 units), carrying capacities (1000) and growth rates for the native species, with brief ecological justifications (e.g. moths 0.06 "as their populations grow quickly"). The interaction matrices are described as "approximated" (p. 17, p. 19–20). Economic inputs are online prices, a labour-time estimate and state Gini coefficients (p. 18–21). Each per-person benefit is a single assumed figure, for example $0.002 per person for dandelions (p. 18).

## Assumptions
Assumptions are given in tables with numbered rows and an "Assumption | Justification" layout: 8 for Part 1 (p. 4) and 3 more for Part 2 (p. 14). Representative examples:
- Nitrogen and potassium are the only nutrients that matter, citing their role in germination and growth speed (Assumption 5, p. 4). They also explain why rainfall was not calibrated separately: potassium levels are closely correlated with soil moisture (p. 9).
- Native species are in competitive equilibrium before the foreign species arrives, which lets before/after Lotka–Volterra runs isolate its effect (Assumption 9, p. 14).
- Social benefit and health risk can be measured in dollars, giving a standardized economic axis (Assumption 11, p. 14).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- **Agreement with known ecology:** two blooming spikes per year (spring and summer) and winter dormancy in New York are reported as consistent with extension findings (p. 9).
- **Monte Carlo hyperparameter sensitivity:** the curves keep the three-phase shape and approach a carrying capacity set by the minimum-spacing parameter. Light levels and seed lifespan have the largest effect and soil nutrients the least, which the team says is consistent with field observations (Figs. 11–12, p. 12).
- **Part 2:** no sensitivity analysis. The stated reason is that the evaluator's dividing line dominates the decision (p. 22).

## What makes it stand out
- **A genuine agent-based life-cycle model.** Each seed carries its own position, germination date, growth state and death date. The appendix code (p. 25) shows the mechanics, which makes the model concrete and reproducible.
- **Biology-grounded stochastic ingredients.** Linking exponential death dates to the Type III survivorship curve, and Poisson move counts to wind strength, gives each distribution a stated ecological reason (p. 5–6).
- **Regional calibration as a design principle.** The "hyperparameter" framing and consolidated default table make the model portable, and the three regions directly match the problem's three climate words (p. 8–11).
- **Insightful seasonal results.** Instead of only counting plants, they report *when* blooming happens in each climate: spring and summer in New York, late-year in Arizona, winter in the Florida Keys (p. 9–10).
- **Readable sensitivity visualization.** Overlaid Monte Carlo trajectories annotated with phases, plus an RMSE radar chart, communicate parameter influence at a glance (p. 12).
- **A bold reframing of "impact factor."** Treating impact as a two-axis ecological-vs-economic trade-off speaks directly to the problem's "Friend? Foe? Both? Neither?" title and to the statement's mention of edible and medicinal uses (p. 13, p. 17).
- **Interdisciplinary toolkit.** It combines population ecology (Lotka–Volterra, logistic growth) with economics (Gordon–Schaefer, Sen's welfare function), and it ends in a general procedure (DSD) that a practitioner could follow (p. 14–17, p. 22).
- **Engaging voice.** Light, well-placed wordplay ("spread" the news, effects that can "blow" you away) makes the paper memorable without displacing the technical content (p. 1).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** a playful hook, then a two-sub-task overview. A Part 1 paragraph covers the method, the three named sites, the headline result (500–900 plants, cyclical blooming) and the sensitivity findings. A Part 2 paragraph covers the trade-off idea and its components, then the classification of the three species and the DSD framework, followed by a closing line. Clear, but light on numbers in Part 2.
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (life-cycle background with phase subsections, restatement) → Part 1 model (analysis, assumptions, overview, dispersal, death date, growth stages, overview diagram) → Application by region → Model discussion (hyperparameters, sensitivity, strengths, limitations) → Part 2 trade-off model → Application to three species → Discussion (generalization, strengths, limitations) → Conclusion → References → Code appendix.
- **Devices:** a two-column table of contents, assumption–justification tables and variable tables for each part. There are a life-cycle diagram, index-function plots with optimal bands shaded, and an overview diagram. Results include map grids titled with t and n, phase-annotated Monte Carlo plots and a radar chart. Part 2 adds a profit-maximization figure, a score-comparison table with a written analysis column and a three-step framework graphic. Lively section titles such as "The Way Of The Wind" and "Race the Clock: Beating a Death Date" add character.
- **Phrasing:**
  - "We are here to "spread" the news about dandelions and their effects on our environment." (p. 1) is a hook that sets the tone in one line.
  - "We use the term hyperparameters to designate global values that are not directly passed into our model but still change the output of the model." (p. 9) defines a term before using it.
  - "Through this concept, we can represent our "impact factor" as the trade-off between ecological and economic impacts." (p. 13) states the reframing in one sentence.
  - "Physical risk, human well-being, and cold, hard profits are all important considerations to determine the overall effect of a foreign species on humans. But, how can we measure these values, such as happiness?" (p. 15) uses a rhetorical question to motivate monetization.

## Notes for reuse
- χ sums native populations' new/old ratios (Eq. 12), so it *increases* when native species grow. In the dandelion case most of χ = 17.794 comes from moths growing twelvefold (p. 17–18), yet a higher score is read as greater harm. An imitator should define a native-impact metric whose direction matches "harm", e.g. by counting declines.
- The Lotka–Volterra interaction matrices, native growth rates and per-person benefits are approximated by the team (p. 17–21), and the economic axis scales with an assumed firm's size. English ivy's $7225 vs dandelion's $105.46 drives the "non-invasive" verdict, which then rests on a judgment-drawn dividing line with no sensitivity analysis (p. 22).
- In the appendix code, the default regional inputs are generated (sinusoidal temperature and light, random K and N and wind) (p. 25), while the text describes calibration to 2022 regional data (p. 9, p. 11). The step from cited data to simulation inputs is not shown.
