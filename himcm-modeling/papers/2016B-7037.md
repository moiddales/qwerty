# 2016B-7037: Population and Tax Ratios for Warehouse Placement

## At a glance
The team selects 28 warehouses to reach populated areas, then scores each location by out-of-state population divided by in-state population times sales tax. Low-scoring warehouses are replaced; a clothing-tax term and weighted average tax guide the final map. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They prioritize minimum coverage first, then tax liability, then clothing. The ratio asks whether keeping a warehouse in a taxed state is worth the extra out-of-state population it serves. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Candidate locations are linked to ZIP/state coverage. A score SR=out-of-state population/(in-state population × tax) ranks warehouses; the lowest quartile is replaced. Clothing tax changes the ratio and a weighted average tax estimates system impact (pp. 4–22).

## Data
The paper uses UPS coverage map, state populations, sales and clothing tax rates. The source is the UPS ground transit map or extracted ZIP/pixel reachability, combined with state sales-tax and clothing-tax tables, population and road-access information. The unit of coverage is a stated modeling choice. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes UPS one-day reachability can be represented by states, ZIP codes or map pixels and that candidate warehouses cover every unit assigned to them. Taxes are applied at warehouse-state level, with clothing treated as a second tax schedule. Population and uninhabited areas are handled differently by each team and are reported as modeling choices (pp. 4–12). Its central choice is population coverage score, supported by state-tax ratio; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The paper compares initial, sales-tax and clothing-tax maps and checks whether replacements preserve coverage. Quartile thresholds and alternative maps are shown in tables/appendices (pp. 20–30).

## What makes it stand out
The ratio is an interpretable policy heuristic and makes the tax trade-off visible to a business reader. The sequential objectives prevent tax from silently sacrificing one-day reach.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
A president’s letter explains the ratio in an example before the technical derivation. Maps, state tables and an appendix list the final warehouses (pp. 1–3, 20–30).

## Notes for reuse
- The ratio can favor extreme population imbalances and does not price construction or transport. An imitator should use population-weighted demand and test a joint objective.
