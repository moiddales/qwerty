# 2021A-11900: AHP battery choice with climate- and wealth-dependent weights

## At a glance
The team treats battery choice as a multi-criteria decision and uses AHP over eight battery attributes (cost, weight, footprint, height, continuous and instantaneous power rating, round-trip efficiency, capacity). To generalize (Requirement 2), they do not change the method; they change the *weights*, building separate pairwise-comparison matrices for nine contrasting locations (Iceland, Sudan, Singapore, Texas, …) whose climate and GDP shift what matters. They test the entropy weight method as an objective alternative, explain why they reject it, and recommend the Discover AES 7.4 kWh (LFP), with the Tesla Powerwall+ second (pp. 1, 22–24).

## How they read the problem
- Restates the goal as a "cost-efficient purchasing plan" that balances internal battery factors with external factors such as socio-economic level and sunny days (p. 4).
- Splits criteria into **internal determinants** (battery specifications) and **external determinants** (sunshine hours, minimum sunshine, rain seasons, rainy/snowy days, wealth, temperature extremes), and uses the external ones only for Requirement 2 (pp. 7–9).
- Interprets "adaptable to individual needs" as "location profile changes the relative importance of criteria", which gives a concrete, explainable generalization mechanism (pp. 10–11).

## Models and methods
- **AHP.** Each member scored each factor 1–10; the mean scores were turned into pairwise ratios (e.g. cost 8 vs weight 2.5), extended with a 1.1–1.9 "only slightly more important" band added to Saaty's scale (pp. 10, 12). Weights come from column-normalizing the 8×8 matrix and averaging rows; λmax, CI and CR are computed explicitly for Iceland (CR ≈ 0.5%) (pp. 13–17).
- **Location weighting.** Rules link location traits to criteria: lower GDP per capita raises the weight of cost; extreme temperatures raise continuous and instantaneous power rating; rainy-season or high-latitude locations raise capacity; island locations raise size (p. 11). Rationale is qualitative and stated explicitly.
- **Entropy weight method.** Min–max normalization with separate formulas for benefit and cost criteria, entropy, then weights (pp. 18–19). A combined AHP/EWM weighting was also computed (p. 19). The team rejects EWM because it gave height weight 0.17 and instantaneous power rating 0 (data were missing), which contradicts domain importance (p. 19). This is a good example of judging a method by whether its output makes sense.
- **Concrete battery estimate.** Assumed 40 ft × 40 ft single-storey house, 6-inch concrete walls and ceiling: V ≈ 44.29 m³; capacity = energy density × volume (p. 20).

## Data
COMAP's five-battery table; EIA statistics for household size and monthly use; an online load calculator for peak load; World Bank GDP per capita for the nine regions (p. 11). Missing instantaneous ratings for the lead-acid batteries are set equal to the continuous rating (Assumption 4, p. 6).

## Assumptions
Numbered assumption–justification pairs (pp. 5–6). Representative ones:
- "The off-the-grid system supplies energy for a family of three members", justified by the EIA average of 2.48 people per household, rounded up to cover more families (p. 5).
- Monthly consumption of 900 kWh, justified by EIA's 10,715 kWh/year and the state-level spread (Hawaii 537 to Louisiana 1,201 kWh/month) (pp. 5–6).
- Missing instantaneous ratings are set to the minimum possible value, the continuous rating (p. 6).

## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
- AHP consistency is checked with CI and CR (p. 17).
- The model is compared with an alternative weighting method (EWM) and the disagreement is discussed (p. 19).
- The result is cross-checked against engineering arguments and practitioner recommendations; the paper says the result "coincides with the suggestions offered by professional engineers" (pp. 17, 22–23).
- No numerical sensitivity analysis on the weights.

## What makes it stand out
- **Generalization through weights, not through a new model.** Varying pairwise judgments by location profile is a simple, defensible answer to "adapt to individual needs" (pp. 10–11).
- **External-factor taxonomy.** Sunshine hours, monsoon seasons, polar night, wealth and temperature are each tied to specific battery attributes, which makes the weighting reasoning traceable (pp. 8–9, 11).
- **Honest method comparison.** Trying EWM, showing its weights, and explaining why it is rejected shows judgment rather than method-stacking (p. 19).
- **Engineering sanity check of the result.** Lead-acid cycle life, the LG Chem recall and NMC thermal runaway are used to confirm that the top-ranked LFP battery is sensible (pp. 23–24).
- **Requirement 3b answered as a structured information list** (durability and repair, health, performance in extreme weather) with concrete questions (p. 22).

## Writing and presentation worth learning
- **Summary sheet:** a context paragraph, the method with a reason for choosing it, the named answer with reasons, and a cement-battery verdict. It is readable but uses few numbers (p. 1).
- **Paper structure:** Introduction (background, restatement per question, our work) → Assumptions → Criteria → Weight determination → AHP → EWM → Concrete battery → Conclusion → References (p. 3).
- **Devices:** per-question restatement (pp. 4–5); "(For question 2)" and "(For question 3)" tags on headings that map sections to requirements (pp. 8, 10, 20); an appliance load table (p. 6); the Saaty scale table (p. 12); a pie chart of weights (p. 15); a strengths and weaknesses list placed right after the model (pp. 17–18).
- **Article:** designed as a magazine page with a photo and two columns, placed right after the summary. It opens with the COP26 news hook and ends with a teaser into the paper (p. 2).
- **Phrasing:**
  - "Our modified AHP model helps you identify your personal needs, and then compare the criteria by ranking a priority order" (p. 2)
  - "Therefore, we decided not to include the EWM model in further calculation." (p. 19)

## Notes for reuse
- The concrete-battery capacity treats 44.29 m³ as 44.29 L (E = 0.8 Wh/L × 44.29 L = 35.36 Wh, p. 20). At the stated density, the volume gives about 35 kWh, so the "far less than other batteries" conclusion needs re-checking if imitated. Keep units explicit in any volume × density estimate.
- Pairwise ratios come from team members' 1–10 scores (p. 10). If you reuse this, say whose judgment the weights encode, and test how much the ranking changes when the weights change.
