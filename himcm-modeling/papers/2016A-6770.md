# 2016A-6770: 24-Division Triathlon Waves

## At a glance
The team estimates segment and transition times for 24 divisions, groups compatible registrants into waves, and models swimming slowdown, constant cycling speed and late-run acceleration. A 2.5-minute wave interval yields a 5.3-hour road closure in the proposed schedule. (pp. 1–3).

## How they read the problem
They preserve gender, age and status categories where useful, split large open divisions, and place faster waves first. A distance-time graph first gives a simple screening model, then a refined piecewise curve handles each race segment. They keep the official deliverable in view and use stated assumptions to make the decision variables finite (pp. 3–6).

## Models and methods
Quartiles summarize segment times; wave start times are chosen so the ranges of adjacent waves overlap minimally. Logarithmic, linear and exponential functions approximate speed through swim, bike and run, with transitions added explicitly (pp. 4–28).

## Data
The paper uses HiMCM triathlon dataset and segment-time references. The source is the prior-triathlon spreadsheet of age, gender, category, segment and transition times. Teams derive quartiles, clusters or fitted distributions and generate simulated entrants and schedules. It distinguishes prompt values, cited constants and generated scenarios so that a reader can see which inputs would need recalibration for a new setting (pp. 4–8).

## Assumptions
This team assumes prior-race times represent this year’s entrants, segment speeds can be summarized by groups or distributions, and wave starts do not change individual ability. Congestion is represented by passing, density or speed overlap; road closure is a hard limit or an explicitly weighted term (pp. 4–10). Its central choice is quartile divisioning, supported by distance-time wave model; those choices determine which behavior is visible in the reported results (pp. 4–8).
## Validation, sensitivity and robustness
The refined model is compared with the simple graph model and tested under alternative wave intervals and groupings (pp. 27–35). The schedule is checked against the 5.5-hour road limit and congestion criterion.

## What makes it stand out
The paper converts heterogeneous athlete data into a readable schedule while retaining a physical interpretation of each race segment. It also gives awards and public-facing logistics, making the result implementable.

## Writing and presentation worth learning
A two-page letter, race-day timetable, model diagrams and explicit division table support the organizer (pp. 1–4, 27–40).

## Notes for reuse
- Piecewise speed curves are approximations, especially for swim and run. An imitator should validate against actual split-time distributions and model transition-area capacity rather than only course trajectories.
